Two things decide a D-4 application, and neither of them is the form.

The first is **whether the school can sponsor you at all.** The second is
**whether your money looks like your own.** Everything else is paperwork
around those two questions.

## D-4, D-2, and why people prepare the wrong file

These are different statuses and they are not interchangeable.

| | D-2 | D-4-1 |
|---|---|---|
| What it is for | Enrolling in a degree | Korean language training |
| Where you study | University department | University-affiliated language institute |
| Outcome | Bachelor, master, doctorate | Certificate of completion, TOPIK progress |
| Typical length | Years | Terms, renewed |
| Called what in English | "Student visa" | Also called "student visa" |

Both get called a student visa, which is exactly how someone ends up
reading D-2 requirements and preparing D-2 documents for a language
institute term. If your plan is to study Korean at an institute rather
than to enrol in a degree, **D-4-1** is the class.

## The sponsorship question comes first

This is the part people discover too late.

A D-4-1 rests on a **certificate of admission** issued by an institution
whose course meets the standards set for hosting foreign students on that
status. In practice the representative route is a Korean language institute
attached to a university, and the government's own listings show course
after course run that way. A private academy — however good, however much
it advertises to foreigners — is generally not a sponsor route.

The distinction matters because the rule is about **the course**, not about
a category of building. Ask the institute directly whether this specific
course supports D-4-1, and get the answer in writing.

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">Check accreditation before you pay, not after</p>
  <p>"They teach foreigners" and "they can sponsor a visa" are unrelated facts.
  Confirm the institution appears on the government's own list of accredited
  institutions before any money moves. An academy that answers this question
  vaguely has answered it.</p>
</div>

If you are already in Korea on another status and simply want Korean
lessons, a private academy is fine — the sponsorship question does not
arise. It only matters when the visa depends on the school.

## Financial capability: the structure, not the number

You have to show you can pay for the study and for living here. The
evidence is normally a bank balance certificate, held for a period, in
your name or a guarantor's.

Three things about it are worth knowing before you gather documents.

<div class="steps">
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">1</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>The required amount is not one number.</strong> It differs by where the institution is — capital area institutions sit at a higher bar than regional ones — and immigration adjusts it. Get the current figure from HiKorea or the Immigration Contact Center on <strong>1345</strong>, or from the consulate you will apply at, and get it for <em>your</em> institution's region.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">2</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>The holding period matters as much as the balance.</strong> A large sum that appeared last week is treated differently from the same sum that has been sitting there for months. This is the single most common reason a financially capable applicant is refused.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">3</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>A guarantor has to be explainable.</strong> If a parent or relative is funding you, the relationship needs documentary proof and their income needs to make the sum plausible. Money from someone with no stated connection to you is the weakest possible evidence.</div></div>
</div>

```
Enough money  ≠  money that reads as yours
```

The assessment is not arithmetic. An officer is deciding whether the story
the documents tell is the story of your actual life.

## What is being judged

Beyond the school and the money, the recurring themes in refusals are
consistency and plausibility.

- **Purpose that fits the person.** A study plan that does not connect to
  your education, work history or stated future reads as a cover for
  something else.
- **Documents that agree with each other.** Dates, names, addresses and
  employment across your file should not contradict one another. Small
  inconsistencies do real damage because they are cheap to find.
- **Immigration history.** Previous overstays, refusals or removals in
  Korea or elsewhere are visible and they matter.
- **Nationality-based screening.** Applicants from countries with high
  overstay rates face additional scrutiny. This is not a rumour and it is
  not personal; it is a policy setting that changes how thin your margin
  is.

## Two routes to the visa

There are two ways this normally runs, and the school usually tells you
which one applies.

| Route | How it works | Who it suits |
|---|---|---|
| **Direct application** | You take the certificate of admission and your file to a Korean embassy or consulate and apply there | Most applicants |
| **Certificate of Confirmation of Visa Issuance** | The institution applies to immigration in Korea on your behalf first; you then collect the visa at the consulate with that confirmation number | Institutions that handle this for their intake |

Either way the decision is made **outside Korea**. You cannot arrive on a
tourist entry and convert to D-4-1 as a matter of course, and planning to
do so is how people end up leaving and re-entering at their own expense.

## What happens the week you land

The visa gets you in. It does not get you a life here — that starts with
the alien registration card, and almost everything practical waits behind
it.

Before then, two things are worth doing while you are still at home:
**ask the institute in writing about its refund policy if the visa is
refused**, and **confirm the current financial requirement for that
institution's region** rather than the number a forum quoted last year.

Those two questions cost an email each. Getting either of them wrong
costs a term.
