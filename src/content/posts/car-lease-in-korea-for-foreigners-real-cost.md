A car lease in Korea is not "a car for a monthly fee." It is a financial
contract that separates ownership from use so you can engineer your cash
flow. Buying converts cash into an asset; installment financing acquires the
asset while creating debt; a lease has the finance company own the car while
you pay for the right to use it.

That framing changes what you should compare. For a company CEO — including
a foreigner running a Korean 법인 — the monthly payment is close to the
least important number. What decides whether a lease makes sense: initial
cash outflow, total financing cost over the term, *when* the tax system
actually recognizes your expenses, the end-of-term residual, the odds of
early termination, and the effect on your financial statements.

## Operating lease, finance lease, long-term rental: substance over labels

Korean dealers present 운용리스 (operating lease), 금융리스 (finance
lease), and 장기렌트 (long-term rental) as separate products. The real
question underneath is: who bears the risks and rewards of the asset?

| | Operating lease | Finance lease | Long-term rental |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Economic nature | Right-of-use | Close to financed purchase | Vehicle rental |
| End of term | Return / buy / re-lease | Buyout common | Return or buy |
| Insurance | Usually your own policy | Usually your own policy | Usually included in rental contract |
| Residual value | Set relatively high | Set relatively low | Per contract |
| Plates | Normal passenger plates | Normal passenger plates | Rental-series plates (허/하/호) |
| Initial cash | Deposit / prepayment options | Varies by contract | Deposit / prepayment options |

An operating lease assumes return at maturity and sets a comparatively high
residual value — the monthly payment falls, but end-of-contract vehicle
value and return conditions start to matter. A finance lease puts you in an
economic position close to ownership: buyout is likely, residuals are set
low. Long-term rental differs structurally — the car is registered to the
rental company, and insurance and vehicle tax are typically bundled into
the monthly fee.

One accounting warning for companies: the old line "it's an operating
lease, so no debt appears on the balance sheet" is obsolete. Under K-IFRS
1116, lessees in principle recognize a right-of-use asset *and* a lease
liability, and the Korea Accounting Standards Board has confirmed that
lease liabilities meet the definition of financial debt. If your company is
subject to external audit or applies K-IFRS, do not sign an operating lease
purely to manage your debt ratio on the strength of its name. Smaller
companies on K-GAAP, and specific contract structures, can be treated
differently — confirm with your accountant before signing, not after.

## The real advantage of leasing is cash preservation, not tax

Pay ₩80,000,000 cash for a car and, at signing, ₩80M of liquidity becomes a
depreciating vehicle. The car remains; the cash is gone.

A lease lets you choose, say, a 20% deposit plus monthly payments — keeping
the rest of that cash as working capital. For a company with volatile
revenue, keeping tens of millions of won available for inventory, payroll,
and tax payments is often worth more than owning the car outright.

Here you must separate two words that get blurred in every showroom:

- **선수금 (prepayment)** is effectively lease payments made in advance —
  and it is normally **not refundable**.
- **보증금 (deposit)** is returned at the end of the contract, net of any
  unsettled amounts.

Loading a big prepayment to shrink the monthly payment and concluding "the
lease got cheaper" is exactly how total costs get distorted. Compare leases
with this equation instead:

> **True total cost = prepayment + total lease payments + insurance/taxes
> and other separate costs + end-of-term buyout + fees − refunded deposit**

Then divide by the months you will actually use the car to get a true
monthly cost. This is precisely what our
[car lease cost calculator](/tools/car-lease-calculator/) computes — run
your actual quote through it before signing anything.

## Company cars: distrust the phrase "the whole lease payment is deductible"

For company vehicles, what matters in Korean tax is not lease-versus-buy —
it is **business use, and the cap on depreciation-type costs**.

The Corporate Tax Act excludes from deductible expenses any portion of
company passenger-vehicle costs not attributable to business use. Covered
costs are broad: depreciation, lease/rental payments, fuel, insurance,
repairs, vehicle tax, tolls, and finance-lease interest.

Two numbers control everything:

**₩8,000,000 — the annual depreciation cap.** If you buy, depreciation is
capped; if you lease, the *depreciation-equivalent portion of the lease
payments* faces the same per-vehicle annual limit of ₩8M, with the excess
carried forward to later years under set conditions. If your lease's
depreciation-equivalent comes to ₩11M this year, ₩8M is recognized now and
the remaining ₩3M waits. Sole proprietors subject to the parallel
income-tax regime face the same ₩8M structure.

**₩15,000,000 — the no-driving-log threshold.** This is widely misquoted
as "₩15M of car costs is always deductible." The actual rule: if the
company meets the requirements (including business-use auto insurance,
업무전용자동차보험) and keeps **no driving log**, business use is deemed
100% only when total vehicle-related costs are ≤ ₩15M for the year. Above
that, without a log, the recognized ratio becomes 15M ÷ total costs. So
₩15M is a *business-use-ratio rule tied to driving records*, not a blanket
expense ceiling — and the ₩8M depreciation cap still applies separately.

This is why leasing an expensive car under the company name does **not**
let you expense the vehicle's value quickly. The law is specifically built
to prevent that.

## For expensive cars, compute capital efficiency before tax savings

Comparing a ₩100M car against a ₩50M car on corporate-tax savings alone
reliably produces bad decisions. At an assumed 20% corporate rate, ₩10M of
recognized expense saves roughly ₩2M of tax. Signing up for tens of
millions in financing costs and depreciation, then calling it a win
"because it's deductible," is not economics — a deduction reduces the
after-tax price of spending; it does not un-spend the money.

The comparison that matters: **after-tax lease cost versus the expected
return on that cash inside your business.** If ₩50M kept liquid can earn a
10%+ operating margin as working capital, the lease has real economic
value. If the company is sitting on excess liquidity while financing costs
are high, buying outright may simply be cheaper.

## For foreigners, no visa type auto-approves (or auto-blocks) a lease

A foreign resident's lease application gets one extra question a Korean
applicant's does not: *can this person service the contract from Korea for
its entire term?*

Status like F-5, F-4, or E-7 is meaningful context for the lessor — but no
visa auto-approves a lease, and no visa categorically blocks one either.
Internal underwriting standards are largely unpublished and differ by
company. What realistically gets weighed: Korean credit records, income,
employment or business tenure, length and stability of stay, ARC identity
data, a domestic address, banking history, and how the contract term
compares to your remaining authorized stay.

The practical implications by situation:

- **Employment-based stay (E-7 and similar):** employment certificate,
  contract, and income documentation carry the weight.
- **Long-term settled status (F-5):** stay stability is easy to
  demonstrate; the file turns on income and credit like anyone else's.
- **F-4:** the question is whether sufficient *Korean* income and credit
  history exist, not the visa itself.

Treat internet folklore — "F-4 means X% deposit," "E-7 can't lease" — as
noise. And if you are new to Korea with no domestic credit at all, car
financing is the wrong place to start: build the base first with a
[bank account, salary deposits, phone bills, and a credit card](/banking/how-to-open-a-bank-account-in-korea-as-a-foreigner/),
then approach vehicle finance with a file that underwriters can actually
read. (More on Korean credit in
[can foreigners get a loan in Korea](/loans/can-foreigners-get-a-loan-in-korea/).)

## CEO personal lease vs corporate lease: decide by purpose, not habit

A car mostly driven by the CEO is not automatically better leased through
the company. A personal lease keeps the asset off the company, simplifies
the company's vehicle accounting, and avoids business-use scrutiny. A
corporate lease is structurally natural when the car genuinely serves the
business and business use is high.

The configuration to avoid is the classic audit target: an expensive,
substantively personal car run through corporate expenses with **no
business-use records at all**. Tax examinations look at substance, not the
name on the contract — the law conditions deductions on business-use
amounts and imposes reporting obligations for company passenger vehicles.
For corporate contracts, confirm 업무전용자동차보험 (business-use auto
insurance) enrollment; the enforcement decree ties expense recognition to
it. If a company is part of your setup, see
[registering a business in Korea](/business/registering-a-business-in-korea-as-a-foreigner/).

## Underwriting looks past the car to your repayment capacity

Showroom conversations start with car price and deposit percentages, but a
lease is a credit decision. For corporate applicants: company age, revenue
and profit, existing debt, banking history, the CEO's personal credit, and
tax-arrears status. Young companies with thin credit files should expect
conditions — a CEO guarantee, or a larger deposit. For individuals and
foreign residents: credit score, income type, tenure, existing debt, and
monthly payment burden.

One caution on "buying" approval with a large prepayment: it may lower the
underwriting hurdle, but a non-refundable prepayment magnifies your loss if
the contract ends early. If the goal is credit enhancement, compare a
**refundable-deposit structure against a prepayment structure explicitly**
— they are not interchangeable.

## Early termination is the most expensive button in the contract

The core risk of a lease is not the interest rate. It is failing to carry
the contract to maturity.

At signing, the lessor spreads vehicle cost, projected residual, and
financing cost across the full term. Terminate mid-way and you are not
simply repaying a remaining balance as with a loan — termination penalties,
unrecovered principal, residual settlement, and disposal gains/losses all
surface per the contract terms.

So when weighing 36 versus 60 months, a ₩50,000 monthly difference matters
far less than the probability that your business or your address changes
at month 24. Foreigners carry extra term risk by definition — repatriation,
job changes, visa transitions — which makes long contracts structurally
riskier for you than for a local.

The standard escape hatch, lease transfer (승계), moves the contract to a
new user — but it requires the lessor's re-underwriting, typically carries
transfer fees, and depends heavily on whether anyone wants your car:
popular models transfer, unpopular ones sit. Never sign on the assumption
"I'll just transfer it later."

## Decide the buyout question before you sign, not at maturity

The classic operating-lease mistake is comparing quotes on monthly payment
alone. A low payment often means a high residual — you are renting cheaply
because keeping the car will be expensive. If the projected market price at
maturity is ₩40M and the contractual buyout is ₩48M, returning the car is
the economic answer absent special reasons. If the buyout is ₩30M against a
stable used-car market, keeping or reselling becomes a real option.

Get three numbers in writing before signing, and evaluate them together:
**total lease payments, the end-of-term residual/buyout price, and the
early-termination terms.** The cheapest monthly quote is frequently not the
cheapest contract.

## Your stage of stay should set the strategy

There is no reason a six-month visitor and a ten-year resident should use
the same vehicle strategy.

- **Students and short-term assignees:** a long lease is usually the wrong
  instrument. Public transit, short-term rental, car sharing, and cards
  preserve flexibility — and for short-term visitors, FX fees, foreign-card
  surcharges, and ATM costs typically move the budget more than vehicle
  finance ever would.
- **Long-term workers and business owners:** build the domestic financial
  track record and step up in sequence — bank account, credit card, vehicle
  finance, then housing finance.
- **Family-based settlement (F-6 and similar):** the unit of analysis
  shifts from one car payment to the household — housing costs, spousal
  income, children, insurance, debt service, and long-term investment. A
  car lease is one line item in that budget, not a standalone decision.
- **Companies:** early-stage, cash preservation argues for leasing; once
  cash flow stabilizes, direct purchase can be more efficient. The answer
  changes contract by contract — not because of tax, but because the price
  and the usage period of money differ each time.

## The pre-signing checklist

- Separate the deposit from the prepayment, and know which one comes back.
- Compute total payments — not the nominal rate, not the monthly figure.
- Get the end-of-term buyout price in writing.
- Read the early-termination and transfer clauses yourself, in the
  contract, before signing.
- Corporate car: confirm business-use insurance and set up business-use
  records from day one.
- Foreign resident: price in the probability of leaving or changing jobs
  before signing anything longer than your realistic horizon — and run the
  numbers in the [lease calculator](/tools/car-lease-calculator/).

Whether a lease is "worth it" cannot be settled by the sentence "it's
deductible." It is settled by four numbers: how much cash you preserve,
how much financing cost you pay, what the after-tax cost really is, and
what options remain when the contract ends.
