The clauses that get a tenant safely to the balance payment are one half of a
lease. This is the other half: the two years you live there, and the exit.

Both halves have the same failure mode. A sentence in the contract is treated as
settling something the law has already decided differently.

## 1. "All repairs at the tenant's expense" is not a magic sentence

Under the Civil Act, a landlord is obliged to keep the leased property in a
state fit for the agreed use and enjoyment **throughout the term**.

So a major failure in what the property needs to be usable at all — the boiler,
main plumbing, electrical installations, structural water ingress — raises the
landlord's repair responsibility, whatever the contract says in general terms.

The other direction is genuinely allocable. Bulbs, batteries, simple consumable
replacements, and damage arising from the tenant's own intent or negligence can
properly be put on the tenant.

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">A blanket clause is not a settled question</p>
  <p>Writing "all repair costs are borne by the tenant" does not mean that
  sentence will hold for every situation it appears to cover. It is worth reading
  the clause you are being asked to sign as a starting position rather than as the
  answer — particularly in an older building, where the things most likely to fail
  are exactly the things the landlord's obligation reaches.</p>
</div>

## 2. Restoration is not "make it new"

At the end of the lease the tenant returns the property. That does not extend to
replacing everything that has aged and worn through ordinary use.

What is actually in dispute is narrower: damage to walls, structural alterations
made without permission, excessive soiling, broken fittings, and installations
put in without consent.

Which is why the single most useful thing a tenant does happens on **move-in
day**, not move-out day.

<div class="steps">
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">1</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Photograph every wall, floor and ceiling</strong> before any furniture goes in.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">2</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Film a continuous walkthrough</strong> — it timestamps the whole unit in one file.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">3</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Close in on existing marks</strong>: scuffs, stains, cracked tiles, silicone, window seals.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">4</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Test and record the fittings</strong> — boiler, taps, drainage, ventilation, every switch.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">5</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Send the landlord a dated message</strong> noting anything already damaged, and keep the reply.</div></div>
</div>

Without evidence of the condition on day one, a restoration argument has no
anchor and tends to resolve against whoever cannot prove their version.

## 3. The address filing is the protection — and for foreigners it is a different filing

Opposing power comes from delivery of the property plus the address filing. Not
from the contract, and not from having paid.

Foreign nationals do not make a resident registration, and guides written in
Korean say 전입신고 without qualification — which leaves foreign tenants either
assuming the protection is unavailable to them or assuming the contract is
enough. Neither is right.

There are
[two recognised equivalents](/tracked/foreign-resident-address-registration-equivalent/).
A registered foreign national who reports or changes their **stay address** to the
leased housing can be recognised as meeting the registration requirement. For
overseas Koreans, the **domestic residence report** does the same work.

So the sequence a foreign tenant has to connect is: alien registration card,
actual move-in, and a stay-address change report at the leased address — on the
same day the balance is paid, not eventually.

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">A stay address that does not match the lease</p>
  <p>Leaving the immigration record pointing at an old address for months while
  living somewhere else is the version of this that causes real trouble. The
  filing is what carries the protection. If it names a different property, the
  question of whether the requirement was met at the relevant time becomes an
  argument rather than a fact.</p>
</div>

## 4. Winning on paper and losing on procedure

A tenant with opposing power and a fixed date still has to act inside the
enforcement procedure. Exercising priority repayment in an auction can require a
**demand for distribution**, and missing it can mean not being paid out of a
process you were entitled to be paid from.

And case law treats the requirements — delivery and the address filing — as
having to be **maintained** through to the closing date for distribution demands.

Which produces the trap: a tenant whose deposit has not come back, who moves out
and transfers their address to a new home, can break the very continuity the
protection rests on.

## 5. The lease registration order is the instrument for that

A lease has ended, the deposit has not been returned, and the tenant has to
leave — a job in another city, a new place already committed.

Simply moving out and handing back possession puts the accrued opposing power
and priority repayment at risk.

The lease registration order exists for exactly this. Once the lease registration
is completed, the tenant can move out and change their address while retaining
the opposing power and priority repayment already acquired.

<div class="callout callout-note">
  <p class="callout-title">Applied for is not registered</p>
  <p>The protection follows the completed registration, not the application. Check
  that the registration has actually gone on before the removal van is booked —
  the order of operations is the whole of the protection here, and it is not
  recoverable afterwards.</p>
</div>

## 6. Being a small-deposit tenant is not being safe

There is a separate regime for small-deposit tenants: a portion of the deposit
can be repaid ahead of other secured creditors. It is not the same thing as the
fixed-date priority, and it is not a guarantee.

| Seoul | Figure |
|---|---|
| Qualifying deposit ceiling | [₩165,000,000](/tracked/small-tenant-priority-threshold/) |
| Maximum protected ahead of secured creditors | [Up to ₩55,000,000](/tracked/small-tenant-priority-amount/) |

Other regions have their own, lower, thresholds — the Seoul figures do not travel.

Read the two rows together. A tenant with a ₩160,000,000 deposit qualifies, and
has ₩55,000,000 protected ahead of secured creditors. The other ₩105,000,000
ranks by fixed date like any other claim.

There are further limits: payouts are capped by reference to a share of the
housing's value, and the tenant must have met the opposing-power requirements
before the auction commencement registration.

So "my deposit is under the small-deposit line, therefore I am fine" is
reasoning from the wrong number — the qualifying ceiling, rather than the
protected amount.

## The order that holds, at both ends

  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">1</div>

  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">2</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Living there</strong> — keep the immigration stay address matching the lease, and keep repair requests in writing.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">3</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Deposit not returned</strong> — do not move out and do not transfer the address yet.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">4</div>

  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">5</div>

</div>

The dangerous moment in a Korean residential lease is not signing the contract.
It is paying the balance, and then it is leaving — because both are moments when
the tenant does something irreversible on the assumption that the paperwork
already protects them.
