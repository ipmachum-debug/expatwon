Registering a business in Korea means taking on a tax that is not levied
on your profit at all.

VAT is collected, not earned. You charge it to your customer, you deduct
what you paid on your own purchases, and you remit the difference. Your
margin never enters the calculation.

Which is why a foreign owner faces no special VAT regime. Run a taxable
business here and the Value-Added Tax Act applies exactly as it does to
a Korean-owned one. The decisions that matter are made somewhere else
entirely — at registration.

## The threshold is ₩104,000,000, not ₩80,000,000

A simplified taxpayer is an individual business whose supply value in the
**preceding year was under ₩104,000,000**.

The ₩80M figure still printed in a lot of material is the old one.
Working from it will put you in the wrong category.

Three qualifications matter more than the number:

- **Some sectors are excluded regardless of turnover** — mining, parts of
  manufacturing, wholesale, licensed professions and certain property
  leasing among them.
- **Property leasing and taxable entertainment venues** keep their own,
  far lower line: **₩48,000,000**.
- **A corporation can never be a simplified taxpayer.** Set up a company
  and you file as a general taxpayer from the start — one more input into
  [the sole-proprietor-versus-corporation decision](/business/sole-proprietor-vs-corporation-korea/).

## General taxpayers: output minus input

The rate is **10%**, and the arithmetic is a subtraction:

> output VAT − deductible input VAT = VAT payable (or refundable)

On ₩100,000,000 of taxable sales, output VAT is ₩10,000,000. If you
incurred ₩6,000,000 of properly documented input VAT on equipment, rent
and materials in the same period, you remit ₩4,000,000.

And when input exceeds output — a fit-out, a machine, opening
inventory — **the difference is refunded to you.**

That refund is the single most important feature of general status, and
the reason a café, restaurant, workshop or e-commerce business with real
start-up spend should not choose simplified status on turnover alone.

## Simplified taxpayers: turnover × sector ratio × 10%

Simplified taxpayers do not remit 10% of sales. Turnover is multiplied by
a **sector value-added ratio** first, then by 10%.

| Sector | Ratio | Effective on turnover |
|---|---|---|
| Retail, recyclable material collection, food service | 15% | 1.5% |
| Manufacturing, agriculture, forestry, fishery, small-parcel transport | 20% | 2.0% |
| Accommodation | 25% | 2.5% |
| Construction, other transport, warehousing, information & communications, other services | 30% | 3.0% |
| Financial & insurance services, professional/scientific/technical services, facility management & business support & rental services, property-related services, property leasing | 40% | 4.0% |

So a restaurant's output VAT lands near 1.5% of turnover, a
manufacturer's near 2%, a professional service's near 4%. Then the
purchase credit is applied — and that is where the two regimes part
company.

## The 0.5% credit, and the refund that never comes

A simplified taxpayer does not credit input VAT the way a general
taxpayer does. The credit is **0.5% of the supply value of purchases**
evidenced by tax invoices and other qualifying documents.

And more decisively: **a simplified taxpayer's excess is never refunded.**
Where the credit exceeds the payable amount, the excess is treated as
nil.

  <p>Spend ₩100,000,000 on fit-out and equipment in your first year. As a
  general taxpayer, the VAT inside that spend is credited and the excess
  refunded. As a simplified taxpayer, you credit 0.5% of it and the rest
  is simply a cost. No later change of status recovers it.</p>
</div>

<p class="hand">If you are spending real money before you sell anything, run both numbers at registration. This is the one that is genuinely irreversible.</p>

## Side by side

| | General | Simplified |
|---|---|---|
| Who | Any individual or corporation | Individuals under ₩104M only |
| Output | 10% of supply value | Turnover × sector ratio × 10% |
| Input credit | Full, on qualifying evidence | 0.5% of purchase supply value |
| Refund | Yes, when input exceeds output | **Never** |
| Tax invoices | Issued as a matter of course | Depends on turnover — see below |
| Filing | Individuals twice a year; corporations more often | Once a year, with an exception |

Which suits you follows from the shape of the business, not its size.
Consumer-facing, low purchases, little equipment — simplified is often
cheaper. Business customers, or meaningful capital spend — general
usually wins, sometimes by a wide margin.

## ₩48,000,000 exempts the payment, not the return

Where a simplified taxpayer's supply value for the period comes in
**under ₩48,000,000**, the obligation to *pay* VAT is waived.

The obligation to *file* is not.

You still submit the return setting out sales and purchases for the
period. The filing is what establishes that the exemption applies. "My
turnover was under ₩48M so I didn't need to file" is wrong, and it is
expensive — because a missed filing carries its own penalty whether or
not any tax was due.

## Whether you can issue a tax invoice also turns on ₩48,000,000

"Simplified taxpayers can't issue tax invoices" is another
over-simplification.

- Preceding-year supply value **under ₩48,000,000**, and certain newly
  registered simplified taxpayers: **receipts**, not tax invoices.
- **At or above ₩48,000,000**: a tax invoice obligation applies within
  defined limits.

This is a commercial fact before it is a tax one. A business customer
needs a tax invoice to claim its own credit; if you cannot issue one, you
are a more expensive supplier than your price suggests. Check your own
position against your registration status and last year's supply value
before you quote B2B work.

## The filing calendar is different for individuals and companies

The year splits into two taxable periods:

| Period | Covers |
|---|---|
| First | 1 January – 30 June |
| Second | 1 July – 31 December |

**Individual general taxpayers** normally file final returns twice: in
July and the following January.

**Corporations** run the fuller cycle:

<div class="steps">
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">1</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Q1 preliminary</strong> — January to March, filed and paid in April.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">2</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>First period final</strong> — January to June, filed and paid in July.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">3</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Q3 preliminary</strong> — July to September, filed and paid in October.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">4</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Second period final</strong> — July to December, filed and paid the following January.</div></div>
</div>

Individual general taxpayers frequently receive a **preliminary
assessment notice** instead of filing a preliminary return. Do not map a
company's calendar onto a sole proprietorship.

## Simplified taxpayers file once a year — unless

The simplified taxable period is the whole calendar year, with the final
return in the following January.

The exception catches people out: a simplified taxpayer who **issued tax
invoices during the first half** may have 1 January to 30 June treated as
a taxable period, with a July filing and payment. The NTS includes such
taxpayers in its first-period final-return guidance.

So "simplified means one filing every January" is only true until you
issue a tax invoice.

## Evidence decides your VAT more than the rate does

Spending money is not the same as being able to deduct it. The deduction
requires evidence the law recognises:

## VAT and income tax are different taxes

A recurring confusion among first-time owners.

**VAT** is indirect: it sits on transactions, and the amount turns on
output minus input. **Income tax** is direct: it sits on what you
actually earned.

Turnover of ₩100,000,000 with ₩70,000,000 of deductible expenses gives
roughly ₩30,000,000 of business income. Income tax is computed from that
figure; VAT is computed from an entirely separate one.

Paying VAT does not reduce income tax, and filing income tax does not
discharge VAT. They are two calendars and two calculations, and both are
mandatory. Where you have staff, payroll withholding is a third —
covered in [hiring your first employee](/employment/hiring-your-first-employee-in-korea/).

## Missing the deadline costs more than the tax

Late filing is not simply late payment.

- **Failure to file:** generally **20%** of the unreported tax, rising to
  **40%** where the non-filing is by improper means.
- **Late payment:** a separate charge, computed on the unpaid amount by
  days elapsed at the statutory rate.

Which is why a period with negligible turnover still deserves a filed
return. The penalty attaches to the missed obligation, not to the size of
the business.

## Crossing into general status is a pricing event

Grow past the threshold and you convert. Output calculation, input
credit, tax-invoice obligations and filing frequency all change at once.

The part that catches owners: if you have been quoting **VAT-inclusive
prices** and you hold the price after conversion, your margin absorbs the
VAT. A business approaching ₩104,000,000 should review pricing and
customer contracts *before* the switch, not after the first return.

The reverse move exists too. A simplified taxpayer who needs the fit-out
refund or has to issue tax invoices can **waive simplified status**. But
having opted into general status, returning to simplified is restricted
for a period — so weigh it on more than one year's refund.
