In Korea, a foreigner's mobile number is not merely a way to make calls.
Bank accounts, card issuance, internet banking, government logins,
delivery and shopping apps — a large share of Korean services runs
through **phone-based identity verification**. Which reframes the plan
decision entirely: before comparing ₩20,000 against ₩70,000 a month, the
first question is whether *your alien-registration identity and your
carrier's subscriber record connect cleanly enough for Korea's
verification systems to recognize you.*

That is not an accident of app design. The Telecommunications Business
Act requires carriers to verify subscriber identity at contract — a
Korean phone number is a **real-name-verified contract**, not a SIM
serial. This is why sorting the phone comes before
[sorting the bank](/banking/how-to-open-a-bank-account-in-korea-as-a-foreigner/).

## The passport phone and the ARC phone play different roles

On arrival, a passport buys you a prepaid SIM or travel eSIM — LG U+'s
current foreigner eSIM products issue a Korean 010 number with voice and
data on passport verification alone. For a short visit, done.

But a tourist line opened on a passport is not a settler's financial
phone. Once the ARC arrives, the carrier record needs to exist under
your **alien-registration identity**, because banks, card issuers, and
PASS match not just your number but your name and registration number
against the carrier's subscriber data. The stable sequence:

> passport-based prepaid/eSIM → ARC issued → new own-name line (or
> subscriber-record update) under the ARC

Whether an existing number can keep its digits while the identity
behind it changes depends on the carrier and product — ask the
operator.

## MNO vs MVNO: same towers, different business

SK Telecom, KT, and LG U+ are MNOs — they own the networks. 알뜰폰
operators are MVNOs, wholesale-leasing those same networks and selling
their own plans. An SKT-network MVNO customer is not on worse towers
than an SKT customer; the price gap comes from distribution, call
centers, retail stores, bundles, handset subsidies, and marketing —
not degraded radio.

The 2026 market on MVNO Hub shows plans around ₩10,000-something for
~10GB, and ₩10,000–30,000 for 11GB to 100GB+ or throttled-unlimited
data. Promotions dip below ₩10,000 — some to ₩10 or ₩990 — but jump
sharply after the promo window. So "알뜰폰 = ₩10,000 flat" is wrong in
both directions: compute on the **post-promotion standard rate**.

### The savings are real — when the comparison is honest

A heavy-data user paying an MNO ₩80,000 who can match usage on a
₩25,000 MVNO saves ₩55,000/month — **₩660,000 a year, roughly ₩1.98M
over three years**. For someone with their own handset and no bundle
needs, that is one of the cleanest fixed-cost cuts in an expat budget.

The honesty condition: if your MNO price includes family bundles,
internet bundles, contract discounts, or loyalty pricing, compare
**actual billed amounts**, not list prices. A ₩75,000 line that anchors
₩25,000 of household internet discounts is not really ₩75,000 — which
is why settled families should run the household formula below, not a
single line.

## Yes, MVNOs can do identity verification — with a check

"알뜰폰 can't do bank verification" is mostly outdated. Official PASS
guidance covers MVNO users on all three networks, and foreigners can
use phone identity verification and PASS certificates when the app's
conditions are met. The structure:

> MNO real-name line → phone verification works
> MVNO real-name line → verification and PASS work **if the operator
> supports them**

That conditional is the practical difference: PASS itself notes some
MVNOs carry service limitations. So a foreigner for whom financial and
government authentication is mission-critical should verify **four
things before price**: ARC-based activation, phone identity
verification, PASS support, and foreigner-capable customer support.

## Cheap plans outsource the support cost to you

The big three's real advantage is not speed — it is offline stores and
call centers that handle handset changes, USIM swaps, subscriber-record
corrections, and loss reports quickly. For foreigners the gap widens: a
Korean's record correction is a counter formality; a foreigner's may
require extra identity documents. Many MVNOs run no offline presence at
all, and when a foreigner's automated self-activation fails, resolution
can stretch across days of calls. If saving ₩20,000 costs hours of
support labor at each hiccup, recompute the economics.

Hence the practical arc for new arrivals: **start on an MNO while your
identity, accounts, and PASS stabilize; migrate to an MVNO once the
verification stack is proven.**

## Verification failures are data mismatches, not phone problems

Phone verification matches name, birth date or ARC number, carrier, and
number against the carrier's database — one divergent field fails the
check, and PASS states it will not send the authentication request when
customer data does not match.

The classic foreign failure is the romanized name. If the ARC says
`NGUYEN VAN AN` but the carrier holds `NGUYEN VANAN` or
`VAN AN NGUYEN`, a human sees one person; the system sees different
values. (Keep the claim calibrated: no official statistic pins "90% of
failures" on name spacing — ARC-number typos, un-propagated record
changes, prepaid-line verification limits, and post-porting sync delays
all contribute.)

The rules that prevent it:

- **The ARC is canonical.** Match the carrier record to the ARC's
  printed English name; use the same rendering at banks, cards, and
  insurers — the same
  [single-identity discipline that protects your credit file](/loans/korean-credit-score-for-foreigners-nice-kcb/).
- **Never let a clerk "helpfully" reorder your name Korean-style** or
  truncate it for convenience.
- **After a correction, allow propagation time.** Verification partners
  sync on their own schedules — retry after a pause instead of
  hammering account-opening flows minutes after the fix.

And a self-activation failure is not an eligibility verdict: automated
onboarding has no human override, so if your registration data is
correct but the robot says no, ask that MVNO's support for
assisted/offline activation — or simply choose an MVNO that supports
foreigners properly.

## Prepaid is a bridge, not a foundation

For C-3 visitors and pre-ARC arrivals, prepaid USIM/eSIM is the right
tool — passport-based, no credit screening, sold at airports by the
MNOs themselves. But a settler heading toward accounts, cards,
brokerage, insurance, and government services needs the ARC-based
own-name line: a mis-founded telecom contract resurfaces as repeated
verification errors across every financial service — including the
[non-face-to-face flows that unlock limited accounts](/banking/korean-limited-accounts-hando-gyejwa-explained/).

One taxonomy fix while we're here: **eSIM ≠ MVNO.** eSIM is a SIM
technology (profile downloaded to the device instead of a card); both
MNOs and MVNOs offer it. The decision axes are `MNO vs MVNO` and
`USIM vs eSIM`, independently — a visitor might best fit a passport
MNO eSIM, a settled resident an ARC-based MVNO eSIM.

## When the expensive carrier is worth it

Picture a just-landed E-7 or D-8 employee stacking alien registration,
bank account, credit card, certificates, home internet, and company
apps in the same month. For that person, ₩30,000/month extra buys an
offline counter that verifies identity documents in person and fixes
errors on the spot — cheap insurance during the highest-friction weeks
of Korean life. Companies managing multiple foreign staff get a second
dividend from consolidating corporate and personal lines with one
operator: fewer HR hours per incident.

The mirror case: a resident two or three years in, with ARC, bank,
PASS, and cards all stable, faces near-zero migration friction — and
has no reason to donate ₩500,000–700,000 a year to brand loyalty.

## Families compare households, not lines

For F-6 couples and settled families, "MVNO is always cheaper" fails
often enough to check. Two to four mobile lines bundled with internet
and IPTV at one MNO can price well below the sum of parts:

> **Real telecom cost = mobile + internet + TV + add-ons − family /
> bundle / contract discounts**

Worked example: moving two lines to MVNOs saves ₩30,000 each —
₩60,000/month — but forfeits ₩35,000 of internet/family discounts. Net
saving: **₩25,000**, not ₩60,000. Run MVNO comparisons through the
same formula.

### And promotions through a 24-month average

The 2026 MVNO market is dense with 6-, 7-, and 12-month teaser rates.
A plan at ₩5,900 for 7 months, then ₩29,700:

> (5,900 × 7 + 29,700 × 17) ÷ 24 ≈ **₩22,760/month**

The advertised ₩5,900 and the lived cost differ by 4×. Long-term
residents should decide on the 24-month total, never the first bill.

## Port the number; mind the ripple effects

A Korean number change touches more systems than expected — banks,
cards, insurers, brokers, delivery apps, government portals all hold
the old number, and PASS certificates need reissuing on number or
device changes. Which is why migrating to an MVNO via **number porting**
(keeping your 010 number) is the settler's default: same digits, new
carrier, and most financial contact-update chores disappear.

Also decouple two administrative systems in your head: the
[stay-address change report](/cost-of-living/rental-deposit-protection-korea/)
to immigration does not auto-update your carrier's (or bank's) address
records — each institution maintains its own. After a move, file the
immigration report first (for jeonse tenants it anchors your legal
priority), then sweep bank, card, insurer, and carrier addresses
separately.

## The cheapest carrier is not automatically the best one

- **1–3 month visitors:** passport-based prepaid eSIM/USIM — simplest.
- **New ARC holders:** an MNO's offline support often out-earns its
  premium during the account-card-PASS setup phase.
- **Stable long-term residents:** MVNO efficiency wins — especially
  with a self-bought handset and no bundles.
- **Families:** compare the MNO bundle total against MVNO per-line
  totals with the household formula.

The selection order is fixed: **does own-name verification work → do
PASS and financial services connect → how much support do you need →
then compare price.** In Korea a phone is a living-cost line item and a
piece of financial infrastructure at once. A line ₩20,000 cheaper that
intermittently fails bank verification is expensive; a line ₩20,000
richer than a settled resident needs is a subscription to nothing. The
MNO/MVNO choice was never about signal quality — it is about **how much
you are willing to pay for administrative convenience**, and the honest
answer changes as your Korean life matures.
