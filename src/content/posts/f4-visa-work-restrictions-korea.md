An employer asks what sounds like a formality: *you have F-4, so you can work
anywhere, right?*

The safe answer is no — and the reason is structural rather than fussy. The
decree that gives F-4 its unusual freedom creates the limits in the same
provision. A person with this status is generally not confined by the activity
categories that bind other residence statuses, except for simple labour, work
contrary to good customs or social order, and occupations closed to protect
the public interest or the domestic employment order.

Which is why two statements that contradict each other both sound right. *F-4
holders can work freely* — broadly, yes. *Some jobs are closed to F-4* — also
yes. The second sentence is the boundary of the first.

## The list in front of you may be the wrong list

The controlling instrument is **Ministry of Justice Notice No. 2026-65,
effective 12 February 2026**, which replaced the 2023 notice.

That date carries extra weight because Korea moved on overseas-Korean
residence policy at the same time, consolidating the former H-2/F-4 structure
toward F-4 treatment. So the old reflex — *that's an H-2 job, F-4 can't do it*
— is no longer a reliable way to reason about a current case either.

A guide written in 2022, 2023 or even early 2026 can carry an occupation list
that is not merely dated but wrong in both directions. The current
simple-labour restriction runs to

## What is actually closed

**Delivery, in nearly every form.** Parcel delivery, quick-service couriers,
delivery-agency workers, restaurant delivery, supermarket and
department-store delivery, regular food and beverage delivery, newspaper
delivery and other regular delivery occupations.

**Building cleaning.** Offices, public buildings, officetels, apartments,
hospitals, hotels — plus certain transport-equipment and industrial-equipment
cleaning.

**Apartment security and simple building management.** Apartment and villa
security, building patrol, certain building-management duties.

**Waste and street cleaning.** Household, bulky, food and recycling
collection; street and public-place cleaning; environmental monitoring.

**And a long tail that is easy to miss**: moving workers, postal delivery,
leaflet and poster distribution, parking attendants, meter readers, gas
inspectors, forest-fire monitors, shoe-shining, manual laundry and ironing,
ticket inspection, and other simple service work.

Then, separately, the public-interest group: foot-care workers, bath
attendants, wedding-service assistants, karaoke-room service workers, certain
amusement-facility workers such as PC-room or video-room attendants, golf
caddies, liquor-service workers, street and mobile vendors.

That second group is the reason "is this simple labour?" is not a sufficient
test. A golf caddie is not classified as simple labour and is still closed.

## The employer's industry is not the employee's occupation

A logistics company hires four F-4 holders: a software developer, an
accountant, a warehouse scheduler and someone who drives parcels to customers'
homes. One employer, four occupations, and not one immigration answer between
them.

| Three jobs, one apartment complex | |
| --- | --- |
| Accountant — invoices, payroll, management fees | Not converted into simple labour by the employer being an apartment complex |
| Security guard — entry control, patrol | Apartment security is on the current list |
| Cleaner — hallways, lifts, common areas | Building cleaning is on the current list |

The same exercise at a restaurant separates a kitchen employee, an accountant
and a delivery rider — and only the third is expressly restricted.

So neither side should stop at the business-registration category or the
contract title. Ask: what work will actually be performed, which occupational
classification is closest, and does that occupation appear in any of the three
groups in the current notice.

## A title cannot launder the activity

If the daily work is collecting parcels, driving a route and handing packages
to customers, writing *Logistics Operations Manager* on the contract does not
move the classification. Immigration follows the substance of the activity.

This matters most for titles that are genuinely ambiguous — facility manager,
customer-service staff, field manager, operations assistant — because each can
describe a permitted role or a restricted one, and the document gives no way
to tell which. Casual English translation of a Korean job title is where this
usually goes wrong.

## The regional exception, stated accurately

The notice provides that certain F-4 holders connected with Ministry of
Justice regional-specialised visa project areas can be exempted from the
simple-labour and public-interest restrictions where the specified residence
and regional-employment conditions are satisfied. It does **not** lift the
good-customs restrictions.

What it is not: *F-4 holders can do simple labour outside Seoul.* The
exception is tied to designated population-decline areas and the programme
itself, not to being somewhere rural.

Before starting restricted work on this basis, confirm the area is currently
designated, that the individual's situation falls within the programme, that
the intended workplace is inside the permitted geographic scope, and that any
required immigration procedure is complete.

## What is at stake if the classification is wrong

Broad work authorisation does not suspend the Immigration Act. Employment
outside the permitted scope can be an immigration violation, with
investigation, monetary penalties and consequences for continued residence —
and the Act carries criminal ceilings for specified unauthorised-employment
violations.

The practical exposure is larger than one job, because an extension, another
permission, a status change or a review of past compliance all come later and
all look backward. *Other F-4 workers here do the same thing* is not a source
of law.

Employers have their own reason to check: seeing an F-4 card is not the end of
the review, since unauthorised foreign employment can reach employers and
intermediaries too. Document the actual occupation before the first day, and
where it is genuinely unclear, verify with immigration rather than after an
inspection.

## Five steps, in order

Write the duties before the title — *drives parcels to homes*, *cleans
hallways*, *prepares food*, *runs payroll*. Match them to the Korean Standard
Classification of Occupations, which is what the notice itself uses. Test all
three restriction groups, not just simple labour. Check whether a genuine
regional exception applies rather than assuming distance from Seoul creates
one. Then, if classification is still uncertain, confirm with the Immigration
Contact Center on 1345 or the competent office **before** work starts.

<p class="hand">Write what the person will do each day. Everything else follows from that paragraph.</p>
