The decision is usually framed as price and location. The difference that
actually matters is who your attendance belongs to.

At a university-affiliated Korean language institute, **attendance is a
condition of your status**, tracked and reported. At a private academy it
is a matter between you and the school. That one difference reshapes
everything else: the term structure, the intensity, the level system, and
what happens on the day you decide to skip.

<p class="hand">Neither is the better school. They are answers to different questions.</p>

**Whether an academy can sponsor a visa at all** is the separate and
prior question, answered in
[the D-4 visa for studying Korean](/study/d-4-visa-korean-language-study/).
This guide assumes you have settled that and are choosing what to study
in.

## The term structure decides your year

An institute year is built from fixed blocks. Four terms of about ten
weeks, starting at set points in the year, with a placement test at the
front and a level test at the end.

That structure has consequences people do not anticipate.

| | University institute | Private academy |
|---|---|---|
| When you can start | Set term dates | Most weeks |
| Length of commitment | A full term | Often monthly |
| Hours | Around four a day, weekday mornings or afternoons | Whatever you book |
| Class size | A class, streamed by level | Small groups or one-to-one |
| If you fall behind | Repeat the level next term | Adjust the lessons |
| Attendance | Tracked and reported | Between you and the school |
| Certificate | Level completion, recognised | Varies |

**Missing an intake costs you a whole term**, not a week. Someone whose
visa lands late, or who arrives between terms, waits — and pays for
somewhere to live while waiting. Line up the term dates against your visa
timeline before you book flights.

## Attendance is not a school rule

This is the part worth reading twice.

A study status carries the expectation that you are studying. Institutes
track attendance and report it, and falling below the threshold is not
handled the way a university handles a student who skips lectures. It puts
your standing with the school and your standing with immigration at risk
in the same moment.

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">The two problems are not equally fixable</p>
  <p>A school problem can usually be discussed. A status problem is
  administrative, and it follows you into every later application — an
  extension, a change of status, a future visa. Protect the attendance record
  as if it were part of your visa file, because in effect it is.</p>
</div>

Illness and emergencies are provided for. They are provided for by
**telling the office at the time and keeping the documentation** — a
clinic receipt, a certificate — not by explaining afterwards. The
difference between an absence that was reported and one that was noticed
is the whole difference.

## The six levels, and where people actually stall

Institute programmes run six levels, broadly aligned to TOPIK.

<div class="steps">
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">1–2</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Survival and foundations.</strong> Hangul is assumed or taught fast. Most people move through these on schedule.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">3–4</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Where the repeats happen.</strong> The grammar load jumps and the material stops being about daily life. Plan for the possibility of repeating one of these; it is common and it is not failure.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">5–6</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Academic and professional register.</strong> This is the range universities and employers care about, and it is where the certificate starts being worth something outside the classroom.</div></div>
</div>

Six levels at one per term is six terms — **about eighteen months of
continuous full-time study**, and that assumes passing each level first
time. Anyone quoting a year is quoting a best case.

## The institute certificate and TOPIK are different things

An institute level is that institute's assessment of you. TOPIK is the
national test.

They correlate, and they are not interchangeable. Universities, employers
and immigration processes overwhelmingly ask for **TOPIK scores**. A
student who completes level 6 and never sits the test has a certificate
that means a great deal inside the building and less outside it.

```
Institute level 6  ≠  TOPIK level 6
```

Sit the test **while you are still studying**. Momentum, structure and
classmates all disappear the week the programme ends, and the people who
plan to take it "in a couple of months" often take it in a year.

## Choosing between them, honestly

<div class="callout callout-note">
  <p class="callout-title">A short decision rule</p>
  <p>Does your right to be in Korea depend on studying? If yes, you need an
  accredited institute and the academy question does not arise. If no, ask
  what you actually want — a structured qualification, or better spoken
  Korean around the life you already have. Institutes are much better at the
  first. Academies are frequently better at the second.</p>
</div>

Two more things to weigh, both of which cost money if you get them wrong:

**Where the institute is** changes your total cost more than its tuition
does, because housing does the moving. That comparison is in
[Seoul against a regional city for language study](/study/seoul-vs-regional-korea-language-study-cost/).

**What comes after** should shape the level you aim for. Stopping at level
4 is a reasonable choice if the goal is living comfortably; it is a poor
one if the plan is a Korean degree or a job that runs in Korean. Decide
that before you book the first term, because it determines how many terms
you are budgeting for.
