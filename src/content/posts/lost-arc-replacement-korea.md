A missing Korean residence card can ruin a perfectly ordinary week. A bank asks for it, mobile verification fails, and an airline wants proof that you can return to Korea. Online advice adds a worry: *you must replace your Alien Registration Card (ARC) within 14 days.*

That last claim needs correcting. The **old 14-day submission phrase was removed from the relevant replacement provision in 2020**. The version of **Immigration Act Enforcement Decree Article 42(2)** effective on October 2, 2026, requires a replacement application with **one photograph**, but does **not** contain that former 14-day phrase. This does not prove that every related reporting duty has disappeared, or that delaying replacement is harmless. It establishes something narrower and more useful: do not present the historical 14-day text as today's national deadline for this application.

There are three separate problems to solve: the missing physical card, the validity of your underlying permission to stay, and the institutions that need proof of identity. Keeping them separate is faster and safer than trying to solve everything with a scan of the old card.

## The old rule and the current rule are not the same

Older versions of Enforcement Decree Article 42(2) directed applicants to submit a replacement application within 14 days after the reason for reissue arose. The version in force before the 2020 amendment still included that time limit. Article 42(2) was amended on February 18, 2020, and its currently effective wording instead states the application-and-photo requirement without the former 14-day phrase.

| Question | Historical provision | Provision effective October 2, 2026 |
|---|---|---|
| Was lost-card replacement covered? | Yes | Yes, under Article 42(1)(1) |
| Did the replacement paragraph say “within 14 days”? | Yes | No |
| Photograph requirement | One photo in the later historical version | One photo |
| Where is the application submitted? | Residence-jurisdiction immigration office | Residence-jurisdiction immigration office |
| Does the paragraph guarantee a card-processing time? | No | No |

This comparison is supported by the historical and current versions of the decree, not by an unofficial immigration checklist. A removed deadline is **not** the same as a promise of unlimited time, protection from identity misuse, or permission to ignore an upcoming visa expiry. Apply promptly, especially if you must travel, verify your identity at a bank, or renew your stay.

## First determine what has actually been lost

A lost physical card does not by itself establish that the residence permission recorded in the immigration system has expired. Equally, a valid residence status does not force a commercial bank or an airline to accept an old image of a card as identification. The physical document and the underlying immigration authorization perform different jobs.

Use the distinction below before calling anyone:

| Situation | Immediate concern | What to verify |
|---|---|---|
| Card lost; passport retained | Replacement and everyday identification | Current application route and interim acceptable documents |
| Card and passport stolen | Both Korean identification and passport/travel identity | Embassy passport procedure, immigration sequencing and fraud prevention |
| Card lost near visa expiry | Replacement **and** lawful stay | Separate period-of-stay extension procedure and its deadline |
| Card lost before overseas trip | Re-entry evidence and airline boarding | Individual travel documentation with immigration and the airline |
| Card found after reporting loss | Whether the previous document remains usable | Office instructions; do not assume a replacement/cancellation has no effect |

Do not treat your residence card, passport, visa sticker, period-of-stay approval and electronic authentication credentials as interchangeable documents. One agency may verify a record internally; another may require a document you can physically or digitally present through an approved system.

## Which losses qualify for replacement?

Article 42(1) lists several grounds for reissuing a foreign registration card. Loss is explicitly included in **item 1**. Other grounds include a damaged or unusable card, insufficient space on the card, a relevant change of status or registration information, and a broader official replacement program. The ground matters because it affects what must accompany the application.

Under Article 42(2), applicants attach **one photo** to the prescribed reissuance application. When the application arises from the specified grounds in items 2 through 6, the **original card** must also be attached. Loss under item 1 is different for an obvious reason: the original card cannot be produced. This is a statutory distinction, not permission to invent a substitute-document requirement.

If the card was damaged rather than lost, bring the damaged original. If a change of name or nationality is involved, ask whether the relevant registration-change process must be handled as well. Reissuing a piece of plastic does not itself correct inconsistent official records.

## The statutory fee is ₩35,000

The amount is not a guess based on a blog's last visit to an immigration office. **Immigration Act Enforcement Rule Article 72(10)** sets **₩35,000** for issuance or reissuance of a foreign registration card. Article 72(10-2) separately addresses permanent-resident card reissuance, also at ₩35,000; other permanent-resident rules should not be automatically substituted for an ordinary lost-card case.

The payment citation has a **separate effective-date trap**. The Enforcement Rule carries a 2026 amendment that *publishes* a restructured Article 73, with the cash/payment-proof rule moved to the main text of paragraph (1) and former subitem (a) deleted. **However, the amendment specifies a separate effective date of September 16, 2027 for Article 73.** As of October 9, 2026, the **existing Article 73(1)(a)** is still the operative provision for the card fee: cash or evidence of cash payment. A search result showing both versions is not proof that the later version has already taken effect. Ask the office what payment facilities it actually offers.

The fee provision tells you the statutory amount. It does **not** promise a particular office counter, an appointment-free visit, a credit-card option on every machine, a courier service, or a fixed turnaround. Confirm how the relevant immigration office accepts payment and how cards are collected or delivered.

## Prepare a usable application, not a bag of guesses

Start with the current immigration reissuance form, your passport, the required photograph, and the details needed to locate your foreign-registration record. The office may need to verify identity or clarify the loss circumstances. Confirm the exact appointment channel and any additional local-office instructions before traveling to the counter. If you no longer have your passport, address that complication with your embassy and Korean immigration rather than assuming an old card photograph resolves it.

A practical application packet has two layers. The first contains what the law establishes: the application, the photograph, the relevant identity record and the statutory fee. The second contains situation-specific supporting material, such as a police report if a theft was reported, appointment evidence, correspondence about an imminent trip, or proof of address if the office requests it. Do not promote the second layer into a universal statutory checklist without an official source.

Keep a copy of your submission and any dated receipt. Ask the clerk or official channel exactly what that receipt proves. It may prove filing; it should not be described as a universal substitute for a residence card in banks, airports or online identity systems.

## What to do during the first 24 hours

If the card might simply be at home, check the last known places it was used. If a wallet or bag was stolen, secure your bank cards and phone access first, then contact the relevant lost-property or police channel as appropriate. Describe events accurately; a false theft report is not a shortcut to faster paperwork.

Record the date the card went missing, the last known location, and who might have access to it. If you take photos for the record, do not expose the full registration number in a group chat or post a copy on social media. A card reveals enough personal data to make privacy and fraud prevention part of the response.

Keep service-provider conversations in writing where possible. A dated bank response saying which identification it will accept can be more useful than three contradictory phone conversations. A police report and a card-reissuance receipt serve different purposes; neither guarantees the other institution's decision.

## Banks and phone verification may become the urgent problem

A Korean bank's customer-identification workflow can depend on how the customer's name, residence identifier, passport and existing account were recorded. A photocopy may help a banker locate a file but can be insufficient for a restricted transaction. Digital services can fail because the telecom subscriber record, foreign-registration number and banking record no longer align.

Ask the bank two specific questions: “Which alternative documents will you accept until my card is reissued?” and “Do I need an in-person identity update after receiving the replacement?” Avoid a vague request such as “can I use my passport?”; acceptance may differ between checking an account balance, opening a new product and recovering blocked digital authentication.

If you have rent or utility payments due, test already authorized channels and arrange legitimate alternatives before the due date. Do not register a service under somebody else's identity to bypass verification. That could create a compliance or fraud issue independent of the lost card.

## A mobile residence card is not a universal workaround

Korean rules now address mobile foreign-registration cards, but the existence of a mobile format does not mean every person has one, can issue one after physical-card loss, or can use one for every purpose. The Enforcement Rule contains separate provisions on issue, validity and reissue of mobile residence documents, including changes when a physical document is reissued.

A person with an already-issued mobile credential should confirm whether it is still valid after reporting loss or receiving the physical replacement. A person without one should not assume instant issuance is available on their current phone. Banks, airlines and foreign authorities may have different acceptance policies. Treat official mobile identity as a separately verified option, not as a replacement for asking what the receiving institution requires.

## Travel is a three-party document problem

A traveler must satisfy at least three separate checkpoints: the carrier's boarding requirements, Korean immigration's exit and re-entry rules, and the destination's admission rules. A domestic immigration receipt may be relevant evidence but is not automatically enough at airline check-in. Likewise, a passport alone does not answer every residence-based re-entry question.

For a flight leaving in a few days, give the immigration authority and airline the same concrete facts: nationality, current status, passport validity, card-loss date, intended departure and return dates, and documents still in hand. Ask what they will accept **for the return journey**, not just for departure. Confirm whether a re-entry permit or exemption question arises under the person's actual status. Avoid buying an irreversible ticket solely because a forum said re-entry is automatic.

Consider a simple Monday-to-Friday example. Someone loses the card on Monday and has a Friday overseas business trip. The correct sequence is not to assume the Friday trip is impossible, nor to assume the Monday replacement receipt guarantees boarding. It is to check eligibility and documentation with both immigration and the carrier early enough to change the travel plan if necessary.

## Do not confuse replacement with extending your stay

A period-of-stay extension, a change in residence status and a replacement identification document are different administrative decisions. If the card is lost one week before your current authorized stay expires, a replacement application should not be described as extending that permission. Resolve the stay-expiry question with immigration directly and follow the relevant filing requirements independently.

The reverse is also true: the absence of a physical card does not automatically mean your permission ended yesterday. Use current official records, not the loss of an object, to establish your immigration position.

## When you receive the replacement

Check the printed name, identifying number, nationality, status information and expiry date against your official record before leaving the office if possible. If information is wrong, ask how to correct it. Keep the issuance record and update any bank, telecom provider or employer that actually requires the new card information.

If the missing card later turns up, do not casually alternate between the two documents. Ask whether the original was invalidated or must be surrendered. A printed expiry date on old plastic does not establish that it remains valid in the issuer's records.

## The practical bottom line

The most important distinction is not between “lost” and “not lost.” It is between **what the current law actually requires** and what a bank, airline or immigration office will accept in your specific circumstances. The old 14-day wording is gone from Article 42(2). The current ordinary replacement application calls for one photograph and the statutory card fee is ₩35,000. Those findings should reduce confusion—not encourage delay. Act promptly, maintain an evidence trail, and resolve travel or status-expiry questions as separate matters.
