Not being enrolled in Korean national health insurance does not stop you being
treated. Hospitals see self-paying patients as a matter of course, and the
international centres at the large university hospitals exist substantially for
them.

What changes is who pays. An enrolled patient splits the cost of covered
treatment with the insurer; someone without cover pays all of it.

<p class="hand">Uninsured and non-covered are two different words. Most confusion starts there.</p>

## Uninsured is not the same as non-covered

These get used interchangeably in English and they describe different things.

| | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Non-covered** | The treatment falls outside insurance benefits — for **everyone**, enrolled or not |
| **Uninsured / self-pay** | *You* have no cover, so treatment that would ordinarily be covered is billed to you in full |

Someone without enrolment is therefore not "receiving only non-covered care".
They are receiving ordinary care with no insurer contribution behind it, plus
whatever genuinely non-covered items appear on the bill — two different
mechanisms on one invoice.

The distinction matters when you claim, because a foreign insurer will ask what
the treatment was, not what Korean insurance would have done about it.

## There is no ratio to reverse

The common derivation looks reasonable and is wrong:

```
insured patient pays 30%
  → uninsured pays 100%
      → therefore 3.33× more
```

The cost-sharing proportion is not one national figure. It varies by treatment
setting and by institution type — a local clinic, a hospital, a general
hospital, a tertiary hospital, inpatient care, the emergency department and the
pharmacy do not share a single ratio.

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">So a self-pay price cannot be computed from home</p>
  <p>It comes from the hospital's own billing for the specific treatment. Any
  guide handing you a multiplier has substituted arithmetic for the enquiry you
  actually need to make.</p>
</div>

## Institution type moves the bill, without a fixed factor

Broadly, and without pretending to a formula:

<div class="steps">
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">1</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>A cold, a rash, simple abdominal pain</strong> — a local clinic is usually the sensible first stop, clinically and financially.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">2</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Specialist assessment or detailed imaging</strong> — a hospital or general hospital.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">3</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Serious or complex treatment</strong> — a tertiary hospital, where specialist consultation, tests, imaging and facility costs accumulate.</div></div>
</div>

Note also that tertiary hospitals operate a referral requirement within the
insured system — Severance, for instance, states that an insured patient
normally needs a referral from a primary institution to receive tertiary
benefits. A self-paying foreign patient sits outside that benefit process, which
is a reason to confirm the expected cost at booking rather than to assume the
route is simply open.

## The prices you *can* look up

For non-covered services, Korea publishes them.

The Health Insurance Review and Assessment Service discloses non-covered
treatment prices **by individual institution** — a list that reached
[753 items in 2026](/tracked/hira-disclosed-noncovered-items/), covering imaging,
ultrasound, vaccinations and a range of tests and procedures.

Prices for the same item differ between hospitals, which is the reason it is
published per institution rather than as a schedule.

```
identify the treatment
  → is it a non-covered item?
  → check prices by hospital
  → then book
```

<div class="callout callout-note">
  <p class="callout-title">This is the strongest cost tool available to you</p>
  <p>It beats every estimate in every guide, including this one, because it is
  specific to the procedure and the hospital. It does not cover what a hospital
  bills an uninsured patient for an ordinarily covered service — that has no
  equivalent public schedule, and remains a question for the hospital.</p>
</div>

## Emergencies are not a cost decision

Emergency care is billed differently from an outpatient visit — assessment,
tests, imaging, procedures, plus emergency-specific charges — and at night, at
weekends or at a tertiary hospital the total can be substantial. Without cover,
all of it is yours.

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">Do not route a real emergency through a clinic to save money</p>
  <p>Where there is a threat to life or a need for immediate treatment, call 119
  or go to an emergency department. Settle the money afterwards, with your
  insurer. The cost of getting this wrong is not financial.</p>
</div>

## What an international healthcare centre is actually for

The large university hospitals run international centres, and what they provide
is documented: appointment coordination, interpretation, connection to the
right department, cost estimates on request, and in some cases direct billing
with foreign insurers.

Seoul National University Hospital's centre is operated substantially for
foreign patients **without** Korean national health insurance, with support in
several languages.

There is no national rule that using one costs more. If cost is your concern,
ask before booking:

- the first consultation fee
- the specialist consultation fee
- expected test and imaging costs
- certificate and document fees
- whether an international patient administration or interpretation fee applies

That produces a number. "Is it 1.5 times or 3 times" produces an argument.

<div class="callout callout-note">
  <p class="callout-title">And weigh the administrative saving</p>
  <p>Booking, consultation, tests, payment and insurance paperwork are five
  separate problems in a language you may not read. A centre that handles them
  as one — and that can bill your insurer directly — may cost less in total than
  a cheaper route you have to navigate yourself. That is the second half of
  today's pair.</p>
</div>

## Ask for an estimate before anything large

For non-emergency inpatient care, surgery or detailed investigation, ask for an
expected cost in advance. Severance's international centre states that it can
provide an estimate before treatment on request; Seoul National University
Hospital takes a deposit against expected inpatient costs, with the final figure
depending on the treatment actually given.

Foreign insurers frequently want that estimate anyway before issuing a payment
guarantee.

## Who this guide is for

Short-term visitors, business travellers, people in Korea before enrolment
completes, and those outside the scheme. If you are employed here, you may be
enrolled sooner than you think — workplace subscribers do not serve the
residence waiting period that applies to regional enrolment, and some statuses
enrol on their own terms. Which of the three positions applies to you is in
[NHIS health insurance for foreigners](/insurance/nhis-korean-health-insurance-for-foreigners/).

Being uninsured in Korea is not about whether you can be treated. It is about
who pays first, what price applies, and what evidence you leave with — and the
last of those decides whether the money comes back.
