Opening a bank account in Korea is not an exotic financial product — it is an
identity check plus a purpose check. Pass both, and you get a normal deposit
account. The practical problems foreigners run into almost never come from
nationality; they come from arriving with the wrong documents, or from not
knowing that a freshly opened account may carry daily transaction limits.

The legal footing matters here. Since August 2024, Korean financial
institutions have an explicit statutory basis (under the Special Act on
Telecommunications-based Financial Fraud) to verify **why** you are opening an
account. The bank can request supporting documents, and if what you provide
does not sufficiently establish your purpose, it can open the account as a
**limited account (한도제한계좌)** instead of refusing you outright.

So the useful way to prepare is to answer two separate questions before you
walk into a branch: *what ID can I show*, and *what documents explain my
purpose*.

## What banks actually check: identity and purpose, not nationality

Being a foreign national does not by itself block you from normal banking in
Korea. What the bank verifies first is your identity — typically a passport
and, where you have one, your Alien Registration Card (ARC). The Financial
Supervisory Service's guidance for foreigners describes presenting a passport
or ARC at account opening, and notes that you can register a signature instead
of the Korean-style seal (도장).

The second layer is purpose, and this is where documentation varies:

- **Salary account** — an employment contract or certificate of employment
  (재직증명서) is the cleanest way to establish purpose.
- **Business account** — business registration documents or contracts.
- **General living** — banks may ask for proof of address or a Korean phone
  number to confirm contactability.

Some people do walk out of a branch with an account opened on a passport
alone. Treat that as branch discretion, not as a rule you can rely on. The
requirements differ between banks, between branches of the same bank, and
between customers with different residence status.

## An ARC makes everything easier

Long-term residents who hold an ARC have a much smoother time, for a simple
reason: the bank can anchor your file to a domestic ID number, address, and
phone number. That same domestic identity is what unlocks the services you
will actually live on later — internet banking, a check (debit) card, and
mobile phone identity verification.

Short-term visitors, or new arrivals whose ARC has not been issued yet, are
not automatically excluded — but expect the identity and purpose checks to be
noticeably stricter, and expect some services to be unavailable.

One distinction worth internalizing: **being able to open an account is not
the same as being able to use every service.** Some non-face-to-face (앱 전용)
products exclude foreigners entirely — Shinhan Bank's notices for certain
mobile-only products state that foreigners cannot enroll remotely. That does
not mean foreigners cannot use Shinhan's mobile banking; it means enrollment
rules differ product by product. The realistic path for a first account is:
open the basic account at a branch, then layer on internet banking, the check
card, and remittance settings from there.

### Before your ARC arrives

New arrivals often face a gap of several weeks between landing and receiving
the physical ARC. During that window your options narrow, but they are not
zero:

- Some banks will open a basic account against a passport plus supporting
  documents (an employment contract helps enormously) — at branch
  discretion, and very likely as a limited account.
- Some employers pay the first salary in cash or delay the first transfer
  until the account exists; if you are negotiating a start date, it is worth
  asking how the company usually handles this.
- A Korean phone number in your own name is close to a prerequisite for
  everything downstream (apps, identity verification, autopay), and getting
  one is itself easier with an ARC — which is why many people simply wait
  for the card and then do bank + phone + apps in one sweep.

If you truly need an account in week one, call branches near your workplace
and ask specifically: "passport only, ARC applied for but not issued — will
you open a basic account, and with which documents?" You will get different
answers from different branches; that is normal.

## Traditional banks and internet banks are different tracks

Korea's banking market splits into the traditional branch banks — KB
Kookmin, Shinhan, Woori, Hana, plus NH and IBK — and the app-only internet
banks (Toss Bank, KakaoBank, K bank).

For a foreigner's **first** account, the traditional banks are usually the
practical choice, for one structural reason: a branch can look at your
documents, ask questions, and exercise judgment. An app cannot. Non-face-to-
face onboarding depends entirely on automated identity verification, which is
exactly where foreign customers hit edge cases — name formats, ID types the
system does not recognize, missing Korean phone verification.

The internet banks have been opening up: Toss Bank introduced
non-face-to-face account opening for foreign residents (ARC holders with a
Korean phone number), and KakaoBank has added products for foreign
residents. But eligibility rules change frequently and differ by product, so
treat internet banks as a *second* account you add once you hold an ARC and
a Korean phone number — not as the place to solve your first-account
problem. Check the current policy in the app itself before relying on it.

What the internet banks are genuinely good at, once you qualify: fee-free
transfers, better app UX than most legacy banks, and easy sub-accounts for
budgeting. Many long-term residents settle into a pattern of one traditional
main bank (salary, remittance, anything requiring a branch) plus one
internet bank for daily spending.

## What actually happens at the branch

Knowing the sequence removes most of the stress. A typical first-account
visit looks like this:

1. **Take a number, state your purpose.** Say you want to open a deposit
   account (입출금 계좌) and mention the purpose — salary, living expenses,
   business.
2. **Hand over ID.** Passport plus ARC if you have one. The teller may
   photocopy both.
3. **Purpose questionnaire.** You will fill in (or the teller fills in with
   you) a financial-transaction-purpose form. Answer plainly and
   consistently with your documents — this form is what decides whether you
   get a normal or a limited account.
4. **Register a signature.** Foreigners can sign instead of using a Korean
   seal. Use a signature you can reproduce reliably; you will be asked for
   it again for years.
5. **Set a PIN** (4 digits) for the card and passbook.
6. **Receive the passbook (통장) and/or account number.** Many banks still
   issue a physical passbook; you can decline it if you prefer.
7. **Check card and app enrollment.** Ask for the check card and
   internet/mobile banking in the same sitting, and have the teller confirm
   your transfer limits and whether the account is limited.

Set aside an hour, go on a weekday morning if you can, and bring more
documents than you think you need — the cost of an unused document is zero,
and the cost of a missing one is a second visit.

## The real issue is not the account — it is the limited account

The single most common surprise for new arrivals is not a rejected
application. It is discovering, weeks later, that their account has a daily
cap.

When a bank cannot sufficiently verify your transaction purpose, it can open
the account as a limited account. The system exists to choke off fraud
accounts used in voice-phishing schemes, and the caps are real. Following the
Financial Services Commission's April 2024 announcement, the standard limits
from **May 2, 2024** are:

| Channel | Daily limit |
| --- | --- |
| Electronic banking (transfers) | ₩1,000,000 |
| ATM withdrawal / transfer | ₩1,000,000 |
| Teller (창구) transactions | ₩3,000,000 |

Receiving a salary into a limited account is usually fine. The problem
arrives the day you need to move real money: a housing deposit, a car
purchase, business funds, or an overseas transfer. At that point a
₩1,000,000/day cap stops the transaction cold.

Two practical rules follow:

1. **The day you open the account, ask whether it is a normal account or a
   limited account.** Do not assume.
2. **If it is limited, ask exactly which documents lift the restriction.**
   The bank has a legal duty to verify purpose, so the fix is objective
   evidence of how you use the account — an employment contract, business
   registration, a lease. "I'll wait a few months" or "I'll build up
   transaction history" is not how the system works.

owners, and people without income, plus the foreigner identity-matching

## Salary accounts: settle the purpose (and your name) on day one

If you work for a Korean company, salary is the easiest purpose to document.
Bring proof of employment to the account-opening appointment rather than
after the fact.

If you are the employer, three checks save real pain later: the account is in
the employee's own name, the account has no transaction restrictions, and the
name on the account matches the name in your HR records.

That last one deserves emphasis. Foreign names get mangled between passport
format and Korean banking systems — spacing, hyphens, and family-name order
all vary. If the payroll system's name string and the bank's registered name
diverge, transfers can fail or get flagged. If you ever plan to connect
overseas remittance, confirm at opening exactly how your romanized name is
registered.

## Keep personal and business money in separate accounts

If you are starting a business in Korea, do not run everything through one
account. Whatever the legal minimum, accounting and tax practice strongly
favors separating living expenses from business flows — sales deposits,
supplier payments, rent, taxes, and payroll mixed with groceries make your
books expensive to reconstruct and your expense claims harder to defend.

For a corporation (법인) the line is harder still: company funds must be kept
apart from the CEO's personal money. Moving corporate cash through a personal
account creates 가지급금 (provisional payment / shareholder loan) problems and
can carry tax consequences. If a company is in your plans, read our guide to
[registering a business in Korea as a foreigner](/business/registering-a-business-in-korea-as-a-foreigner/)
— and open the business account as a separate, purpose-documented account
from the start.

## "Resident" and "foreigner" are different classifications

This confuses almost everyone. Being a foreign national does **not**
automatically make you a non-resident in Korean banking.

For foreign-exchange and tax purposes, banks classify customers as residents
or non-residents under separate rules — Woori Bank's guidance explicitly
notes that nationality or permanent-residency status alone does not determine
the classification. A foreigner living and working in Korea long-term is
typically treated as a resident.

For everyday deposits and transfers the distinction rarely surfaces. It
matters a great deal for overseas remittance, investment products, and how
interest income is taxed. If you plan to move significant money in or out of
Korea, ask the bank how you are classified — do not infer it from your visa.

## If you will send money abroad, set it up when you open the account

Remittance is one of the main reasons foreigners keep a Korean account — part
of a salary going home, supporting family, or bringing business capital in.

The fee is the least of it. Check, at account opening:

- remittance limits, and whether online/mobile remittance is enabled for you
- foreign-currency account linkage and how FX preferential rates (환율 우대)
  are applied
- which purposes require supporting documents

As amounts grow, expect to explain the source of funds and the purpose.
Business payments, real-estate-related funds, and investment capital are not
processed like living-expense remittances. Running your salary and your
remittance through the same bank makes your transaction history easy to
explain — though for any single large transfer, it is still worth comparing
rates and fees rather than defaulting to your main bank.

## Do not open multiple accounts "just in case"

Comparing sign-up perks across five banks and opening accounts at all of them
is counterproductive in Korea. Opening several accounts in a short period,
with no clear purpose for each, is exactly the pattern the anti-fraud rules
target — the FSS guidance notes that applications to open multiple accounts
without a specific reason can be refused.

Start with **one** main account that handles salary, living expenses, bills,
card payments, and remittance. Add accounts later, when a concrete need
exists — a business account, a jeonse-deposit account, whatever it is. (Your
transaction history at one bank also becomes useful context if you later
apply for credit — see
[can foreigners get a loan in Korea](/loans/can-foreigners-get-a-loan-in-korea/).)

## While you are at the branch, finish the whole setup

Do not leave with just an account number. In the same visit, sort out:

- check card issuance
- internet / mobile banking enrollment
- transfer and ATM limits (and whether the account is limited)
- overseas remittance availability
- how to update your contact details from abroad

And keep your registered information current. Foreigners change phone
numbers, renew passports, and change visa status more often than locals — if
the bank's records stop matching your real identity documents, mobile
verification and larger transactions start failing at the worst possible
moments.

## Opening by proxy is harder than showing up

If you cannot visit a branch yourself, confirm in advance whether a proxy can
act for you. The FSS guidance notes that proxy account opening can require
extra documents such as proof of family relationship or a notarized power of
attorney — and a POA executed abroad adds notarization, apostille, and
translation questions on top. For an ordinary personal account, going in
person is by far the simplest path, and identity-critical steps like PIN and
signature registration may not be delegable at all.

## Choose a bank on usability, not a few basis points

For a checking account, the deposit rate is close to irrelevant. What
actually matters day to day: the quality of the English mobile app, a
foreigner help desk, remittance features, a branch near where you live or
work, check-card convenience, FX services, and how painful it is to update
your customer information.

If you plan to run a company or stay long-term, look one step ahead: can this
bank carry your future business or corporate account, FX transactions,
payroll transfers, and cards? Keeping one primary bank where your salary
lands and your history accumulates has real practical value — not because
history guarantees a loan approval (it does not), but because organized
transaction records make every later financial application easier.

## Before you go: call the bank

The single most reliable preparation is to contact the branch beforehand and
ask what *they* require for *your* situation. Internal KYC standards differ
by bank, and required documents differ by residence status and purpose even
within one bank.

Call ahead especially if any of these apply: your ARC has not been issued
yet; you are on a tourist or short-term stay; the account is for business
rather than salary; you expect large inbound transfers from abroad; or you
are about to incorporate a company.

When you ask, do not ask "can foreigners open an account?" — describe your
visa status, the ID you hold, and what the account is for, then ask which
documents they need. Note that opening an account itself is generally free
(per FSS guidance); ATM, teller, and remittance fees apply afterward.

## When you leave Korea: do not just abandon the account

Plan the account's end as deliberately as its beginning. If you are leaving
Korea for good, you generally have two clean options: close the account and
remit the balance out (be ready to document the source of funds for a large
final transfer), or keep the account open with your contact details updated
to a reachable number and address.

What you should not do is walk away and forget it. Accounts with no activity
for an extended period are classified as dormant (휴면계좌), and an account
attached to a dead phone number and an expired ARC is genuinely painful to
recover from abroad — identity re-verification from overseas is exactly the
scenario Korean banking handles worst. If you expect to return, or you have
recurring Korean payments (tax refunds, deposit returns, final salary), sort
out remote-access arrangements with the bank *before* your ARC expires and
your Korean phone number dies.

## After opening: verify before you rely on it

An account number is not the finish line. Before you depend on the account,
confirm: the limit status, mobile banking login, transfer limits, the check
card, ATM access, and remittance availability. If it is a salary account,
re-check the account-holder name and number before handing them to HR. If you
will use autopay, test that phone, rent, and insurance debits register
correctly.

And if the account will handle anything large — a car, a housing deposit,
business funds — deal with the transaction limits **before** you need them.
Discovering a ₩1,000,000 daily cap on the day the deposit is due is the
avoidable failure this entire guide exists to prevent.
