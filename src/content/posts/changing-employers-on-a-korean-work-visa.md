For a Korean employee, changing jobs ends one contract and starts another.
For a foreign employee it does that and then asks a second question: does your
status of stay permit work **at this employer, in this role**, and has anyone
authorised it yet.

The date that produces trouble is almost never the last day at the old place.
It is the first day at the new one.

<p class="hand">Two systems again: the labour one ends a job, the immigration one starts the next.</p>

## Permission is the rule; notification is the exception

Article 21 of the Immigration Act requires a foreign national with a status
permitting employment to obtain **permission** before changing or adding a
workplace within that status.

The Enforcement Decree then carves out an exception: among **E-1 to E-7**
holders, those meeting criteria announced by the Ministry of Justice may
instead **notify within
[15 days](/tracked/workplace-change-notification-deadline/) after the change**.

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">So the first question is not "am I on E-7"</p>
  <p>It is whether this E-7 sub-type and this situation fall inside the
  announced criteria. Two people both holding E-7 can be on opposite routes, and
  the routes have opposite rules about when work may begin. Building a table
  that says "E-1 to E-7 = prior permission" is wrong; so is one that says
  "E-1 to E-7 = notify within 15 days."</p>
</div>

The notification route removes the prior step, not the substance. The new
employer and the duties still have to fall within the status, and a
notification can fail to be accepted.

## F statuses are not on this map

F-2, F-4, F-5 and F-6 differ from each other in what employment they permit,
and they are not variants of the workplace-designation system that governs E
statuses.

Where a status is not tied to a particular employer — permanent residence and
marriage-based residence being the clearest cases — the question is what
employment the status allows, not whether a designated workplace may be
changed.

<div class="callout callout-note">
  <p class="callout-title">Do not extend "15 days" across the F statuses</p>
  <p>It produces a rule that does not exist, and it obscures the question that
  does: what work does your particular status actually permit? That is answered
  per status and per sub-type, not by a shared deadline.</p>
</div>

## Three fifteen-day rules that are not the same rule

The number appears in unrelated places, and conflating them is the most common
factual error in this whole subject.

| Whose duty | What it is |
|---|---|
| **The worker**, on the notification route | Notify a workplace change within 15 days **after** moving |
| **The employer** | Report to immigration within 15 days when employment ends — [the reporting deadline](/tracked/foreign-worker-employment-change-report-deadline/) |
| Neither | There is **no** general rule giving a departing foreign employee 15 days to change status |

The employer's failure to report is sanctioned by an administrative fine on the
employer. It confers nothing on you and imposes nothing on you.

## The gap period: the risk is unlawful work, not unlawful stay

If you leave a job with time left on your card, you have not automatically
become an overstayer.

But starting work at a new employer without the required permission or a valid
notification route is a separate problem, and it usually arrives first.

```
risky   old job ends → start at the new company → file with immigration
safe    old job ends → new contract → establish the route
          → permission or notification → then the start date
```

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">"Not paid yet" is not a defence</p>
  <p>The assessment is whether you actually carried out employment activity at
  the new workplace. Whether payroll had processed anything is a weaker fact
  than whether you were doing the job.</p>
</div>

## "Just come in for the training" is the trap

The most common way people get this wrong is not a deliberate early start. It
is an employer saying the permission comes through next week and asking for a
few days of onboarding in the meantime.

If the training is part of performing the job and happens under the employer's
direction, it can be assessed as employment activity — paid or not. So can
handover, on-site training, customer contact, production or research work, and
joining a live project.

<div class="steps">
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">1</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Where prior permission applies</strong>, the contract's employment start date should not precede the permission date.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">2</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Make the HR system agree with the contract.</strong> A start date in a payroll or attendance system that predates the permission is a record someone else made about you.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">3</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Push the start date rather than the paperwork.</strong> Moving a start date is an inconvenience; the alternative is an immigration finding.</div></div>
</div>

## The new employer has to qualify too

E-7 is not a permit that belongs to you and travels. Each move re-opens the
employer-side questions: the E-7 sub-type and occupation code, the actual
duties, the wage against the requirement for that sub-type, the employer's
sector and scale, the number of Koreans employed and the foreign employment
ratio.

So a lawful E-7-1 at one company does not transfer to a role that falls under a
different occupation, or to an employer that does not meet the conditions. What
those conditions are, and how they differ between the statuses, is in
[E-9 vs E-7: what actually separates them](/employment/e-9-vs-e-7-korean-work-visas-compared/).

## E-9 runs two procedures, in sequence

An E-9 worker is not filing the same form with an extra step. There is a whole
employment-permit procedure before the immigration one.

<div class="steps">
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">1</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>A statutory ground.</strong> The employer terminating or refusing to renew, suspension or closure, cancellation of the employment permit, breach of working conditions, unfair treatment, or another ground in the Act.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">2</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Apply through the employment centre</strong> within <a href="/tracked/e9-workplace-change-application-period/">one month</a> of the contract ending.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">3</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Be placed with a new workplace</strong> and obtain workplace change permission within <a href="/tracked/e9-new-workplace-permission-window/">three months</a> of applying — or leave the country.</div></div>
</div>

The three months run from the **application**, not from the day the job ended.
Applying late shortens the total rather than extending it. Occupational injury,
illness and pregnancy or childbirth can change how the periods are counted.

## Where a long search is needed

If no qualifying employer is available quickly, the question becomes whether
you can change to a status that permits job-seeking. D-10 is the usual
candidate and it is not automatic: eligibility, and the period that attaches to
your own route, both have to be established. That, and what happens to the
employment insurance claim running alongside, is in
[losing your job on a Korean work visa](/employment/losing-your-job-on-a-korean-work-visa/).

## Violations are not priced as a fee

Breaching the permission or notification requirements is an immigration
offence. Depending on the case it can produce a notification disposition, a
penalty or fine, or an investigation — and the record can weigh against later
extensions, status changes and permanent residence applications.

Actually working at an unauthorised workplace is treated more seriously than a
missed filing.

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">Which is why "we'll pay the fine" is a bad plan</p>
  <p>The cost is not the fine. It is a record that follows every subsequent
  application you make in Korea, at the point where you most want a clean
  history.</p>
</div>

## The order that works

```
old contract: termination terms and date
  → new employer: does it meet the status conditions?
  → which route: prior permission or notification?
  → evidence that the old employment ended
  → sign the new contract
  → permission, or the notification after moving
  → set the lawful start date
  → new employer files the four insurances
```

The one item that most often goes missing is the fourth — evidence that the
previous employment ended, when the previous employer is not willing to provide
it. That is the other half of today's pair.
