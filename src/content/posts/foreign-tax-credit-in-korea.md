You live in Korea and receive income from another country. That government has
already taken income tax. Then you prepare your Korean return and find the same
income may be taxable here too.

Twice?

Not necessarily. Korea gives a foreign tax credit — 외국납부세액공제 — but it
is not a refund of whatever another country collected. The income has to be
inside Korea's taxable scope, the foreign tax has to qualify, the credit is
capped by a calculation, and the claim has to be supported.

So ₩3 million paid abroad might produce a ₩3 million Korean credit. For
someone else the same ₩3 million produces ₩2 million of current-year relief.
For a third person it produces nothing, because the underlying foreign-source
income is outside Korea's scope in the first place.

The difference is not which country took the money. **It is how much of that
foreign-source income Korea taxes, and how much Korean tax is attributable to
it.**

## Why two countries can tax one payment

Countries claim taxing rights on different connections. One taxes income
because it arose in its territory; another taxes the same income because the
recipient lives there.

A Korean tax resident with rent from overseas property can be taxed by the
property's country under its domestic law and by Korea under its resident
rules. Neither has made a mistake — two jurisdictions simply overlap.

Treaties and credits both address that overlap, differently:

**A treaty determines or limits taxing rights. A foreign tax credit relieves
qualifying overlapping taxation after the fact.**

Do not use one where the other belongs.

## First: does Korea tax this income at all?

This sounds obvious and it is the step most often skipped.

Residents are generally taxed on domestic and foreign income within the scope
the Income Tax Act prescribes; non-residents on Korean-source income. But art.
3 carves out qualifying foreign residents: where cumulative Korean domicile or
residence over the preceding ten years is five years or less, foreign-source
income is taxed only where it is paid in Korea or remitted to Korea.

So a recently arrived foreign resident may hold overseas dividends that are
neither paid in nor remitted to Korea. If the exception applies and the income
is genuinely foreign-source, it can fall outside Korea's scope — and **foreign
tax on excluded income does not create a Korean credit, because there is no
Korean tax on it to relieve.**

Change the facts — the person no longer qualifies, or the payment or
remittance conditions are met — and the income comes into the Korean
calculation. Only now does the credit matter.

```
residence → source → Korean taxable scope → foreign tax → credit
```

Which is why the credit is never step one. Source is [Korean-source or
foreign-source](/business/korean-source-vs-foreign-source-income/); residence
is [resident or
not](/business/income-tax-for-foreigners-resident-or-not/).

## Credit, not deduction

Article 57 is the statutory basis for individuals: where foreign-source income
is included in a resident's comprehensive or retirement income amount and
qualifying foreign income tax has been paid or is payable, a credit may be
claimed within the statutory limit. The calculation differs by income
category; this guide is about comprehensive income tax.

A credit reduces the tax. A deduction reduces the income the tax is computed
on. If the Korean calculation produces ₩5 million and the allowable credit is
₩1 million, the tax becomes ₩4 million before other adjustments.

That is not ₩1 million received from the foreign government, and it is not a
business expense. Foreign income taxes follow their own treatment — **the same
tax does not get both deducted and credited.**

## Which foreign taxes qualify

Not everything a foreign government calls tax. Article 57 and Enforcement
Decree art. 117 identify the qualifying foreign income taxes — generally taxes
computed by reference to income, plus certain similar taxes and relevant
additional income-tax components. **Penalties are excluded.**

| Foreign payment | What to examine |
|---|---|
| Foreign income tax on qualifying income | Potentially creditable |
| Foreign dividend withholding | Potentially creditable, subject to the rules |
| Foreign income tax on rental profits | Potentially creditable |
| Foreign tax penalties | Not qualifying foreign income tax |
| Foreign VAT or sales tax | Not automatically income tax |
| Foreign property tax | Not automatically income tax |
| Social insurance contributions | Not automatically income tax |

Not an exhaustive classification — the actual tax has to be examined on its
legal nature. A foreign tax using gross receipts as its base can still fall
inside a qualifying category under the Decree; a tax arising because someone
owns property may not be equivalent to income tax at all. The foreign
authority's label is evidence, not the test.

**It must qualify under Korean law, not merely appear as a deduction on a
foreign statement.**

## The limit is the whole point

Korea does not let you subtract every won of foreign income tax from your
Korean bill. Article 57 caps it:

```
credit limit = Korean calculated tax × (qualifying foreign-source income
                                        ÷ comprehensive income amount)
```

Three components, each defined:

- **Korean calculated tax** — the comprehensive income tax computed under art.
  55, before this credit.
- **Qualifying foreign-source income** — determined under the applicable
  rules, including adjustments for attributable expenses and certain exempt or
  reduced-tax income.
- **Comprehensive income amount** — the relevant comprehensive income amount
  for the year.

🔴 Two ways to get this wrong. The denominator is the **comprehensive income
amount**, not the taxable base after personal deductions — substituting the
base produces the wrong limit. And putting **gross foreign revenue** in the
numerator, without the attributable expenses, overstates it. The NTS guidance
uses this same framework.

## The worked case: ₩3 million paid abroad

| Item | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Korean-source income amount | ₩40,000,000 |
| Qualifying foreign-source income | ₩20,000,000 |
| Comprehensive income amount | ₩60,000,000 |
| Korean calculated national tax | ₩6,000,000 |
| Qualifying foreign income tax | ₩3,000,000 |

Those income amounts are already after the income-determination rules — the
₩20 million is not gross foreign sales — and the ₩6 million is an assumed
result, not a figure derived from some universal rate.

```
₩6,000,000 × (₩20,000,000 ÷ ₩60,000,000) = ₩2,000,000
```

| | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Qualifying foreign tax | ₩3,000,000 |
| Credit limit | ₩2,000,000 |
| **Credit this year** | **₩2,000,000** |
| Excess above the limit | ₩1,000,000 |

Korean tax falls from ₩6 million to ₩4 million before other credits. Nobody
refunded ₩3 million. Korea allowed ₩2 million against its own calculated tax,
and the remaining ₩1 million goes to the carryforward rules.

## When the foreign tax is below the limit

Same Korean figures, so the limit is still ₩2 million — but only ₩1.2 million
of qualifying foreign tax was paid. The credit is the **smaller** of the two:
₩1.2 million, leaving Korean tax of ₩4.8 million.

| | Higher foreign tax | Lower foreign tax |
|---|---:|---:|
| Korean calculated tax | ₩6,000,000 | ₩6,000,000 |
| Credit limit | ₩2,000,000 | ₩2,000,000 |
| Qualifying foreign tax | ₩3,000,000 | ₩1,200,000 |
| Credit this year | ₩2,000,000 | ₩1,200,000 |
| Excess carried | ₩1,000,000 | ₩0 |

The unused ₩800,000 of limit capacity is not a refund and not a credit you can
claim from somewhere else. **The limit is a ceiling, not a promise.**

## Foreign revenue is not foreign income

A business receiving ₩100 million from overseas customers does not put ₩100
million in the numerator. Expenses directly attributable to foreign-source
income, and appropriately allocated shared costs, affect the figure — Decree
art. 117 sets the rules, and the NTS illustrates it for creators earning from
overseas platforms.

```
foreign revenue       ₩30,000,000
attributable expenses ₩12,000,000
foreign-source income ₩18,000,000   ← the numerator, not ₩30,000,000
```

Use ₩30 million where ₩18 million is correct and the numerator is overstated
by ₩12 million, which means claiming more credit than permitted.

**Shared costs.** Where ₩20 million of costs relate jointly to the activity and
the applicable method allows allocation by revenue ratio, a 30% foreign
revenue share allocates ₩6 million, leaving ₩24 million before further
adjustments. That resembles the approach in the NTS's creator guidance — it
does not mean every multinational business allocates by revenue. Direct costs,
shared costs, statutory methods and special rules can each require different
treatment.

What it does mean: a foreign payment statement showing gross receipts cannot
supply the numerator by itself. And expenses must not be assigned to Korean
income simply to enlarge the credit.

## Ten years, and what that does not guarantee

Article 57(2) carries qualifying excess foreign tax forward to tax periods
ending within **ten years** from the beginning of the following tax period,
creditable within each later year's own limit.

⚠️ Some older NTS English material still says five years, reflecting the
earlier rule. The ten-year figure is from the current statutory text, and this
site tracks it as a figure precisely because the two numbers circulate
together.

Carrying forward is not recovering. A carried credit needs **limit capacity**
in a future year:

- No foreign-source income later → no usable limit for that period.
- Plenty of foreign income but little Korean calculated tax → a small limit.
- Ceasing to be a Korean tax resident → the ability to use earlier credit
  needs its own examination. The credit does not simply follow you.

Article 57(2) also provides that an amount still uncredited when the period
expires may, subject to conditions, be included in necessary expenses for the
period containing the day after expiry. **That is a different treatment from
the credit, not an automatic cash refund** — and whether the rule applies has
to be determined.

So the carryforward record needs year of origin, amount, credit used each
year, amount remaining and expiry.

### Using it the following year

Year 1 left ₩1 million of excess. Year 2:

| Item | Year 2 |
|---|---:|
| Comprehensive income amount | ₩80,000,000 |
| Qualifying foreign-source income | ₩30,000,000 |
| Korean calculated tax | ₩8,000,000 |
| Current-year qualifying foreign tax | ₩2,200,000 |

```
limit      ₩8,000,000 × (₩30,000,000 ÷ ₩80,000,000) = ₩3,000,000
used now   ₩2,200,000
capacity   ₩3,000,000 − ₩2,200,000 = ₩800,000
```

On the simplifying assumptions that the prior excess remains eligible against
this limit and no priority rule alters it, ₩800,000 of the carried amount can
be used, leaving **₩200,000** still carried — not refunded, and still subject
to its original period. Where foreign business income arises through
establishments in several countries, separate country calculations may be
required.

## The foreign country may have taken too much

A treaty-reduced rate matters before the credit does. On a ₩10 million
dividend where the source country's domestic rate is 25% and the treaty
permits 15%:

```
withheld  ₩10,000,000 × 25% = ₩2,500,000
permitted ₩10,000,000 × 15% = ₩1,500,000
excess                        ₩1,000,000
```

Decree art. 117 generally **excludes** foreign tax above the amount calculated
under an applicable treaty's exemption, reduction or limitation provisions —
the Decree also carries a specific exception concerning certain Russian tax
under treaty-suspension measures. So the ₩1 million over-withheld is a refund
or correction claim in the source country, not a bigger Korean credit.

The 25% and 15% are hypothetical; the real rate depends on the country pair,
income type, beneficial ownership and other conditions.

**Paying more than the treaty permits does not buy more Korean credit.**

## Treaty and credit, in order

```
1  source-country tax   can that government tax the payment at all
2  treaty limitation    does a treaty reduce or remove it
3  Korean scope         is the income in Korea's calculation
4  credit               qualifying foreign tax, against Korea's limit
```

Do not assume a treaty eliminates every instance of double taxation — some
permit both countries to tax, with the residence country giving relief. And no
treaty applies identically across dividends, interest, employment income and
business profits. The article matters.

## The foreign tax can change after you file

A corrected foreign assessment, a treaty refund, an audit — any of these can
move the number after a Korean credit has been claimed on it.

Decree art. 117 addresses delayed foreign assessments and later changes. Where
the assessment notice is late or the foreign tax period differs from the
Korean filing period, it provides a special application procedure within
**three months** of receiving the relevant notice, subject to its conditions,
with similar provisions for subsequent corrections.

Receive a foreign refund after claiming the credit and the original
calculation may no longer be correct. Work out whether the Korean credit and
filing need adjusting — which is one more reason the file outlives the May
return.

## The tax years may not line up

Korea computes individual income tax on the calendar year. Other countries use
other fiscal periods, and a foreign assessment can be finalised months after
the Korean return is due.

Article 57 and Decree art. 117 address foreign tax paid *or payable* and the
relevant period, generally applying the credit to the period in which the
foreign-source income enters the Korean calculation, with special rules for
delayed assessments and mismatched periods.

So do not drop the whole credit into the year cash left for the foreign
authority. Keep both: **the Korean year the income is included in, and the
foreign tax period with its assessment or payment dates.** Related, not
identical.

## Dividends: more questions than they look

An overseas company pays through a foreign broker, which withholds before the
money arrives. Then: was the dividend foreign-source; was it in Korea's scope
for this person; was the withholding lawful under the domestic and treaty
provisions; is it comprehensively taxed in Korea or treated as financial
income another way; and was it received directly or through an investment
vehicle?

That last one matters. Article 57-2 has special provisions for foreign
corporate tax paid through qualifying collective investment vehicles and
similar arrangements — not the same thing as the ordinary direct credit under
art. 57. **Foreign tax embedded in a fund cannot simply be entered as if it
had been withheld from your personal dividend.**

For an ordinary direct dividend the position is simpler, but the broker's
displayed net figure is not enough. You need the gross dividend, the foreign
withholding, the treaty treatment and the Korean taxable amount.

## Rent needs a profit calculation

₩20 million of annual overseas rent does not go into the numerator whole.

| | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Gross overseas rent | ₩20,000,000 |
| Allowable expenses | ₩7,000,000 |
| Foreign-source rental income | ₩13,000,000 |

And if the foreign country taxed an amount computed under different expense
rules, the two bases will not match — that difference has to be reconciled.
Separate foreign income tax from local property taxes and other charges too:
tax arising because you own the property is not automatically creditable
against Korean income tax. Paying a foreign government is not the test.

## Employment across two countries

A Korean tax resident working abroad for part of the year may be taxed there
on the duties performed there, while Korea may include the foreign-source
employment income in scope. Where it is taxable here and qualifying foreign
tax was imposed, the credit becomes relevant.

But a salary covering work in both countries may need allocating. Do not treat
the whole salary as foreign-source because an overseas payroll provider paid
it, and do not treat foreign payroll withholding as eligible foreign tax on
all of it. **The credit does not correct a wrong source classification** —
that has to be fixed first.

## A US return is a separate problem

A US citizen living in Korea may have US filing obligations alongside Korean
ones, and will meet the US foreign tax credit system as well as this one.
They are not the same system: Form 1116 and the IRS guidance govern US relief
and do not decide what Korea allows, and Korea's art. 57 formula cannot be
dropped into a US federal return. The direction of relief matters. This guide
is the Korean side only.

## Korean-source income taxed abroad

Duties performed entirely in Korea, salary paid by an overseas company, and
the foreign country taxes it anyway. Automatic Korean credit?

Not necessarily. The ordinary art. 57 credit concerns qualifying foreign
income tax on **foreign-source** income included in the Korean calculation. If
the salary is Korean-source under the applicable rules, foreign tax imposed
elsewhere does not automatically become creditable under that formula — the
resolution may lie in the treaty, or in seeking relief from the jurisdiction
that imposed it.

Which is why source comes first. **The legal character of the income still
matters after the tax has been collected.**

## No credit on income Korea does not tax

Back to the five-in-ten-year exception. A qualifying foreign resident has
foreign-source income neither paid in nor remitted to Korea, outside Korea's
scope, with foreign tax paid on it. Can that tax reduce Korean tax on
unrelated Korean-source income?

Not merely because it was paid.

| Foreign income position | The credit question |
|---|---|
| Excluded under the applicable exception | No ordinary credit just because foreign tax was paid |
| Inside Korea's taxable scope | Credit may be available, subject to qualification and the limit |
| Korean-source income taxed abroad | Needs separate treaty and legal analysis |

This is what stops the credit becoming a general reimbursement for tax paid
anywhere in the world. Its job is relief from qualifying **overlapping**
income taxation.

## It is not one box on the return

The NTS identifies specific supporting statements: the **외국납부세액공제
신청서** (Foreign Tax Credit Application), the **국가별 외국납부세액공제
명세서** (country-by-country statement) and the **소득종류별 외국납부세액
명세서** (statement by income type). They exist to tie the foreign income and
foreign tax to the right country and category.

Electronic filing may require the corresponding entries in Hometax, and the
documents and submission arrangements should be confirmed for the year. **A
screenshot of a broker's tax deduction is not the claim.**

## What to keep

The file has to let the income and the tax be traced: the **foreign income
statement** (income, gross amount, period, recipient); the **withholding or
assessment** issued by the payer or authority; **evidence of payment** where
relevant; **source documentation** — contracts, employment records, leases,
investment statements — supporting the legal character and source; **expense
records** and the allocation method where the income is computed after
attributable expenses; **currency conversion records**; **treaty
documentation** where a rate or exemption is relied on; and the **prior-year
carryforward history**.

Not every document you own — the ones that establish which foreign income was
taxable in Korea, which foreign tax legally applied, and how the credit was
computed.

## The exchange rate is part of the calculation

A statement showing USD 2,000 is not ₩2 million by assumption, and you do not
pick whichever rate yields the biggest credit. The conversion method and date
follow the applicable rules — and the income-recognition date and the foreign
assessment or payment date may differ, which matters.

Keep the source currency, the original amount, the conversion date or method
and the resulting won figure. **Conversion is tax computation, not
formatting.**

## Filing it

1. **Confirm Korean taxable income** — which foreign-source income is in the
   calculation. Not starting from the withholding statement.
2. **Compute foreign-source income** under Korean rules, with attributable
   expenses.
3. **Identify qualifying foreign tax** — separating penalties, VAT, property
   taxes; checking treaty limits and special provisions.
4. **Calculate the limit** from Korean calculated tax, qualifying
   foreign-source income and the comprehensive income amount, with
   country-specific calculations where required.
5. **Prepare the application** and its statements.
6. **Enter it** in the comprehensive filing at [Hometax](https://www.hometax.go.kr/),
   under `종합소득세 신고`. Menus and form layout change.
7. **Check national and local separately.** Local relief follows local rules;
   do not assume the national credit reappears identically.
8. **Submit and keep** the confirmation and the foreign-tax evidence. If the
   foreign tax is reassessed later, revisit the Korean position.

### When Hometax shows no credit

Foreign income appearing in the comprehensive calculation does not mean the
foreign tax information went in. A missing credit can mean the income was
classified wrongly, the tax was not entered in the relevant section, the
application or supporting data is missing, the limit is zero or smaller than
expected, the tax does not qualify, the income is separately taxed, or the
settlement uses another process.

🔴 **Do not raise the foreign-source income figure to make a bigger credit
appear.** Check the income calculation and the documentation instead. The NTS
126 consultation service or a qualified professional is the next step —
especially here, where the error is often treaty interpretation or
classification rather than arithmetic.

### Through year-end settlement?

Some foreign-source employment income may be included in employment-income
year-end settlement, and Decree art. 117 provides for the application to be
submitted with the relevant return or settlement as applicable.

That does not mean handing an employer an overseas tax receipt produces a
payroll refund. The employment arrangement, residence, income inclusion and
settlement process all have to be examined, and where a foreign assessment is
finalised after the settlement the Decree provides procedures in qualifying
cases. Someone with several income sources may still need a comprehensive
return — [who has to file](/business/may-global-income-tax-filing-for-foreigners/).

## Five mistakes

**Claiming all foreign tax paid.** The statutory limit governs; excess is not
refundable in Korea.

**Using gross revenue as foreign-source income.** The numerator needs
attributable and allocated expenses taken out.

**Claiming tax on income Korea does not tax.** The credit cannot generally
offset unrelated Korean tax.

**Ignoring treaty limits.** Withholding above the treaty amount may not
qualify; the claim belongs in the source country.

**Losing the carryforward history.** Without records, establishing what
remains eligible becomes guesswork.

All five can happen while the foreign income and tax were reported perfectly
abroad. The work is a reconciliation between two legal systems — **one
country's tax statement does not establish the other's allowable credit.**

## The order

**Resident?** Establish it. **Foreign-source?** Apply the source rules, not the
payer's bank location. **Taxable in Korea?** Consider the five-in-ten-year
exception. **Does the foreign tax qualify?** Exclude what does not. **Treaty?**
Check whether the source country stayed within its limit. **The limit?**
Compute it under art. 57 and the Decree. **Excess?** Track it and its period.
**Documents?** Prepare the application and statements.

Only then is the credit a final figure.

In the worked case the taxpayer paid ₩3 million abroad, the limit was ₩2
million, the credit was ₩2 million and ₩1 million went forward. For a
qualifying foreign resident inside the five-in-ten-year rule, the first
question is still whether the foreign income is taxable in Korea at all — and
that one has to be answered before any of this arithmetic means anything.

**The foreign tax credit is not a refund of tax paid abroad. It reduces
qualifying Korean tax on income that has already met foreign income taxation.**
The distinction is what separates real double-taxation relief from an
unsupported claim.
