The most dangerous misunderstanding among foreign freelancers in Korea is
this sentence: *"They withheld 3.3%, so my income is legal."*

The 3.3% is a **tax processing method** — nothing more. It is not an
employment permit. Income can be perfectly reported under tax law and
still arise from activity your visa does not allow; conversely, activity
your visa permits can still create labor-law problems if you work like an
employee under a contract labeled "freelancer."

A foreign freelancer therefore has to pass three separate tests, and all
three must hold:

1. **Tax:** what kind of income is this?
2. **Immigration:** does my status of stay permit this activity?
3. **Substance:** is the relationship genuinely independent contracting?

This guide walks all three — plus the May filing math, the expense rules,
and the point where "freelancer" should become "registered business."

## 3.3% is a prepaid tax, not a final rate

Business income from continuously provided personal services — think
translation, design, development — is withheld at source: the NTS
prescribes **3% income tax**, and the 10% local surtax brings the
familiar **3.3%**.

On a ₩5,000,000 invoice: ₩150,000 income tax + ₩15,000 local tax =
₩165,000 withheld, ₩4,835,000 received. But ₩165,000 is not your tax
bill — it is a deposit. Your actual annual tax is computed in the
comprehensive income-tax filing: total revenue minus necessary expenses
gives business income, other income is added, deductions applied,
progressive rates run. Withheld more than the final figure? Refund.
Less? Pay the difference. The NTS states explicitly that 3.3%
personal-service earners are comprehensive-filing taxpayers.

"I paid my 3.3%, so taxes are done" is simply wrong.

## Withholding 3.3% does not convert an employee into a freelancer

The contract's title and the tax treatment cannot classify a working
relationship. What matters is substance: does the company direct and
supervise you, are your hours and workplace fixed, do you work
independently, can you serve other clients, do you bear your own
commercial risk?

Someone reporting at 9 a.m. daily, following a manager's instructions,
doing identical work for a fixed ₩3,000,000 a month is not an independent
contractor because the paper says "freelancer" and 3.3% was withheld. If
the relationship is substantively employment, Labor Standards Act issues
— wages, severance — and social-insurance coverage arise separately.

Nor is "3.3%" the opposite of "4대보험": tax income classification and
labor-law employee status are each judged on substance, independently.

## The tax office's 3.3% and immigration's permission are different worlds

Immigration Act Article 18 requires a foreigner to hold a work-eligible
status to work in Korea — and where the status designates a workplace,
working elsewhere without authorization is prohibited. Article 20 adds
that performing activities belonging to a *different* status alongside
your current one requires **advance extra-activity permission**
(체류자격 외 활동허가).

The pivotal point: **the payer's tax withholding never substitutes for
immigration permission.** A Korean company can pay an E-7 holder a side
fee and report the 3.3% flawlessly — if that side work falls outside the
approved E-7 scope and workplace relationship, the immigration question
stands untouched. Reporting tax on unauthorized work does not make it
authorized work.

## F-5 and F-6 are freest — but do not lump the F-series together

**F-5** permanent residents face no employment-scope restrictions, and
**F-6** marriage migrants are likewise unrestricted under the enforcement
decree — ordinary freelancing or running a business carries none of the
workplace tethering of E-series statuses.

**F-4** overseas Koreans have very broad work rights, but not unlimited:
restrictions exist on simple manual labor, activities against public
morals, and fields the Ministry of Justice restricts — and licensed
professions still require the license.

**F-2 deserves the most care.** "F-2 can freelance freely" is not a safe
generalization: the enforcement decree differentiates employment-scope
rules by F-2 *sub-type*. Many sub-types are unrestricted; some carry
conditions such as continuing the prior status's activity scope. Check
your precise sub-code, not the letter F.

## E-7: a legal main job does not legalize the side project

E-7 is an occupation-and-workplace status. Take a software developer on
E-7 who independently lands weekend app projects for another company at
3.3%: tax-wise, reportable business income — probably fine.
Immigration-wise, an entirely separate question of the current permission
scope, workplace additions, and extra-activity rules.

Same professional field does not mean automatically permitted, and the
word "Freelancer" on the contract changes nothing. Before starting any
outside project, confirm with the competent immigration office or the
1345 hotline whether it needs a workplace addition/change report or
extra-activity permission — and if permission is required, obtain it
**before the work starts**, not after the money arrives.

## D-2 and D-10: platform gigs are not "just a part-time job"

For **D-2** students, study is the purpose of stay; permitted part-time
work runs on its own permits, hour caps, and sector rules. Recurring paid
design, translation, or development gigs through online platforms are
not equivalent to an approved part-time job — and "I worked online from
home, so it isn't employment" is not a safe argument, because sustained
paid services performed in Korea are judged on the actual activity.

**D-10** is a job-seeking status — search, defined internships, and
startup preparation, per Ministry of Justice guidance. Reading it as "not
employed, therefore free to earn as a freelancer" is exactly backwards:
recurring paid services beyond permitted search-and-preparation activity
need advance confirmation.

## May is not "refund season" — it is when your real P&L gets settled

The annual filing determines what you actually owe:

> **Business income = total revenue − necessary expenses**

Small earners without books may fall under the **simplified expense
rate** (단순경비율) or **standard expense rate** (기준경비율) regimes,
divided by industry code and prior-year revenue — some service groups
use a prior-year threshold around ₩36,000,000 for simplified-rate
eligibility, but the applicable line depends on your industry code and
whether you are newly registered, so check yours specifically. Under the
simplified rate, expenses are recognized as a fixed percentage of
revenue; under the standard rate, your real evidence for rent, labor,
and purchases suddenly matters a great deal — and at larger scale,
double-entry bookkeeping obligations enter. Growing revenue while still
tossing receipts like a first-year gig worker is how freelancers
overpay.

Two worked outcomes kill the "refund season" myth:

- Revenue ₩30,000,000, withheld ₩990,000; final tax after expenses and
  deductions ₩400,000 → **refund ≈ ₩590,000**.
- Same withholding, but other income and thin expenses push final tax to
  ₩2,000,000 → **additional payment ≈ ₩1,010,000**.

A refund only ever means you prepaid more than you owed.

## Expenses require business relevance — not creative relabeling

Real business costs are deductible: a developer's software, equipment,
and workspace; a consultant's travel and professional services. Personal
living costs, family trips, and personal car use do not become business
expenses because you freelance. Relevance plus documentation is the
whole test — and foreigners, who often mix foreign services and overseas
accounts, should be especially disciplined about keeping card
statements, invoices, and contracts that explain each cost's character.

## There is no won threshold that triggers business registration

Another durable myth: "up to ₩24M you can stay a freelancer; past ₩33M
you must register." **No such statutory amounts exist.** The Income Tax
Act's rule is that a person who commences a business shall register —
and the substantive trigger is **continuity, repetition, and business
character**, not a revenue line. A one-off engagement differs from
independently serving multiple clients, continuously, for profit. If you
invoice several clients monthly, market yourself, and have run the
operation for years, "I'm not a business because they withhold 3.3%"
does not hold.

Nor is every freelance service VAT-exempt. The NTS frames the 3.3%
withholding around VAT-exempt personal services — but form, service
nature, and whether you operate with facilities and staff can place an
activity in VAT-taxable territory, dragging registration and VAT filing
with it. An organized operation billing like a solo freelancer is a
structure worth re-examining: **tax follows the actual shape of the
business, not the payment method.**

## Reported income is what banks can read

A 3.3% freelancer is not unbankable: withholding receipts, payment
statements, and the post-filing income certificate (소득금액증명) all
prove income. Lenders will scrutinize the volatility — business tenure,
declared income, and actual account inflows — more than they would a
salary, as covered in
[the loans pillar](/loans/can-foreigners-get-a-loan-in-korea/).

Two structural payoffs of formalizing as the work stabilizes: a
[registered business with a dedicated account](/business/registering-a-business-in-korea-as-a-foreigner/)
turns your revenue into third-party-verifiable records — tax invoices,
business-account inflows, VAT and income filings — which also happen to
be exactly the evidence that
[lifts limited-account caps](/banking/how-to-open-a-bank-account-in-korea-as-a-foreigner/).
And a warning that cuts the other way: earning ₩80M while aggressively
minimizing declared income optimizes this year's tax at the cost of
every future loan review. **Tax minimization and financial leverage do
not move in the same direction.**

## For long-term status plans, legality and reported-ness must both hold

For anyone eyeing long-term residency (F-2 points systems and beyond),
*how* the income arose matters as much as how much. Faithfully reporting
3.3% income from activity immigration never permitted does not cure the
immigration defect; conducting permitted freelance work but skipping tax
filings destroys your ability to prove the income later. The chain that
long-term settlers need unbroken from day one:

> permitted activity → real contract → normal account deposits →
> withholding or business tax filing → comprehensive filing → income
> certificate

The freelancer → sole proprietor → corporation progression, likewise,
has no magic revenue trigger: it is signaled by repeating transactions,
growing expenses and subcontractors, clients demanding tax invoices, and
the need for business financing. And a foreigner adds one test above all
of those: **does my current status of stay actually permit this
business?**

3.3% is a tax number. It is not a work permit and not a business
license. The first thing to verify before freelancing in Korea is not
the rate — it is whether you are legally allowed to do the work at all.
