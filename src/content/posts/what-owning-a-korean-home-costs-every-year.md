The costs of buying a home in Korea split cleanly into two groups, and almost
everyone budgets for the wrong one.

The transaction costs — acquisition tax, agent, scrivener, the bond — are large,
visible, and paid once. They are also
[already covered in detail](/cost-of-living/closing-costs-when-buying-property-in-korea/).

This is the other group: the costs that arrive every year for as long as you own
the property. Over a ten-year hold they are the larger number, and they are the
ones a rent-versus-mortgage comparison leaves out entirely.

## 1. Acquisition tax: establish which schedule applies before quoting a rate

"Acquisition tax in Korea is 1.1% to 12%" is a sentence that appears everywhere
and describes nothing. It runs two entirely separate structures together.

**The ordinary schedule**, for buying a home for consideration:

| Acquisition price | Base rate |
|---|---|
| Up to ₩600,000,000 | 1% |
| ₩600,000,000 – ₩900,000,000 | 1% to 3%, by statutory formula |
| Above ₩900,000,000 | 3% |

Local education tax sits on top, and rural special tax where the conditions
apply, so the effective burden typically lands somewhere around 1.1% to 3.5%.
[Tracked here](/tracked/acquisition-tax-housing/).

**The heavy rates** are something else. Article 13-2 of the Local Tax Act applies
[8% or 12%](/tracked/housing-multiple-owner-acquisition-tax-rate/) to certain
acquisitions of additional homes in adjusted areas, high-count acquisitions
outside them, and acquisitions by companies.

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">12% is not a rate a first-home buyer can land on</p>
  <p>Before any acquisition figure means anything, three things have to be
  settled: how many homes will be held after the purchase, whether the property is
  in an adjusted area, and whether the buyer is an individual or a company. A
  break-even model that puts 12% against a single-home purchase is wrong by a
  factor of four, and one that assumes 1% for a second home in an adjusted area is
  wrong in the more expensive direction.</p>
</div>

## 2. Property tax arrives every year regardless

Property tax is a local tax on the ownership itself. It does not depend on any
national threshold and it does not go away.

It is the floor of the annual cost, and for most owners it is the whole of the
tax line — because the second tax below only starts above a threshold most homes
do not reach.

## 3. The holding tax has an allowance, and it is two numbers

The comprehensive real estate holding tax works on the **aggregate published
price** of all housing held.

From that, a deduction of
[₩900,000,000 — or ₩1,200,000,000 for a single-home household](/tracked/comprehensive-property-tax-deduction/)
is applied. What remains is multiplied by a fair market value ratio set by decree
to reach the tax base.

For an individual holding two homes or fewer, the rate on the housing portion is
[progressive from 0.5% to 2.7%](/tracked/comprehensive-property-tax-rate-individual/)
by tax base.

<div class="callout callout-note">
  <p class="callout-title">Published price is not market price</p>
  <p>The published price used for the assessment is generally below what the
  property would sell for, and the fair market value ratio reduces the base again
  before any rate is applied. Estimating this tax from an asking price therefore
  overstates it substantially — often enough to change the conclusion of a
  buy-versus-rent model in the wrong direction. Use the published price, and treat
  the rate band as a band rather than reaching for its top.</p>
</div>

## 4. Repairs stop being someone else's problem

A tenant's management bill and an owner's are not the same document, even in the
same apartment.

The long-term repair reserve funds replacement and repair of the building's major
systems, and the **owner** bears it. A tenant who paid it through the management
bill can reclaim it from the owner when the lease ends.

Once you own the property, there is nobody to reclaim it from. It is simply a
cost.

The same shift applies to everything inside the unit. Boiler, appliances,
flooring, sanitaryware, the interior itself — a tenant raises these with the
landlord, and an owner replaces them on their own schedule with their own money.

So a projection built from your own past management bills is missing two things:
the reserve you used to get back, and the replacements you used to ask someone
else to make.

## 5. Health insurance: a real factor, and not an automatic one

Property can enter the premium assessment for a **locally insured** person, so
buying a home can affect what a locally insured household pays.

But the sentence that gets written — "buying a home means losing dependant
status" — is not right, and putting it into a cost model as a fixed line is
worse than leaving it out.

Dependant status turns on its own income and property tests, and whether a
purchase changes your position depends on your insurance category and on your own
figures against those tests. It is a question to answer about yourself, not a
consequence to assume. The tests are set out in
[NHIS dependants: the income and property tests](/insurance/nhis-dependants-the-income-and-property-tests/).

## 6. Interest is a cost. Principal is not.

Mortgage interest belongs in the annual cost line, in full.

Principal repayment does not. Paying down a loan converts cash into equity — it
reduces a liability rather than consuming money, and it comes back to you when
the property is sold. Counting it as a housing cost is the single most common
error in these comparisons, and it makes owning look more expensive than it is.

The opposite error is more common still, and it is on the other side of the
ledger: forgetting that the equity itself has a cost.

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">The invisible annual cost</p>
  <p>₩400,000,000 of your own money in a home is ₩400,000,000 not earning
  anything anywhere else. At a 4% alternative return that is ₩16,000,000 a year of
  forgone income — larger than the property tax, the repairs and the reserve
  combined. It appears on no statement, which is exactly why it gets left out.
  Charging it is what makes a comparison with renting honest, and it is the
  subject of the afternoon piece.</p>
</div>

## What an annual cost line actually contains

| Line | Nature | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Property tax | Annual, unavoidable | Applies regardless of the holding tax threshold |
| Comprehensive holding tax | Annual, above the allowance | ₩900M, or ₩1.2B for a single-home household |
| Long-term repair reserve | Annual | Yours now — no longer reclaimable |
| Owner-side repairs | Irregular | Boiler, appliances, flooring, interior |
| Mortgage interest | Annual | A genuine cost |
| Mortgage principal | Not a cost | Becomes equity; recovered on sale |
| Equity opportunity cost | Annual, invisible | Usually the largest line of all |
| Health insurance effect | Case by case | Verify against your own position |

Note the shape of it. Of the eight rows, one is paid once, one is not a cost at
all, and the largest never appears on a statement.

Which is why the question "is buying cheaper than renting" cannot be answered by
comparing a rent figure with a mortgage payment. It needs the same capital priced
the same way on both sides, over a stated number of years.
