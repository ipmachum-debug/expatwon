Ask what the rules are for receiving money in Korea and you will be told, with
confidence, that anything over USD 10,000 is automatically reported and that
USD 50,000 a year requires a declaration.

Neither is the general rule. The real threshold is a different number that does
something different, and it applies to your bank rather than to you.

## The threshold that actually exists

For receipts that do not otherwise require a declaration, ordinary incoming
money is broadly permitted. Where the amount exceeds
[USD 100,000 on the same day for the same person](/tracked/inbound-remittance-bank-confirmation-threshold/),
the foreign exchange bank obtains **written confirmation of the reason for the
receipt**.

Note what that is and is not:

| | |
|---|---|
| It is | An obligation on the bank |
| It is not | A declaration you file |
| It is not | Automatic reporting of everything above USD 10,000 |

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">Two USD 100,000s that mean different things</p>
  <p>Wise's Korean route caps person-to-person receipts for confirmed residents
  at <a href="/tracked/wise-krw-resident-receiving-limit/">USD 100,000 a year</a>.
  That is a provider limit on one settlement path, measured annually. The
  regulatory figure is a same-day, same-person point at which a bank takes written
  confirmation. Same number, different rule, different body imposing it — and
  merging them is how a service's terms get repeated as national law.</p>
</div>

## Capital transactions are assessed first

The thresholds above concern ordinary receipts. Where the money relates to an
**overseas investment, a loan, acquisition of securities, or a real estate
transaction**, the declaration rules for that transaction apply — and they are
not waived by the amount being small.

```
what is the underlying transaction?
  → a capital transaction → its own declaration rules, first
  → an ordinary receipt   → the confirmation threshold above
```

Getting this backwards — checking the amount before checking the nature of the
transaction — is the error that produces confident wrong answers about large
transfers.

## Permitted is not the same as unquestioned

Even where nothing needs declaring, banks have customer due diligence and
anti-money-laundering duties, and they will use them.

Questions become more likely where:

- the amount is out of line with your usual activity
- the relationship between sender and recipient is unclear
- a third party sends repeatedly
- the stated purpose does not fit your occupation or income
- large sums arrive and move straight out again

<div class="callout callout-note">
  <p class="callout-title">Documents requested is not a declaration triggered</p>
  <p>These are separate systems. A bank asking for evidence of source of funds is
  doing routine compliance, not notifying you that a foreign exchange filing has
  arisen. Answering it well is straightforward; treating it as an accusation is
  not.</p>
</div>

## Write the purpose down in one sentence, before sending

What a bank assesses is the economic substance, not the label. So be able to say
what the money is:

- personal savings transferred from my own account abroad
- living expenses from my parents
- tuition payment
- relocation allowance from my employer
- funds for a rental deposit

And make the transfer message, the supporting documents and that sentence agree.

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">"Living expenses" on everything is not a safe default</p>
  <p>It works on small regular sums and works against you on a large one, because
  the description then has to carry weight it was never designed for. A deposit
  described as living costs invites exactly the question you were trying to
  avoid.</p>
</div>

## Your own money is not a gift, and not income

Moving USD 50,000 from your own account abroad to your own account in Korea
transfers property to nobody, so it is not a gift. Nor is it income: the tax
question attaches to **how the money was earned**, not to it crossing a border.

What you should expect is to evidence that it is yours — overseas account
statements, salary records, sale documents, tax filings. Assemble those before
sending.

```
inbound transfer ≠ income
inbound transfer ≠ gift
what was the origin of the money?  ← the actual question
```

## Parental support: two different rules

This is where most confusion sits, and the two provisions do different jobs.

**Living and education expenses for a dependant**, within what is socially
accepted as reasonable, are treated as exempt from gift tax on their own terms.
Tuition, dormitory costs and ordinary living costs paid by a parent for a child
studying in Korea are that.

**A gift from a lineal ascendant** carries a deduction of
[₩50,000,000 cumulative over ten years](/tracked/gift-tax-lineal-ascendant-deduction/)
for an adult recipient who is a Korean tax resident, and ₩20,000,000 for a
minor.

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">They are not the same shelter, in either direction</p>
  <p>Ordinary parental support does not need to be squeezed inside the ₩50m
  deduction — it has its own exemption. And money that goes into the recipient's
  own assets is not turned into living expenses by being called that. A large sum
  sent as "living costs" and used for a rental deposit, a share portfolio or a
  property is being assessed on what it did, not on what the transfer said.</p>
</div>

## Before a large arrival, talk to the receiving bank

For a rental deposit, relocation money or a significant asset transfer, go to
the receiving bank's foreign exchange desk **before** the money is sent.

What may be wanted depends on the nature of the transfer:

<div class="steps">
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">1</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Own funds</strong> — overseas account statements, employment and salary records, sale contracts, tax filings.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">2</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Parental support</strong> — proof of the family relationship, enrolment records, tuition invoices.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">3</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Employer money</strong> — the payment statement or a letter from the company.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">4</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Anything connected to a capital transaction</strong> — check the declaration position for that transaction first.</div></div>
</div>

Asking in advance costs a phone call. Money held at the receiving bank, or
returned abroad, costs a fortnight and sometimes a deposit deadline.

## The five questions, in order

```
who is sending
  → to whom
  → why
  → under what legal relationship
  → in which currency
```

Settle those and the rest follows: the route, the fee and spread optimisation,
the documents the bank will want, and the tax position. The route and cost side
is in
[sending money into Korea: routes and real cost](/banking/sending-money-into-korea-routes-and-real-cost/).

The question worth asking is not how much you can receive without reporting. It
is whether you can explain, in one sentence and with documents behind it, what
the money is — because that is the question you will actually be asked.
