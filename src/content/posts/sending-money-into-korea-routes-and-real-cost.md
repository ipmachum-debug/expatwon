Twenty thousand dollars sent from abroad does not arrive as one number. It
arrives as several, depending on which pipe it came down, who absorbed the
costs along the way, and whether it was converted on arrival or is still
sitting in dollars.

## Two mechanisms behind one word

A traditional transfer travels from the sending bank to the Korean receiving
bank over the international banking network, and depending on the route an
**intermediary bank** may sit in between.

Providers such as Wise and Remitly work differently: they take your money
abroad and pay the recipient through a Korean partner or payout network,
usually in won. At the Korean end it can look less like an inbound
international transfer and more like a domestic one.

Which matters for more than cost — it decides whether you can receive foreign
currency at all.

## SWIFT still earns its place on large sums

Where the amount is significant and the source of funds needs to be evident —
a rental deposit, relocation money, moving your own savings, large tuition, a
property purchase — SWIFT gives you a clean record: the sending bank's
confirmation, the reference, sender, recipient and stated purpose.

The cost is not one item:

```
sending bank fee
+ intermediary bank deductions
+ receiving bank fee
+ the conversion, if it lands in won
```

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">Do not memorise a formula for this either</p>
  <p>"Intermediary fees are USD 10–30 and the Korean bank takes ₩10,000" is the
  kind of tidy figure that circulates and does not hold. It varies by sending
  bank, currency, route, the charge option chosen and the receiving bank. Ask the
  sending bank what it can actually guarantee.</p>
</div>

## OUR, SHA, BEN

Cross-border transfers carry a charge option, and it changes what arrives.

| | Who bears the costs |
|---|---|
| **OUR** | The sender, in principle, including charges along the way |
| **SHA** | Each side bears its own |
| **BEN** | Deducted from the amount received |

How completely any of them controls an intermediary's deduction depends on the
banks and the route. So sending exactly USD 30,000 does not guarantee exactly
USD 30,000 arriving, even on OUR.

If the arriving figure has to be exact — a deposit due to the won — say so when
you send, and ask what the sending bank will commit to.

## Fintech routes have their own limits

These are provider limits on a particular settlement path, not Korean law.

On Wise's Korean route, person-to-person receipts are capped at
[USD 100,000 a year](/tracked/wise-krw-resident-receiving-limit/) for recipients
confirmed as residents, and at
[USD 5,000 per transfer and USD 50,000 a year](/tracked/wise-krw-nonresident-receiving-limit/)
for those who are not.

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">The non-resident limit catches new arrivals</p>
  <p>Before residency is established you may sit in the lower band — which is
  exactly the period when money is arriving from home to set up a life. Check the
  route before the transfer is sent rather than after it is rejected.</p>
</div>

## What you give up on a won-only route

Fintech routes often avoid intermediary deductions and show you the rate before
sending, which is why they suit regular living costs.

But if the service pays out in won:

```
send USD → hold USD in Korea → convert when you choose
                    ── not available ──
```

For anyone managing exchange rate risk on a large sum, or who needs a clear
foreign-currency paper trail, receiving into a foreign currency account by
SWIFT is the route that supports it.

## The foreign currency account is a timing tool

Money arriving into a won account is converted on arrival, at the bank's
**telegraphic transfer buying rate**. The gap between the base rate and the
applied rate is the cost.

Received into a foreign currency account, the dollars stay dollars until you
convert them.

<div class="callout callout-note">
  <p class="callout-title">Which is worth real money when a payment is a month away</p>
  <p>With a rental deposit due in four weeks you can convert the whole amount
  now, or convert in four tranches and average the rate. Neither is guaranteed to
  win. The point is that the won-only route makes the decision for you, on the
  day the money happens to land.</p>
</div>

## Preferential rates are a discount on the spread

When a Korean bank offers 50%, 80% or 90% preferential treatment, it is
discounting **the margin it adds to the base rate** by that proportion — not
selling you currency below market.

The headline number sounds larger than the effect. It is still the right thing
to negotiate:

```
saving the ₩10,000 receiving fee     small
reducing the spread on USD 100,000   large
```

Most people argue about the first and accept the second without asking.

## Side by side

| | SWIFT | Local settlement |
|---|---|---|
| Suits | Large sums, source-of-funds clarity | Living costs, routine transfers |
| Receiving currency | Foreign currency or won | Usually won |
| Intermediary banks | Possible | Avoided on a local payout |
| Cost structure | Send, intermediary, receive, convert | Service fee and spread |
| Speed | Days, depending on the chain | Same day to a few business days |
| Paper trail | Very clear | Provider records |
| Hold foreign currency | Yes, with an FX account | Not on a won payout |
| Limits | By bank and transaction type | Provider limits by route |

Neither is always cheaper. Compare the amount that lands, in the currency you
wanted.

## Most failures are typing, not regulation

Transfers held or returned at the Korean end usually fail on details:

Wise's Korean route requires the recipient's English name and date of birth to
match the Korean account, with verification completed within a stated window.

<div class="callout callout-note">
  <p class="callout-title">Check the exact English name on the account first</p>
  <p>Not what is on your passport, not what you usually write — what the Korean
  bank has recorded, including the order of the names. Sending on an assumption
  is how a transfer spends a fortnight being returned.</p>
</div>
