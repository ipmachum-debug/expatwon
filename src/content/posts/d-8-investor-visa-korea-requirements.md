The most dangerous simplification about the D-8 visa is the sentence
"put ₩100 million into a Korean corporation and you get an investor
visa." The ₩100M matters — it is the flagship foreign-direct-investment
threshold in the typical D-8-1 structure — but visa approval is not
decided by an account balance.

What has to hold together, as one continuous story: a lawful investment
notification under the Foreign Investment Promotion Act, foreign funds
that departed from the right person, a notified investor who matches the
actual shareholder, shares genuinely acquired in the Korean company, and
a company that actually does business. In other words, what D-8 practice
really turns on is not the ₩100M — it is **the legal path the money
traveled.** This is the FDI deep-dive behind
[our business registration pillar](/business/registering-a-business-in-korea-as-a-foreigner/).

## D-8-1: ₩100M and 10% are the starting point

Under the Enforcement Decree in force in 2026, the representative form
of direct investment in a Korean company requires an investment of
**₩100,000,000 or more**, with the foreign investor holding **10% or
more of voting shares or equity** (structures pairing a qualifying stake
with dispatching or appointing a managing officer are separately
provided for).

The canonical D-8-1 sequence — the same order Invest KOREA publishes for
new-corporation FDI:

> **FDI notification → overseas remittance of the investment →
> incorporation and share acquisition → business registration →
> corporate account → foreign-invested-company registration → D-8
> application**

And a boundary to draw immediately: ₩100M is **not** a common minimum
capital for everything labeled D-8. Technology startups run on an
entirely different track.

## D-8-4 is a startup-founder status, not a "₩100M visa"

Explaining D-8-4 with D-8-1 logic misleads founders in both directions.

The established D-8-4 technology-startup track runs on founder
qualifications and program criteria — degree requirements (domestic
associate degree or higher, or an overseas bachelor's or higher, among
others), intellectual-property and technology factors, and the **OASIS**
startup-immigration point system, with 80+ points plus mandatory items
as the representative route. Separate lanes exist for top performers in
the K-Startup Grand Challenge and beneficiaries of certain government
startup programs.

On top of that, the **D-8-4(S) Startup Korea Special Visa** now operates
as its own channel: a private evaluation committee assesses the
business's innovation and viability, the Ministry of SMEs and Startups
recommends, and the Ministry of Justice runs the final stay review.

So telling a tech founder "wire ₩100M first" is simply wrong advice.
**D-8-1 is about FDI capital and equity structure; D-8-4 is about
founder qualifications and the venture's innovativeness.** Different
gates, different evidence.

## Build the notification structure before any money moves

In the standard new-company D-8-1 structure, the foreign-investment
notification comes first — filed through KOTRA's foreign-investment
support function or a foreign-exchange bank. For new-share acquisitions,
Invest KOREA instructs advance notification, with the notification form
and the investor's nationality documents (for an individual, the
passport) as the base file.

A practical optimization at this step: **decide the receiving bank
now.** When the notification bank, the remittance-receiving bank, and
the company's future main bank are the same institution, the deposit
confirmations, foreign-currency purchase certificates, and
foreign-invested-company registration records all connect inside one
relationship. Choosing the FX bank is not a wire-fee decision — it is
the start of the corporate account, FX dealings, trade settlements, and
eventual lending relationship.

## Who sends the money matters as much as how much

The cleanest D-8-1 fund flow is the **notified investor's own overseas
account** remitting directly to the designated receiving account in
Korea. The bank records sender, recipient, purpose, and amount; after
the transfer you secure the foreign-currency purchase certificate and
deposit confirmations, and where the overseas leg needs reinforcing, the
SWIFT MT103 record documents who sent what, when, from which bank.

(MT103 is not a statutory requirement of every D-8 file — it is strong
financial evidence when questions arise about the sending party or
route.)

The inverse cases are where files go sideways. Notified investor A,
funds arriving from friend B, a relative, or an unrelated company:
not categorically fatal, but now the *beneficial ownership* of the funds
needs separate proof — and immigration reviews weigh the substantive
identity of investor and money over the bare fact of a deposit. Cash or
crypto converted through several hops into won before landing in a
Korean account creates the same defect: even legitimately earned money
becomes hard to trace as a continuous foreign investment.

## The capital can arrive before the company exists

A company that has not been registered has no ordinary corporate
account. So in formation-stage practice, the investment is remitted
through the FX bank, share-payment procedures run, and then the
incorporation is registered — Invest KOREA's official process order is
remittance → incorporation → business registration → corporate account.

The bank-side mechanics (the share-payment account and confirmation
documents) vary by establishment method and bank — which is why calling
it all an "escrow account" is imprecise; understand it as Korean
corporate-law share-payment procedure combined with the FX bank's
investment-receipt function. After registration, business registration
follows, with the articles, lease, registry extract, shareholder list,
FX purchase certificate, representative's passport, and FDI notification
papers as the standard set.

## The 60-day registration closes the FDI loop

Incorporation does not end the FDI procedure. Once the investment is
paid in and establishment completed, **foreign-invested-company
registration** follows through the same KOTRA office or FX bank that
took the original notification — in principle within **60 days** of the
triggering event under the current decree, matching Invest KOREA's
guidance. The usual file: the registration application, corporate
registry documents, FX purchase certificate, shareholder list, and
business registration certificate.

This registration is what administratively converts "money arrived from
abroad" into completed foreign direct investment under the Act.

## The ₩100M is not meant to sit in the account

The second-most-common D-8 myth: that the capital must remain untouched
until the visa issues. A company exists to spend on its business —
office deposit, fit-out, equipment, inventory, payroll. What matters is
**explainability**, not preservation.

A healthy worked example — ₩100M deployed as:

| Use | Amount |
| --- | --- |
| Office lease deposit | ₩30,000,000 |
| Equipment and fixtures | ₩15,000,000 |
| Initial inventory | ₩20,000,000 |
| Interior fit-out | ₩10,000,000 |
| Payroll and operating costs | ₩15,000,000 |

— each item matched by contracts, tax invoices, transfer records,
payroll ledgers, and withholding filings, all consistent with the
company's stated business. That is normal capital deployment.

The pattern that draws the "was this a real investment?" question:
capital lands, then promptly moves to the CEO's personal account, with
no business evidence behind it. This is the sharpest version of the
[corporate/personal separation rule](/business/registering-a-business-in-korea-as-a-foreigner/)
— and the most dangerous flow of all is **in-and-out round-tripping**:
₩100M borrowed, paid in, used to obtain registration or the visa, then
returned to its provider. Immigration can and does probe operating
substance — premises, capital usage, transactions, tax records — and a
shared-office address with no staff, no counterparties, no revenue, no
business costs, and repatriated capital leaves nothing to defend. D-8 is
a status for people **actually investing in and managing** a Korean
business, not for people who briefly held ₩100M.

## Four evidence groups that must agree

Exact requirements vary by office and business, but D-8-1 files resolve
into four groups that must tell one story:

1. **Identity and equity:** passport, FDI notification, corporate
   registry, shareholder list, foreign-invested-company registration —
   all naming the same investor.
2. **Funds:** overseas withdrawal records, SWIFT documentation, domestic
   FX deposit records, FX purchase certificate — one continuous path.
3. **Premises:** a real office lease; photos and facility evidence where
   useful.
4. **Operating substance:** business plan, contracts, purchase/sales
   records, tax invoices, payroll and tax filings.

A new company without revenue is not anomalous — but then the other
substance evidence has to carry the weight: what the business is, and
that the capital is actually flowing into it.

## No universal revenue-and-hiring bar at first renewal

"To extend after year one you must show revenue and hire N Koreans" is
not a statutory common minimum for all D-8-1 extensions. What extension
review substantively examines: the investment maintained, the company
genuinely operating, taxes filed, premises existing, and the applicant
actually managing. By industry and stage, revenue, purchases, hiring,
and corporate/VAT filings become the natural evidence — but an
early-stage R&D company, a restaurant, and a trading house cannot be
measured by one mechanical revenue or headcount bar.

D-8-4 runs its own logic again — innovation and program criteria — and
its point-exemption categories have been expanding in 2026, so
judgments based on older guidance materials are unreliable. Check the
current criteria at application time.

## The corporate account opens; free movement of money does not

After establishment, the corporate account opens at the FX bank with the
registry documents, business registration, corporate seal papers,
representative's passport, and shareholder/beneficial-owner
verification. But account opening and unrestricted high-value
transactions are different things: AML/KYC and transaction-purpose
verification run separately, and a zero-revenue foreign-invested company
moving hundreds of millions will be asked for purpose evidence. This is
where D-8 formation meets the
[limited-account unlock playbook](/banking/korean-limited-accounts-hando-gyejwa-explained/)
directly — a foreign-invested-company registration certificate does not
waive the bank's internal controls.

Nor is the paid-in capital a lending track record: a new ₩100M-capital
company has no automatic claim to a ₩300M business loan. Banks lend
against repayment sources — early on, the CEO's credit, collateral,
contracts, settlement inflows, and guarantee institutions; later,
financial statements and operating cash flow, as covered in
[the business-lending sequence](/loans/can-foreigners-get-a-loan-in-korea/).
(And note the CEO-pay dimension: an unpaid representative of a D-8
company sits in the regional health-insurance pool at the
[average-premium floor](/insurance/nhis-korean-health-insurance-for-foreigners/) —
salary design is part of the same structure.)

Two companies that both started with ₩100M — one that mixed capital into
personal spending, one that converted it into business assets and
normal revenue — are, eighteen months later, entirely different
borrowers in a bank's eyes.

## What D-8 practice ultimately rewards is continuity

A normal investment writes one unbroken evidence chain:

> notified investor → investor's overseas account → Korean FX bank →
> share payment → registry and shareholder list →
> foreign-invested-company registration → corporate account → business
> expenditures → tax filings and revenue

Break the chain anywhere — unexplained third-party money entering,
capital exiting to personal accounts, a real shareholder who differs
from the notified investor — and the cost of explanation escalates
sharply.

The core competence of D-8 is not producing ₩100,000,000. It is being
able to prove, years later, **whose money it was, through which legal
procedure it became the capital of a Korean company, and what business
it was spent on.**
