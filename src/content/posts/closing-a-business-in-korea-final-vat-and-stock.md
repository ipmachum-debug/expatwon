Closing a business in Korea is not one filing. It is a sequence, and the part
people treat as the whole of it — the closure report at Hometax — is the
smallest piece.

The order that holds is: stop trading, close the registration, file the final
VAT return, price what is left, close the employees and the insurance schemes,
and then settle income tax or corporate tax. This guide covers the first four.
The rest is in the afternoon piece.

## 1. The closure date is the day you actually stopped

The tax authority treats the closure date as the day the business is actually
discontinued at that place of business. Where that day is unclear, the date the
closure report is received can stand in for it.

This matters because the VAT taxable period is cut off at that date. A business
that stops trading on 12 September 2026 has a final taxable period running from
the start of the period to 12 September — not to the end of December.

So the two convenient manoeuvres both cause problems. Setting the closure date a
month out to leave room for tidying leaves a month of tax invoices and input VAT
sitting in a period that is meant to be over. Filing the closure early while
trading continues does the reverse: real supplies made by a business that on
paper no longer exists.

## 2. Hometax is not the licensing authority

The business registration can be closed through Hometax or at the tax office.
That is the tax side.

If the business needed a separate permit, registration or notification to open —
a restaurant, a hair or beauty salon, medical devices, and a long list besides —
those have to be ended with the authority that issued them, generally the local
government.

The National Tax Service now operates an **integrated closure filing** for many
licensed sectors: the business registration closure and the licence closure can
be lodged together at either the tax office or the city, county or district
office. That is a convenience about where you file, not a rule that one filing
ends everything.

## 3. The deadline that matters is the 25th of the following month

A general taxpayer files and pays the **final VAT return by the 25th of the month
following the month of closure**. Closing on 12 September 2026 means filing by
25 October 2026 for everything up to 12 September.

The legal basis is **article 49** of the Value-Added Tax Act. It is worth naming,
because closure checklists routinely cite article 63 — that is the simplified
taxpayer regime, and a general taxpayer reading it is looking at the wrong rule.
Simplified taxpayers file under their own provision.

What goes into the return is not just the last month's sales. Everything up to
the closure date: sales, purchases, tax invoices, card sales, imports and
exports, fixed assets, and the stock still sitting there.

## 4. Closing the registration does not write the return

This is the single most expensive misunderstanding in the sequence. Completing
the closure report at Hometax does not produce, prepare or file the VAT return.
They are separate obligations.

The failure mode is passive: the business closes, the owner waits for a notice
from the tax office, and the notice that eventually arrives is an assessment
rather than an invitation. The ordinary non-filing penalty is **20% of the tax
that should have been reported**, and a late-payment penalty accrues separately
on the unpaid amount for as long as it is unpaid.

The habit that prevents it is small: the moment the closure date is decided,
write the VAT deadline in the calendar in the same action.

## 5. What is left on the shelves is a taxable supply

Here is the part almost nobody budgets for.

If input VAT was deducted when goods were bought for the business, the law does
not let those goods walk out into personal ownership untaxed. On closure,
remaining self-produced or acquired goods are **treated as supplied by the
business to itself** — the deemed supply of leftover goods.

The candidates are the obvious ones: unsold stock, raw materials, business
fixtures, machinery and equipment, some vehicles, buildings and structures.

But **stock sold before closing is not this**. Selling remaining inventory to
customers or another business at a normal price is an ordinary sale, invoiced and
taxed as one. Selling the fittings to whoever takes over the business is an
ordinary supply too. What falls into the deemed-supply net is what is still there
on the closure date and converts into personal property.

Sorting the shelves into those four boxes before the closure date is the whole
of the work. Doing it afterwards means reconstructing it from memory against a
filing deadline.

## 6. Ordinary stock and depreciable assets are priced differently

Ordinary remaining goods are valued at **market value** for the deemed supply.

Depreciable assets are not. They have their own formula in **article 66 of the
Enforcement Decree**, and it is neither market value nor the depreciated figure
in the accounts.

**The two statutory formulas** — applied to acquisition cost, counting elapsed
VAT taxable periods:

| Asset | Supply value | Maximum periods |
|---|---|---|
| Buildings and structures | acquisition cost × [1 − 5% × elapsed periods] | 20 |
| Other depreciable assets | acquisition cost × [1 − 25% × elapsed periods] | 4 |
| Ordinary stock and materials | market value | — |

Korean VAT taxable periods are half-yearly. Four periods is roughly two years;
twenty is roughly ten.

The rates are tracked separately —
[machinery and equipment](/tracked/deemed-supply-depreciable-asset-deduction-rate/)
and [buildings](/tracked/deemed-supply-building-deduction-rate/) — because the
two get conflated constantly, and using the building rate on a machine
overstates the deduction fivefold.

## 7. An old machine is often worth nothing here — a new one is not

The formula has a consequence worth planning around.

A machine bought with input VAT deducted, held for more than four taxable
periods — about two years — reaches zero under the 25% deduction. There is no
deemed-supply VAT left on it at all, however much it is still worth on the
second-hand market.

A machine bought shortly before closing is the opposite: most of its acquisition
cost is still in the base, and the VAT on it lands in the final return.

Which means the thing to do before setting a closure date is open the fixed
asset register and check four columns for each item — **acquisition date**,
**acquisition cost**, **whether input VAT was deducted**, and **elapsed taxable
periods**. That is the entire calculation, and it is knowable in advance.

## 8. Fit-out is not automatically a leftover asset

Money spent on interior work does not mean that whole spend becomes a deemed
supply on closure.

What decides it is ownership and structure: who owns the physical fixtures,
whether the work was capitalised as an asset, whether it can be removed, and
whether it belongs to the landlord under the lease.

In a leased commercial space the fit-out's fate at the end of the term —
removed, restored to original condition, or left to the landlord — changes the
answer. That is a question for the lease, and it should be settled alongside the
closure rather than after it.
