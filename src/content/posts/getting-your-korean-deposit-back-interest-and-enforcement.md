The leasehold registration order preserves a claim. It does not collect it.

Turning an unreturned deposit into money is a second procedure, and it runs on
two interest rates that start on two different events — neither of which is the
day you got angry.

## 1. The deposit and the keys are concurrent obligations

The landlord must return the deposit. The tenant must return the property. In
principle these obligations are set against each other.

Which has a consequence tenants rarely expect.

A tenant who stays in the apartment and says "the lease is over, so pay me 5% a
year from today" can meet a defence of concurrent performance: the landlord
answering that they have not had the property back, so they are not in default.

So in a late-interest claim the termination date is only half the question. The
other half is **when the tenant actually delivered up the property, or properly
tendered it**.

## 2. Tender means something more specific than saying you are ready

"I'm ready to leave whenever you pay" is a sentence. Making the landlord's delay
provable takes something objective.

<div class="steps">
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">1</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Remove everything</strong> — the property is empty, and photographed empty.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">2</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Settle the management fees</strong> and keep the receipts.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">3</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Have the keys and card keys ready</strong> to hand over.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">4</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Notify in writing</strong> that you will hand over the keys against return of the deposit.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">5</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>If you do hand over</strong>, record the date and the fact — it is far easier to prove than a tender.</div></div>
</div>

## 3. Late interest starts at 5%

Absent an agreed rate, where the landlord is in default on returning the deposit,
the [Civil Act statutory rate of 5% a year](/tracked/civil-default-interest-rate/)
is the reference for late interest.

On a ₩200,000,000 deposit that is ₩10,000,000 a year — roughly **₩27,400 a day**.

It is worth computing, because it converts an argument into a running number and
because it is the figure a landlord's "just wait a bit longer" is quietly asking
you to fund.

When it starts, as above, depends on the facts around delivery rather than on the
expiry date alone.

## 4. The 12% is real, and it is not automatic

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">It does not run from the day you sue</p>
  <p>The
  <a href="/tracked/litigation-promotion-interest-rate/">12% rate</a> applies from
  the day after the complaint — or a document equivalent to it — is
  <strong>served on the debtor</strong>. Not from instructing a lawyer, and not
  from filing. And where the court finds the landlord had reasonable grounds to
  dispute whether the obligation exists or how large it is, the higher rate can be
  restricted for a period before judgment.</p>
</div>

So the ordinary shape of a claim is two segments:

| Period | Rate |
|---|---|
| From the date of default | [5% a year](/tracked/civil-default-interest-rate/) |
| From the day after service of the complaint | [12% a year](/tracked/litigation-promotion-interest-rate/) |

Two rates, two starting events, and both of the events are things the tenant can
document at the time rather than reconstruct later.

## 5. Payment order or lawsuit

**A payment order** fits a clean claim: the amount and the termination are not
genuinely in dispute and the landlord simply is not paying. The court can issue it
on the documents, and if the landlord is properly served and does not object
within the set period it acquires the enforceability of a final judgment. It is
simpler and can be faster.

The limit is that an objection sends the case into ordinary civil proceedings
anyway.

**A substantive action** is often the better first move where the landlord is
actively fighting on the deposit amount, restoration costs or unpaid rent —
because that fight is going to happen regardless, and starting it in the
procedure that will resolve it saves a lap.

## 6. A judgment is a title, not a payment

A finalised payment order or judgment gives you an enforcement title. It does not
give you the money.

What follows is enforcement against the landlord's assets:

- the leased property itself
- other real estate
- bank deposits
- receivables the landlord is owed by third parties
- a portion of wages, if the landlord is employed — subject to the statutorily exempt portion

The wage point deserves the qualifier. Wages are attachable only above a
protected floor, so a landlord on a modest salary is not a fast route to a large
deposit.

## 7. Forcing a sale of the property, and what actually comes back

Where the landlord will not pay and has nothing else, the enforcement title
supports an application for a compulsory auction of the leased housing.

What you receive from it is decided by everything ranking around you: prior
mortgages, taxes, other tenants, attachments and provisional attachments, against
your own fixed date and opposing power.

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">Winning does not guarantee recovery</p>
  <p>A judgment for the full deposit and a distribution of the full deposit are
  different outcomes. An auction does not promise a market price, and everything
  ahead of you is paid first. This is the reason the register and prior-ranking
  claims are analysed <em>before</em> signing — by the time you are enforcing, the
  ranking is already fixed. See
  <a href="/cost-of-living/rental-deposit-protection-korea/">protecting your housing deposit in Korea</a>.</p>
</div>

## 8. "When the next tenant moves in" is the landlord's problem

It is true that the market often works this way — a new tenant's deposit funds
the departing tenant's repayment.

It is not a legal position. Where the lease has properly ended, the fact that no
new tenant has been found, or that the landlord needs to sell the property first,
does not extinguish the repayment claim or extend its due date. That is the
landlord's funding risk, not a term of your contract.

Which sets the rule for how a tenant should behave in this situation: move on
your own termination date and your own procedural steps, not on the landlord's
financing plan.

## The sequence, and what to keep at each stage

| Stage | The step | What to keep |
|---|---|---|
| Before expiry | Refuse renewal, or terminate a renewed lease | Message, content-certified mail, delivery certification |
| Lease ends | Demand return of the deposit | Contract, fixed date, transfer records |
| Not returned | Apply for the leasehold registration order | Register, address filing records, contract |
| Before moving | Confirm the registration is actually recorded | Fresh certified copy of the register |
| Moving out | Deliver the property, hand over the keys | Photographs, settled fees, delivery evidence |
| Claim | Demand the deposit with late interest | Interest calculation, content-certified mail |
| Proceedings | Payment order or civil action | The whole chain of evidence |
| Enforcement | Auction, attachment of deposits and receivables | The finalised enforcement title |

The most dangerous choice in a deposit dispute is trusting the landlord's promise
and letting each legal moment slide past.

Whether the termination notice actually arrived, whether the leasehold
registration was actually completed, when the property was actually delivered, when
the complaint was actually served — those four dates decide your opposing power,
your late interest and whether enforcement gets you paid.

It is not a matter of asking "when will you pay me" more insistently. It is a
procedure for stacking dates and evidence in order until the claim becomes
something a court can enforce.
