**A guarantee can protect the bank, the tenant, or both. Unless you know which obligation is guaranteed, you do not know what you are buying.**

A foreign tenant hears three abbreviations while arranging a Korean jeonse lease: HF, HUG and SGI. One banker mentions a guarantee certificate. Another offers deposit-return protection. A third says foreign customers can sometimes use an SGI-backed facility. The tenant concludes that all three providers offer roughly the same product, differing mainly in fees.

That conclusion can be expensive. A guarantee securing the bank's loan is not the same promise as a guarantee returning the tenant's deposit when a landlord fails to repay it. And the ability of a foreigner to purchase one provider's deposit-return protection does not prove the same applicant can obtain that provider's loan-security guarantee.

The practical test is threefold: **who is insured, against which failure, under which eligibility rules?** The logos are not enough.

## Start with the two debts in a jeonse transaction

In a common jeonse arrangement, the tenant owes the bank money borrowed toward the deposit, while the landlord owes the tenant the deposit back at the end of the lease. These are separate debts.

A **loan guarantee** supports a lender when the borrower fails to repay covered obligations. A **deposit-return guarantee** supports the tenant against specified failures by the landlord to return the deposit. Some products combine or connect both protections; others do not.

Consider a simplified lease with a ₩200 million deposit, of which the tenant supplies ₩60 million and the bank finances ₩140 million. If the tenant defaults, the bank cares about the loan claim. If the landlord cannot return the deposit, the tenant cares about recovering the ₩200 million and settling the bank claim. The existence of one guarantee does not automatically establish that the other risk is covered in full.

Look for the insured party, coverage amount, covered event, deductible or uncovered portion, claim procedure and exclusions. That is more informative than the provider's initials.

## HF: a public housing-finance guarantor, but not universal access

The Korea Housing Finance Corporation (HF, 한국주택금융공사) publishes jeonse funding guarantee products. Its general jeonse guarantee explains that the guarantee supports a bank's loan, that applications are generally handled through participating banks, and that the bank's credit decision remains separate even when guarantee conditions are met.

This is the first important gate: HF's willingness to issue a guarantee is not an order to the bank to lend. The second gate is applicant and product eligibility. A public web page describing deposit amounts and maximum guarantee figures does not establish that every foreign-national tenant qualifies. Some policy products have nationality or household-related restrictions; bank-specific arrangements can differ.

HF also has guarantee products tied to different borrower categories and purposes. Do not take a headline maximum from the general product finder and assume it applies to a foreign applicant, an individual lease, or the selected bank. The real usable amount may be lower because of recognized income, existing debt, rent-deposit limits or the bank's own rules.

Before asking a bank for “an HF loan,” provide your nationality, residence status, lease location, deposit, household position and income record. Ask which **named HF guarantee product** is being evaluated and whether the applicant class is admitted under its current terms.

## HUG: the foreign-tenant distinction that must not be erased

HUG—the Housing & Urban Guarantee Corporation (주택도시보증공사)—offers multiple housing guarantees. Its published materials draw a particularly important line between **전세보증금반환보증**, the jeonse deposit-return guarantee, and **전세금안심대출보증**, a product combining deposit-return protection with a loan-related guarantee component.

HUG does draw a line between the two, and the line matters: the deposit-return guarantee and the loan-associated **안심보증** are separate products with separate eligibility. What we could **not** confirm is where foreign tenants currently sit on each one. HUG's foreign-tenant FAQ did not load when this article was checked on 9 October 2026, so no eligibility claim is made here from it. Ask the bank which of the two products your financing actually uses, then confirm eligibility with HUG directly before you sign anything.

Do not quote “HUG accepts foreigners” without adding **for which product**. Likewise, do not claim every HUG-backed jeonse loan is open to all foreigners based on a different HUG guarantee's acceptance criteria. At publication time, readers should be directed to the live HUG FAQ and product terms because eligibility can change.

For qualifying tenants, deposit-return protection can be a separate risk-management decision even where the loan itself uses another provider or funding arrangement. The tenant still has to meet HUG's property, lease, legal-priority, amount, deadline and documentation requirements. Foreign status is not the only screen.

## SGI: private-sector guarantee arrangements and bank-specific access

Seoul Guarantee Insurance (SGI, 서울보증보험) is another name that appears in Korean rental-deposit finance. Bank products may use SGI guarantee or insurance arrangements, but availability must be evaluated at the level of the specific bank product and SGI policy. “SGI-backed” is a description of a funding or risk-sharing structure, not a blanket approval for any foreign resident.

For foreign applicants, SGI routes are worth asking about precisely because a bank that cannot use one public guarantee may have a different product architecture. This does **not** imply SGI will automatically approve any foreign tenant denied by HF or HUG. Residence status, income, lease terms, property, coverage and the bank's underwriting still matter.

There is a documentation consequence too. A bank can require a particular lease structure, landlord notice, assignment of lease-deposit claims or verification of the landlord's cooperation. These are not cosmetic formalities. They affect the institution's ability to recover money if repayment fails.

Ask the branch to identify the precise SGI product, who pays the premium, when coverage begins, what happens if the lease changes and whether a foreign tenant under your status is eligible on the actual application date. A verbal phrase such as “our foreigner loan uses SGI” is only the start of the review.

## What a guarantee does not do

It does not make an ineligible borrower eligible. It does not turn a defective lease into a valid security package. It does not eliminate monthly interest payments. It does not guarantee the bank will accept the applicant's income. It does not imply the tenant will recover every won under every conceivable landlord default.

Think of the guarantee as a contract with a defined claim—not an abstract assurance that the transaction is safe. The applicant must understand whose liability it supports and which events permit a claim.

A guarantee may also impose ongoing obligations. If the tenant changes the deposit, alters the lease, moves, sublets or fails to complete a registration or notice requirement, the provider may require approval or updated documents. Whether coverage survives a change depends on the contract. “I was approved once” is not an enduring answer.

## The lease itself is part of the credit file

Even strong salary evidence cannot repair a building with unacceptable legal risks. Depending on the guarantee, review can involve the landlord's identity, title, mortgage liens, senior tenant deposits, appraised value, lease duration, deposit amount, address registration and a fixed-date stamp (확정일자).

For foreign tenants, registration and possession require particular care. HUG's FAQ discusses foreigner registration, registered-address change, occupancy and the legal requirements for opposability and priority under Korean residential tenancy protections. It is unsafe to assume that signing a lease alone secures every legal priority needed for a guarantee.

Ask for the property register and have the transaction reviewed before paying a large, nonrecoverable amount. If the guarantee route is essential to financing, the contract timetable should allow sufficient pre-screening. Real estate agents, lenders and guarantors each see different parts of the risk; one party's reassuring answer may not cover another party's requirements.

The worst sequence is to pay a deposit based on a preliminary bank conversation, then discover the guarantee application is structurally ineligible. Reverse the sequence: borrower screening, property screening, contract conditions, and only then final funding decisions.

## Premiums, limits and the four-number trap

A borrower may be told a guarantee supports “up to” a large amount. That figure can be an institutional ceiling, not a personal offer. The actual usable borrowing can be constrained by several limits: guarantee maximum, permitted percentage of the deposit, bank recognized income and debt capacity, and property eligibility or valuation.

The final amount is effectively governed by the binding constraint among these relevant limits. An applicant who plans around the largest advertised number may face a late financing shortfall. Request a written estimate showing the particular calculation used in your transaction.

Guarantee premiums matter but seldom should be evaluated alone. Consider the bank rate, premium, administrative cost, claim protection, lease risk and renewal obligations together. A cheaper premium with a substantially narrower covered risk may not represent a better outcome.

An illustration: if a tenant borrows ₩120 million against a ₩170 million deposit, an advertised maximum guarantee of ₩200 million does not mean the individual can obtain ₩200 million. Other constraints may reduce coverage or the permitted borrowing amount. The illustration is conceptual, not a quotation of any provider's 2026 limit.

## A practical approval sequence

First, decide whether you need financing, deposit-return protection or both. Second, identify your proposed bank loan product. Third, ask which **specific guarantee** is mandatory for that loan and whether it accepts your nationality and residence status. Fourth, have the property and lease structure screened. Fifth, verify the income and DSR conditions. Sixth, check premium and contract dates. Seventh, ensure the written loan and insurance documentation actually match the intended transaction.

For an applicant with an F-6 spouse, do not presume that marital status overrides product eligibility. For an F-4 foreign-national overseas Korean, do not rely on an answer applicable to a Korean citizen abroad. For an F-5 permanent resident, do not presume policy housing guarantees treat permanent residents as citizens. Precise legal identity matters.

If the banker suggests switching guarantee routes, compare not only the interest rate but also whether the tenant personally obtains deposit-return protection. A loan guarantee protecting the bank does not necessarily replace tenant insurance against landlord nonpayment.

## The conversation to have before signing the lease

Ask: “Who is guaranteeing what? Is this protection for the bank's loan, my deposit, or both? Does this guarantee **today** accept a foreign-national tenant holding my exact residence status? Which property conditions must pass? What happens to coverage if my lease or status changes?”

If the institution cannot answer those questions before you commit, treat the financing as uncertain. Korean housing contracts can involve large deposits; ambiguity at this stage can be far more expensive than an extra week spent reviewing documentation.

## Bottom line

HF, HUG and SGI are not three interchangeable stamps of approval. Their guarantee contracts cover different risks and have different eligibility rules. The clearest foreign-tenant warning is HUG's own distinction between deposit-return protection and its loan-associated safe-loan guarantee.

A useful comparison begins with the obligations, not the initials: **who owes whom, what is at risk, what document secures it, and who pays if it fails?** Once those are clear, a bank can explain which financing structure is genuinely available.
