Korea publishes one number and enforces another.

The 2026 minimum wage is **₩10,320 an hour**. The lawful monthly minimum for
someone on a 40-hour week is **₩2,156,880** — and you cannot get from the
first to the second by multiplying by the hours anyone actually works.

The difference is a paid day off that no one works, written into the Labor
Standards Act since 1953.

<p class="hand">209 is a divisor, not a target. Nobody is expected to work it.</p>

## The weekly holiday is a statute, not a bonus

Article 55 requires the employer to give at least one paid holiday a week on
average, and the Enforcement Decree ties it to full attendance on that
week's contractual working days.

So it is not discretionary, not a welfare payment, and not something a
company can drop because business is slow. Where the conditions are met, pay
arises for a day on which no work is done.

For a 40-hour employee the weekly holiday is normally **8 hours**, which
makes the week:

```
worked        40 h × ₩10,320  =  ₩412,800
weekly holiday 8 h × ₩10,320  =   ₩82,560
                                 ─────────
                                  ₩495,360
```

Against 40 hours actually worked, the eight paid holiday hours are a 20%
addition. That is where the "20% uplift" line comes from — and it is worth
being precise about it: **the hourly rate does not rise by 20%.** In the
standard 40-hour model, paid hours exceed worked hours by 20%. Change the
contractual hours and the ratio changes with them.

## Condition one is about your contract, not your timesheet

The 15-hour test runs on **contractual working hours averaged over four
weeks**, and that distinction decides real cases.

An employee contracted for 18 hours a week who worked only 10 in a week
shortened by a public holiday or a company closure has not thereby become a
10-hour employee. The contractual hours did not change.

The reverse is also true, and it is the version employers get wrong: a
contract written for 14 hours that in practice runs at 20 hours every week
is judged on how the relationship actually operates, not on the number typed
into the document.

## Condition two is attendance, not punctuality

Full attendance means you were not **absent** on your contractual working
days.

| Event | Treated as absence? |
|---|---|
| Lateness | No |
| Leaving early | No |
| Annual leave | No |
| Statutory or agreed holiday | No |
| Company closure | No |
| Unexcused absence on a contractual working day | Yes |

Two instances of lateness do not add up to a lost day, and a lost day is not
manufactured out of minutes. Employers running attendance systems need to
keep lateness, early departure, leave, closure and absence as five separate
categories, because only the last one moves the weekly holiday.

## Condition three decides your final week

The week you leave is where this most often goes wrong, because people treat
their last working day and their legal leaving date as the same thing. They
are not.

Take contractual working days Monday to Friday, with Sunday as the weekly
holiday:

| Worked Mon–Fri, then | Weekly holiday pay |
|---|---|
| Left on Saturday | Does not arise |
| Employment ran through Sunday | Arises |

One day of paperwork, one week's holiday pay. Worth checking the leaving
date on the resignation, not just the last day you came in.

## Part-time: the weekly holiday scales

Above the 15-hour line, the weekly holiday is proportional:

```
weekly holiday hours = weekly contractual hours ÷ 40 × 8
```

At the 2026 rate of [₩10,320 an hour](/tracked/minimum-wage/):

| Weekly contractual hours | Weekly holiday hours | Weekly holiday pay | Weekly pay basis |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 40 | 8 | ₩82,560 | ₩495,360 |
| 20 | 4 | ₩41,280 | ₩247,680 |
| 15 | 3 | ₩30,960 | ₩185,760 |
| 14 | none | ₩0 | ₩144,480 |

Hourly rate ₩10,320, no other allowances, conditions met. A simplified
model, not a payslip.

## The one-hour cliff, and why it shapes the job market

Look at the last two rows. One additional contractual hour a week moves the
weekly pay basis by ₩41,280 — an hour of work at ₩10,320 plus three hours of
weekly holiday at ₩30,960.

That cliff is what produces **쪼개기 알바**, splitting one job into pieces
that each stay below the line. A single 28-hour role becomes two 14-hour
contracts, and neither person is entitled.

<div class="callout callout-note">
  <p class="callout-title">Not every short-hours job is avoidance</p>
  <p>Some work genuinely is short-hours, and treating every sub-15-hour
  contract as evasion is as wrong as pretending the incentive does not exist.
  The distinction that matters is whether the contracted hours reflect how the
  job is actually run. A 14-hour contract worked at 20 hours a week is a
  problem; a 14-hour contract worked at 14 hours is a job.</p>
</div>

Splitting is not free for the employer either. More contracts mean more
hiring, more training, more scheduling, more handover and more variance in
service quality — and for the worker, several workplaces, unstable income
and weaker social insurance standing.

## What the reform argument is actually about

The debate over weekly holiday pay is usually reported as pay going up or
down. It is really about structure.

| | Argument |
|---|---|
| For abolition or absorption | Separating the minimum wage from weekly holiday pay makes the employer's real hourly cost higher than the published rate, and creates the 15-hour cliff that distorts hiring — the pressure lands hardest on small and marginal businesses |
| For keeping it | The entitlement has been part of real wages for decades, so removing it cuts monthly income for existing workers, and the paid weekly holiday is itself a rest-and-living-wage protection |

And the distinction that most coverage collapses:

<div class="steps">
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">1</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Abolition with the hourly rate unchanged.</strong> Monthly lawful pay falls for everyone currently entitled.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">2</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Absorption into a higher base rate.</strong> Total pay can be held roughly flat while the structure is simplified.</div></div>
</div>

So "abolish weekly holiday pay" does not by itself mean a pay cut, and
"reform weekly holiday pay" does not by itself mean incomes are protected.
Which base hourly rate replaces it decides the outcome, and that is the part
worth reading for.

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">As of 2026 nothing has been abolished</p>
  <p>Article 55 is in force, and the official 2026 monthly conversion —
  ₩2,156,880 on a 209-hour basis — is published on exactly that assumption. A
  proposal under discussion is not a defence for underpaying now.</p>
</div>

## Checking a real salary

Not by looking at the hourly rate. In this order:

```
contractual working hours in the contract
  → four-week average: 15 hours or more?
  → contractual working days
  → full attendance that week
  → the weekly holiday itself
  → weekly holiday hours (hours ÷ 40 × 8)
  → minimum wage applied
  → monthly total, against 209 hours
  → compare with the payslip
```

Two errors sit at opposite ends of that list. Paying only the base hourly
rate at or above the minimum while never paying the weekly holiday is
underpayment. Taking a monthly salary that **already includes** the weekly
holiday component and demanding it again as a separate allowance is
double-counting. For monthly-paid staff, the question is how the agreed
salary is composed — which is a contract question before it is a payroll
one.

The [209-hour minimum wage calculator](/tools/minimum-wage-calculator/) runs
the arithmetic from your contractual hours. What the resulting gross turns
into after deductions is in
[reading your Korean payslip](/employment/reading-your-korean-payslip/), and
how the hours and weekly holiday should have been written down in the first
place is in
[your Korean employment contract](/employment/your-korean-employment-contract-what-to-check/).

Understanding the Korean minimum wage is not knowing that it is ₩10,320. It
is knowing how contractual hours, the 15-hour threshold, full attendance,
the paid weekly holiday and 209 hours combine into the figure that is
actually enforceable.
