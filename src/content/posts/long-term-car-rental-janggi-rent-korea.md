One of the least meaningful numbers in car finance is the monthly
payment. A ₩800,000/month long-term rental can look cheaper than a
₩950,000/month lease and flip to more expensive the moment you equalize
insurance, vehicle tax, upfront payments, residual values, and the
end-of-term buyout.

What actually differs between the three structures is **who owns the
car, how much cash goes in up front, and who captures (or eats) the
car's value at the end.** The number that settles the comparison is
total cost of ownership:

> **TCO = initial cash out + payments over the term + insurance, taxes,
> maintenance + financing cost + end-of-contract costs − refunded
> deposit − disposal value**

Run 장기렌트 (long-term rental), lease, and installment purchase through
that formula over the same horizon, or you are comparing marketing
copy. (This is the structural sequel to
[the lease pillar](/cars/car-lease-in-korea-for-foreigners-real-cost/)
and [the used-car purchase guide](/cars/buying-a-used-car-in-korea-as-a-foreigner/).)

## Three structures, three owners

| | Long-term rental | Operating lease | Installment purchase |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Vehicle owner | Rental company | Leasing company | Buyer |
| Plates | 하·허·호 (rental series) | Normal | Normal |
| Insurance | Usually inside rental fee | Your own policy | Your own policy |
| Vehicle tax | Inside rental fee | Per contract | Owner pays |
| At maturity | Return / buy | Return / buy / re-lease | Keep / sell |
| Residual-value risk | Mostly rental co. | Mostly lessor (operating) | Buyer |
| Early exit | Penalty / settlement | Termination charges / transfer | Repay loan, sell |

The Credit Finance Association's framework draws the underlying line:
in an **operating lease** the lessor generally bears residual-value risk
and return is the default; a **finance lease** shifts residual risk
toward the user and functions close to acquisition financing.
Installment purchase is the simplest: you own the car, a finance company
lends the purchase money, and every won of depreciation is yours.

## The "rental saves you the 10% VAT" line is not how quotes work

Rental firms acquire cars as business assets and price rentals from
their acquisition cost, taxes, insurance, funding cost, residual
assumptions, and margin. That business structure does differ from a
consumer buying a car — but compressing it into "the rental company
buys tax-free, so you save 10% of the car price" is wrong: whatever
advantages exist are already blended into your quote alongside the
company's costs and profit. The only test that survives is **total
customer outlay over identical months.**

## Deposits and prepayments are the classic monthly-payment distorters

A ₩50M car at ₩1,500,000 down and ₩500,000/month versus zero-down at
₩800,000/month: the first "looks" cheaper monthly. But if the
₩15M is a **prepayment (선수금)** — prepaid usage fees — it is gone. A
**deposit (보증금)** returns at the end absent unsettled amounts. In
TCO terms:

> prepayment → count fully as cost
> deposit → subtract the refund, but charge the **opportunity cost** of
> the locked capital

A ₩20M deposit parked for five years is not "free because it comes
back" — at a 4% alternative return, that is roughly ₩4M of forgone
earnings across the term.

## Purchase TCO: taxes in, resale value out

Buying an ordinary private passenger car triggers acquisition tax at
the standard 7% (2026 Local Tax Act; light cars and exempt categories
differ) — about ₩4.2M on a ₩60M car — plus bond costs, plates and
registration, then annual vehicle tax and insurance. Against all that,
the eventual sale price comes *off* the total:

> **Purchase TCO = price + acquisition/registration costs + total
> interest + vehicle tax + insurance + maintenance − resale proceeds**

The more expensive the car, the more the final resale term dominates
the outcome.

## In a lease, the whole game is the residual value

Identical cars, wildly different lease quotes — the first place to look
is the residual. Set a ₩60M car's 48-month residual at ₩30M and the
lessor recovers less principal during the term than at a ₩20M
residual, so the monthly payment drops. If you intend to return the
car, a high residual is your friend. If you intend to **keep** it:

> **Buyout-path lease TCO = prepayment + total lease payments +
> insurance/taxes/other costs + buyout price + transfer costs −
> refunded deposit**

A quote ₩100,000/month cheaper that carries a ₩10M higher buyout is not
the cheaper contract for a keeper. Demand all four numbers together:
monthly payment, total payments, residual/buyout, and early-termination
charges.

## Insurance history compounds silently across the choice

Rental insurance lives inside the rental company's policy: your
personal premium does not surcharge after an accident the way your own
policy would (deductibles and renewal terms are the exposure instead).
Lease and purchase run on **your own policy** — which means your
no-claims history keeps accumulating.

That cuts both ways for foreigners. A long clean record favors
own-policy structures. A newcomer with no Korean insurance history
faces high first-year premiums — the
[overseas-history recognition process](/insurance/korean-car-insurance-for-expats-explained/)
belongs in this calculation — and anyone who will still be driving in
Korea in year six should price the value of *continuously building* a
personal insurance record into the comparison.

## The company-car "₩15M limit" is not ₩8M + ₩7M

The most-misexplained number in Korean company-car tax. Two separate
mechanisms:

- **₩8,000,000/year** caps the deductible depreciation — or the
  depreciation-equivalent inside lease/rental payments — per vehicle,
  with the excess carried forward.
- **₩15,000,000** is not a fixed cost allowance at all. With **no
  driving log**, total vehicle-related costs up to ₩15M are deemed 100%
  business use; above that, the recognized ratio becomes:

> no-log business-use ratio = ₩15,000,000 ÷ total vehicle costs

₩20M of annual costs without a log → 75% recognized. "Related costs"
sweep in depreciation/rent, insurance, fuel, repairs, vehicle tax, and
tolls. If real business use is higher than the formula gives you, keep
the driving log and prove it — the NTS explains the ₩15M no-log
threshold and the ₩8M depreciation cap as separate rules, exactly as
the [lease pillar](/cars/car-lease-in-korea-for-foreigners-real-cost/)
warned.

And do not confuse deduction with refund: ₩15M of recognized expense at
a simplified 20% marginal rate saves about **₩3M of tax** — you spent
₩15M to save ₩3M. Expensing never makes an expensive car cheap; it
discounts the after-tax price of money you still spent.

One more corporate condition: **business-use auto insurance**
(업무전용자동차보험) gates expense recognition — and rentals are not
exempt from discipline: for qualifying rental vehicles, a rider
restricting drivers to executives and employees is treated as meeting
the requirement. A CEO routing a family-use car through corporate
expenses has a business-use problem no procurement structure fixes.

## The DSR question: excluded from the formula ≠ invisible to banks

Precision matters here, because two true statements get merged into one
false one.

Per FSC guidance, **installment finance and leases sit among the
exceptions in the borrower-level DSR calculation** — so a car
installment's monthly payment does not enter the regulatory DSR formula
the way a credit loan or card loan does. (Our
[DSR/LTV reference](/loans/dsr-ltv-korea-lending-rules-explained/)
covers the exception list.)

But halbu and leases are credit products under the Specialized Credit
Finance Business Act, underwritten through real credit screening — the
obligation, your payment burden, and the credit records exist. A bank's
**internal repayment-capacity review** (CSS) can and does weigh them
when you later apply for a mortgage or credit loan. Long-term rental,
being a lease of the *rental-contract* kind rather than a consumer
acquisition loan, does not create a car-purchase debt in your name —
but rental firms still screen, and can demand deposits from high-value,
thin-credit, or foreign customers.

So separate the questions: *"does it enter the borrower-level DSR
formula?"* (installments/leases: no, as exceptions) and *"can it affect
my future loan review?"* (yes, through credit records and internal
scoring). Anyone planning a mortgage should decide car financing on the
whole-picture basis, not on "it doesn't count in DSR."

Corporate accounting adds its own layer: under K-IFRS 1116, lessees
generally recognize right-of-use assets and lease liabilities — the old
"operating leases stay off the books" line no longer holds for K-IFRS
filers, and a CEO should never conflate personal DSR treatment with the
company's balance-sheet lease debt.

## High mileage rewrites the comparison

A 10,000km/year driver and a 40,000km/year driver should not sign the
same contract. Return-type rentals and operating leases carry contracted
mileage and condition-settlement clauses: contract at 20,000km/year,
return at 150,000km after five years, and excess-mileage charges apply —
along with settlement for accident repairs, wheel and tire condition,
and panel damage. Heavy drivers — sales roles, multi-site owners —
should read the **excess-mileage unit price and return conditions**
before the monthly fee. Planning to buy out at maturity mutes the
return-settlement risk but activates the residual/buyout cost instead.

## The five-year comparison, structure by structure

Same car, 60 months:

> **Rental:** prepayment + 60 rental payments + maintenance/fuel +
> buyout (if buying) − refunded deposit
> *(insurance and vehicle tax already inside the fee — do not
> double-count)*
>
> **Operating lease:** prepayment + 60 lease payments + insurance +
> vehicle tax and other separate costs + maintenance + buyout (if
> buying) − refunded deposit
>
> **Purchase:** price + acquisition/registration + total installment
> interest + insurance + vehicle tax + maintenance − projected resale
> at month 60

Business users then recompute each on an after-tax basis:

> **After-tax TCO = TCO − actual tax saved**

The financially cheapest structure is the one with the lowest after-tax
TCO — never the one with the lowest monthly payment.

## Foreigners: never sign past your realistic horizon

For an E-7 employee, a 60-month rental or lease is a stay-planning
question before it is a car question. Providers screen domestic income,
employment, credit data, ARC details, and stay stability — but no
industry formula like "F-5 approves without deposit" or "E-7 needs two
years remaining" exists. Long-stay statuses with empty
[credit files](/loans/korean-credit-score-for-foreigners-nice-kcb/) and
unverifiable income face conditions; a long-tenured E-7 with high
declared income and clean records gives underwriters plenty. The same
[recoverability-in-numbers logic](/loans/can-foreigners-get-a-loan-in-korea/)
as every other foreigner credit decision.

## Usage decides the winner

- **Hold 7–10 years, drive a lot, accept used-car price risk** →
  direct purchase tends to be simplest and cheapest.
- **Replace every 3–5 years, want disposal/insurance/tax handled** →
  rental or operating lease earns its convenience.
- **Long personal no-claims history, prefer normal plates** → lease
  gains relative value.
- **New company preserving cash for operations** → rental/lease beats
  cash purchase on capital efficiency; a cash-rich company facing
  expensive auto financing may rationally buy outright.

The question is never "how much per month?" It is:

> **Over five years, how much leaves my account in total — and what
> asset remains when the contract ends?**

And for a company, one more line: *of that total, how much does tax law
actually recognize, and what is the after-tax burden?* The ranking of
rental, lease, and installment is not decided by product names. It is
decided when holding period, mileage, financing cost, insurance
history, residual value, tax effect, and the opportunity cost of
capital are all converted into one TCO.
