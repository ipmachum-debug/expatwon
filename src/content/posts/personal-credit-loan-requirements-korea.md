Start by discarding two opposite errors. *"Foreigners can't get unsecured
loans in Korea"* is wrong — foreigner credit loan products exist today
with published terms, and some explicitly accept E-9 workers and D-2
students. But *"it works like a Korean salaryman's loan — score plus
salary, auto-approved"* is equally wrong.

With no collateral in the picture, the lender simultaneously weighs your
stay stability, Korean employment, declared income, credit file, and
existing debt — plus a factor locals never face: if the borrower leaves
the country or loses status, collection gets expensive, and internal
risk models price that in. As a working formula:

**approval odds = domestic credit file + recognized income + employment
stability + stay stability − existing debt and delinquency risk.**

And one myth to clear immediately: there is **no industry-wide legal
requirement** of six-to-twelve months of employment or one year of
remaining visa validity. Published products differ enormously — one
current savings-bank product accepts applicants with as little as **one
month of stay remaining**. What decides your outcome is the specific
product's terms and the lender's internal scoring, not your visa's name.

## A credit loan is secured by your future cash flow

A mortgage has a recoverable asset behind it. A credit loan has only
your future salary and business income. That is why the ARC is merely
the ticket to apply — it proves you exist legally, not that you can
repay.

What the lender actually wants to see is disarmingly simple: that you
live in Korea lawfully, where you work and for how long, what income
shows up in NTS and social-insurance records, and how your existing
debts and payment history look.

Which produces the classic foreigner trap: you may genuinely earn ₩7M a
month — but if part is paid in cash or into an overseas account and
your *declared* Korean income is ₩30M a year, do not expect the lender
to credit you with ₩84M. In unsecured lending, **verifiable income beats
actual income**, every time.

## The visa is a recovery-risk variable, not a grade

F-5 (permanent residency), F-6 (marriage), F-4 (overseas Korean) and
F-2 (residence) read as low stay-uncertainty. E-7 professionals with
regular Korean payroll and long tenure compete perfectly well. But the
tidy table — *F-5 approved, E-7 conditional, E-9 and D-2 rejected* —
does not survive contact with published products:

- **OK Savings Bank's Hi-OK loan** currently lists **E-9, E-7, F-2,
  F-6 and D-2 students** as eligible applicants — income-documenting
  foreign workers with a NICE score of 300 or above, up to ₩60M at
  published rates of roughly 14.23–19.99% per year.
- **Welcome Savings Bank's foreigner loan** covers **E-9, E-7 and
  D-2**, up to ₩30M at 7.61–19.90% for up to 36 months — with
  eligibility from just **one month of stay remaining**.

So "D-2 can't get credit loans," "E-9 is shut out of the financial
system," and "you need a year of visa left" are all falsified by
published product pages. The visa gates which products you can enter;
inside an eligible product, the financial file decides.

## First-tier banks: don't assume the door is open either

The symmetric surprise runs the other way at the major banks.

KB's foreigner guidance likewise notes that lending to foreigners
involves additional identity and residence documentation. The efficient
move at a first-tier bank is not searching product names — it is asking
your branch directly whether their credit screening can process your
foreigner registration number for an unsecured loan, and on which
product.

Meanwhile, some regional banks publish foreigner products plainly —
Jeonbuk Bank runs **JB Bravo KOREA**, a credit loan line for foreign
residents by status. The pattern across the market: the institutions
most explicit about foreigner lending are often not the five big banks
but regional and savings banks that built separate evaluation for
exactly the profile first-tier automation handles worst — the
[thin-filer](/loans/korean-credit-score-for-foreigners-nice-kcb/).

The rational search order is therefore: **your main branch bank first →
regional and foreigner-specialist banks →
[savings banks](/loans/savings-banks-and-capital-companies-korea/) last** — using

formal applications at several lenders in a short window, which itself
reads badly in screening.

## The strongest file is four records that agree

Employment tenure requirements vary by product (3, 6, 12 months — same
as for Koreans; "foreigners need one year" is not a rule). What screens
powerfully is *coherence* across four data sources:

**long tenure at one employer + salary landing in the same account +
workplace health-insurance enrollment + NTS-confirmed earned income.**

When those four line up, the lender can read your cash flow at a
glance. The inverse profile — two months at a job, salary paid
overseas, no insurance enrollment visible, no Korean tax record — loses
in automated screening regardless of the actual salary.

For the self-employed and freelancers, the hierarchy is the same one
from [the freelancing guide](/business/freelancing-in-korea-legally/):
3.3% withholding is not income proof — what counts is the income figure
that survives your comprehensive tax filing. Gross deposits of ₩60M
with a filed income of ₩35M means the lender sees ₩35M. Undeclared cash
income raises your lifestyle, not your borrowing capacity — and
minimizing declared income to save tax is, financially, pre-lowering
your own future loan limits.

## A high bureau score is necessary, not sufficient

NICE and KCB scores matter — and they are never the whole decision. The
lender bolts the bureau score onto its own CSS: transaction period,
salary transfers, delinquencies, existing loans, card behavior,
occupation, income — and for foreigners, stay period and domestic
history on top. A NICE-900 rejection alongside a NICE-800 approval is a
perfectly normal pair of outcomes.

Read the savings-bank floors the same way: "NICE 300+" is an
application threshold, not an approval promise — both products above
state that internal screening can still decline. The
[credit-file strategy](/loans/korean-credit-score-for-foreigners-nice-kcb/)
is not score-chasing; it is building transaction history a lender can
read.

One more boundary from
[the internet-bank comparison](/banking/kakaobank-toss-bank-kbank-for-foreigners/):
account access and loan access are different doors. Internet banks'
emergency and salaryman loans run automated underwriting that largely
excludes or restricts foreigners today — a Toss Bank account does not
imply a Toss Bank loan.

## Stay period and loan term don't have to match

"One year of visa left means a one-year loan maximum" is another
formula the market does not follow: Welcome's product accepts one month
of remaining stay while lending to 36 months. So *loan term ≤ remaining
visa* is not a general rule.

What is true: lenders assess renewal prospects and departure risk, and
loan agreements can name status changes or departure as events
requiring notice — or, if the contract says so, triggering acceleration.
There is no general rule that visa expiry auto-accelerates every credit
loan on that date; there is also no license to ignore your contract. If
your status changes or you leave Korea long-term mid-loan, update your
records with the bank and read the covenant before it reads you.

## Every credit loan spends tomorrow's mortgage space

The cost that outweighs interest for many borrowers: **DSR capacity.**

Under borrower-level DSR (40% at banks), a ₩80M earner has roughly
₩32M/year of allowable debt service in total. If an existing credit
loan consumes ₩10M/year of that, a future mortgage gets ₩22M/year —
and the [stress-rate math](/loans/dsr-ltv-korea-lending-rules-explained/)
then shrinks the principal that ₩22M supports. Credit loans sit fully
inside DSR, and balances over ₩100M now pull stress DSR too.

The conclusion mirrors [the loans pillar](/loans/can-foreigners-get-a-loan-in-korea/):
foreigner credit lending is not a hunt for the borrower with the best
visa. An F-5 holder with no Korean income and a delinquency history is
a bad borrower; an E-7 engineer with five years at one company, ₩80M
declared to the NTS, salary at the lending bank and near-zero debt is a
rich, readable file. Status matters — **the continuity of verifiable
data matters more.**
