Holding foreign currency in Korea and sending money out of Korea are two
different financial acts, and conflating them is where most guidance goes
wrong.

A foreign currency deposit is simply foreign money — dollars, euro, yen,
renminbi — sitting in a Korean bank account. You might hold it to avoid
having every asset denominated in won, or to accumulate currency ahead
of tuition abroad, an overseas payment, or an import bill.

**The reporting, limits and evidence around actually sending money
abroad are a separate foreign exchange payment procedure and are not
covered here.** For that, see
[sending money from Korea abroad](/banking/how-to-send-money-from-korea-abroad/);
for the wider picture of exchanging, spending and moving money, see
[managing money in Korea](/cost-of-living/managing-money-in-korea-fx-remittance-cards/).

## Nationality is not the test. Residency is — and there are two of them

The Foreign Exchange Transactions Act governs foreign exchange dealings
in Korea, and it does not shut foreign nationals out of foreign currency
deposits.

What it does care about is whether you are a **resident or a
non-resident under that Act**. The Foreign Exchange Transactions
Regulations run residents' and non-residents' foreign currency funds
through different account structures, and an individual foreign resident
has their own foreign currency deposit account type available.

So holding an Alien Registration Card and living here a long time does
not automatically place you in one category. The bank looks at your
actual period of stay, your Korean address, your residence status and
your position under the Act, and sets the account type from that.

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">Two different residency tests, and they do not have to agree</p>
  <p>Residency under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act decides what kind
  of account you get. Residency under the tax law decides how the interest is
  taxed. They are separate assessments and you can land differently on each,
  which is why "I am a resident, so 15.4%" is not a safe assumption to make
  on your own.</p>
</div>

Opening one normally means an Alien Registration Card or domestic
residence report certificate, a passport, and a Korean address and
contact number — with more requested depending on the purpose of the
dealing and where the money came from. Foreign currency deposits sit
inside the customer due diligence and anti-money-laundering regime like
anything else, so a large cash deposit or an inbound overseas transfer
can bring a source-of-funds check with it.

## Demand or time: the choice is about when you need it

| | Demand deposit | Time deposit |
|---|---|---|
| Nature of funds | In and out at will | Locked for a term |
| Rate | Usually low | Contracted, by currency and term |
| Early access | Effectively free | Early termination can cost the contracted rate |
| Main purpose | Parking, holding, paying | Putting idle currency to work |
| Currency risk | Present | Present |
| Depositor protection | ₩100M limit, if a protected product | Same |

A foreign currency demand deposit holds what you converted and lets you
take it back to won, or use it for another currency dealing, whenever you
want. The rate is often unremarkable, but there is no maturity, which is
what makes it the right place to build a position in stages.

A time deposit locks currency for a term at that currency's contracted
rate. Currencies with higher international market rates — the dollar
being the obvious one — can pay quite differently from a won deposit at
the same bank. As of August 2026, KB and Woori were publishing pre-tax
rates around 3% a year on USD foreign currency time deposits, varying by
term.

Which is not the return. Take 3% interest and then watch the currency
fall 10% against the won, and the won value of the asset has gone down.
**A foreign currency deposit is a deposit product and a currency position
at the same time**, and only one of those two things is advertised.

## The four big banks differ by currency, not by a single minimum

The major commercial banks do not offer the same foreign currency
lineup, and the differences that matter are the currencies covered and
the individual product terms.

## The spread is the first number to work out, not the last

The rate you are shown as the market rate and the rate you actually
transact at are not the same. The bank works around a mid-market rate and
applies one rate when selling you currency and another when buying it
back. That difference is the spread.

A mid-market rate of ₩1,350 to the dollar does not mean you can buy a
dollar for ₩1,350. The bank's telegraphic transfer selling rate, and its
cash selling rate, both carry the spread.

**And "90% FX preference" does not mean 90% off the exchange rate.** It
means 90% off the spread the bank is charging. Your real acquisition cost
is:

<div class="steps">
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">1</div>
    <div class="step-body">The mid-market rate</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">2</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>plus</strong> the bank's spread</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">3</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>less</strong> whatever the preference rate takes off that spread</div></div>
</div>

For a position you intend to hold, converting a fixed amount at intervals
rather than the whole sum on one day spreads the entry price. Planning to
hold USD 10,000 is not a reason to buy USD 10,000 today. This is not a
strategy that improves returns — it reduces the risk of having bought the
entire position at one particular rate.

## Notes and electronic conversion cost different amounts

Having a foreign currency account does not make paying banknotes in and
out of it free. Handling physical currency costs the bank transport,
storage and exchange, so a cash handling fee can apply on deposits and
withdrawals of notes.

Currencies other than the dollar tend to be worse on this, and some
branches do not handle certain notes at all.

Converting electronically from your won account through internet or
mobile banking and crediting the foreign currency deposit skips the cash
handling entirely. If the point is holding an asset rather than carrying
notes, the electronic route between your won and currency accounts is the
simpler cost structure by a wide margin.

## Interest is taxed. The currency gain is a different question

Income tax law treats interest on a deposit received in Korea as interest
income. For an individual resident, ordinary withholding on deposit
interest is **15.4%** — 14% income tax plus 1.4% local income tax —
taken before the interest is paid out. Earn USD 1,000 of interest on a
dollar time deposit and what arrives is the after-tax figure.

Do not extend that automatically. As above, tax residency is its own
test: a tax non-resident's Korean deposit interest is domestic-source
interest income, where tax treaty provisions and separate withholding
rules have to be looked at. A deposit in a company's name is not
described by the individual 15.4% structure either — corporate interest
income belongs inside the corporate tax computation.

The gain from the currency itself is a separate matter again. An
individual who buys dollars at ₩1,300 and converts back at ₩1,400 has
made an ordinary currency gain from holding foreign money, and that is
not taxed the way deposit interest is.

**But avoid the sentence "FX gains are tax-free."** A product that
combines a deposit with forwards or swaps to manufacture what is in
substance interest can be classified as interest income under the Income
Tax Act. And translation gains and losses on a business's foreign
currency receivables and payables, or a company's realised FX gains and
losses, follow completely different treatment from an individual holding
currency. Keep the individual case and the business case apart.

## Protection is ₩100,000,000, and it is added up per bank

Where the product is a protected deposit under the Depositor Protection
Act, a foreign currency deposit is covered like any other.

Since 1 September 2025 Korea's protection limit has been **₩100,000,000**
per person per financial institution, principal plus stipulated interest
— double what it was before. A foreign currency deposit's protected
amount is worked out by converting to won at the rate applied when the
insured event occurs.

The part that catches people: it is **per institution, and protected
deposits inside one institution are added together**. Holding ₩70,000,000
in won deposits and ₩50,000,000 worth of protected foreign currency at
the same bank does not give you two separate ₩100,000,000 protections.

## Building the position, in order

  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">1</div>
    <div class="step-body">Keep the won salary and living-cost account as it is.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">2</div>
    <div class="step-body">Open a foreign currency demand deposit.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">3</div>
    <div class="step-body">Register for whatever preference channel the bank offers, before converting anything.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">4</div>
    <div class="step-body">Decide what share of assets you want in foreign currency, as a number.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">5</div>
    <div class="step-body">Convert in tranches rather than in one transaction.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">6</div>
    <div class="step-body">Leave currency you will spend soon in the demand deposit.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">7</div>
    <div class="step-body">Put currency with no near-term use into time deposits, laddered by maturity.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">8</div>
    <div class="step-body">Review rate and currency level at each maturity rather than rolling automatically.</div></div>
</div>

Money you will need for won living costs in the near future does not
belong in a foreign currency time deposit. Living expenses in won,
money earmarked for overseas spending in a foreign currency demand
deposit, and currency with no scheduled use in time deposits is the
division that survives contact with an actual month.

A sole proprietor should keep personal and business foreign currency
accounts clearly apart, and a company should never run overseas purchase
or export proceeds through the same account as the representative
director's personal currency holdings.

And the moment the currency actually leaves Korea, this stops being a
deposit question and becomes a foreign exchange payment one —
[which is a different procedure entirely](/banking/how-to-send-money-from-korea-abroad/).
