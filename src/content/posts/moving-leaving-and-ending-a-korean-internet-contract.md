Two events end most Korean internet contracts early, and both are handled badly
for the same reason: people treat them as cancellations when the contract has a
procedure for them.

Moving house is a **transfer**. Leaving the country is a **waiver question with
evidence attached**. Neither is "phone up and cancel".

<p class="hand">The contract has a route for both. Use it before reaching for cancellation.</p>

## Moving is a relocation, not an ending

If the carrier can serve your new address, the standard route is a **change of
installation location**. The contract continues; the term keeps running.

A technician visit normally carries a dispatch charge, and carriers apply
surcharges for weekends, public holidays and evening appointments — KT does
so on its current terms.

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">Do not price this from an article</p>
  <p>Relocation charges are not a national tariff. They differ by carrier, by
  product and by when you signed, and some products discount the second visit
  when internet and TV are moved on the same day. Ask for the combined figure:
  internet relocation, TV relocation, any weekend surcharge, and anything for the
  router.</p>
</div>

Book it early. Several days of lead time is the difference between arriving at a
connected flat and spending a fortnight tethering.

## When the new address genuinely cannot be served

Where the carrier cannot provide service at the address, or relocation is
technically impossible, a **waiver of the clawback** may be available under the
terms.

The test is not preference:

```
"I do not want internet at the new place"      not a ground
"the carrier cannot provide service here"      the ground
```

If a technician attends and finds installation impossible, **get that recorded**
— the finding, the date, the job reference. That record is what a waiver
assessment runs on.

## A landlord's refusal is a different fact

This is where foreign residents most often get stuck, in studios, officetels and
older apartments.

A landlord or building office refusing external drilling or new cabling is a
real obstacle. It is not automatically the same as the carrier being unable to
serve the building.

<div class="steps">
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">1</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Can the existing wiring be used</strong> instead of new cabling?</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">2</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Is another line type available</strong> at the address?</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">3</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Is the building tied to one provider</strong>, and does that provider serve it?</div></div>
</div>

<div class="callout callout-note">
  <p class="callout-title">Ask the question before signing the lease, not after</p>
  <p>"Which carriers can be installed here, and is new cabling permitted" belongs
  in the viewing conversation. It costs nothing then and is a contract dispute
  later.</p>
</div>

## The documents, adjusted for actually being foreign

A carrier assessing a relocation or a waiver wants evidence of the situation.
Korean customers are usually asked for a resident registration extract; you will
not have one.

Keep instead:

- residence information showing the new address
- the lease for the new place
- the relocation order or job reference
- the technician's record where installation was found impossible

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">"You need a resident registration extract" is a script</p>
  <p>It is the default document for a Korean customer, not a legal requirement
  you cannot satisfy. Say what you hold and ask what is acceptable in its place —
  the question the carrier is answering is whether the move happened, not which
  Korean form proves it.</p>
</div>

## Equipment comes back

Modems, ONTs, routers, set-top boxes, remotes and power adapters generally
belong to the carrier. On termination they are collected or returned by a
specified method, and loss or late return attracts equipment charges.

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">Which is what makes a last-week cancellation fail</p>
  <p>Collection has to be scheduled while you and the equipment are both still in
  the country. A box left with a landlord, or in a flat you have handed back, is
  a charge. Put the collection date in the calendar before the flight, not after
  the final bill.</p>
</div>

## Leaving Korea is not an automatic exit

Returning home does not by itself extinguish the contract. Whether emigration or
departure is a recognised ground for waiver or reduction depends on the
carrier's terms, and evidence is normally required — flight documents, proof of
departure, visa expiry, relocation paperwork. Consumer dispute material treats
emigration as a ground recognised in carriers' own terms on production of
evidence, which is precisely the point: it comes from the terms, not from the
fact of flying.

So the timeline is not the last week:

<div class="steps">
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">1</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>One to two months out</strong> — read the terms, ask what waiver grounds exist and what evidence is required.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">2</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Get today's clawback figure</strong>, itemised, so you know what you are arguing about.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">3</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Submit the evidence</strong> and get the decision before you book the equipment collection.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">4</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Schedule collection</strong> with room to spare before the flight.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">5</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Keep a payment method and a contactable address alive</strong> until the final bill has actually settled.</div></div>
</div>

This is the argument for not being in a three-year contract in the first place
if your stay is uncertain — including the
[prepaid product sold to foreign nationals](/tracked/foreigner-prepaid-internet-terms/)
in six or twelve month terms, with nothing to settle at the end. The full
comparison is in
[Korean home internet: the 36-month total](/cost-of-living/korean-home-internet-the-36-month-total/).

## Reaching the end of the term is not the end of the service

Thirty-six months elapsing does not stop the service or the billing. You keep
the internet and you keep paying — usually at a higher post-discount rate.

<div class="callout callout-note">
  <p class="callout-title">But continuing is not re-contracting</p>
  <p>Service continuing and a new three-year term beginning are different things.
  A fresh commitment needs your agreement and its own conditions, so "it renewed
  itself for another three years" is not how it works — though drifting on at the
  undiscounted rate for a year is entirely possible, and common.</p>
</div>

At expiry, get two quotes at once: your carrier's renewal terms, and a
competitor's new-customer offer. Compare them on the whole term rather than the
gift:

```
36 months of charges
  − bundle discounts
  + installation and dispatch
  + equipment rental
  − signup benefit
```

And if a rival's offer requires moving family mobile lines to get the bundle,
put those bills in the calculation too.

## What to keep, from day one

- the application form and the gift terms
- the [contract term](/tracked/internet-contract-term-options/) and the
  no-contract price for the same speed
- a list of the equipment installed
- the relocation and termination sections of the terms

The most expensive number in a Korean internet contract is not the cash at
signup. It is what you pay to get out of a term you were never going to
complete — and both of the events that trigger it have a procedure that costs
less than cancelling does.
