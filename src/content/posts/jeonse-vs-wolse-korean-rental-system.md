Understanding jeonse (전세) as "rent without monthly payments" captures half
the structure and misses the half that matters. Financially, jeonse is a
transaction in which the tenant provides the landlord a large sum of money
for years, and receives the right to live in the property in return.

Pay a ₩500,000,000 jeonse deposit with no monthly rent and you have, in
substance, extended the landlord a ₩500M **interest-free loan**. Unlike an
ordinary loan, your recovery mechanisms are possession of the home,
opposing power, priority repayment rights, and — optionally — a return
guarantee. But the analytical frame is a lender's frame: the cost of tying
up ₩500M, and the certainty of getting ₩500M back at the end.

That frame — deposit as credit exposure — organizes everything below.

## Jeonse is the landlord's leverage

The landlord can deploy your deposit freely: repaying other debt, buying
the next property, funding a business. No interest accrues to you, which
makes jeonse one of the most powerful financing tools a Korean property
owner has.

Example: a landlord acquires an ₩800M apartment with ₩300M of their own
equity and your ₩500M jeonse deposit. They control an ₩800M asset with
₩300M — you supplied 62.5% of the financing. Korea's *gap investment*
(갭투자) strategy pushes this to the extreme: the smaller the gap between
purchase price and jeonse price, the less equity the landlord needs and
the higher their return on equity — and, symmetrically, the faster losses
compound when prices fall.

The tenant-side corollary: a home whose jeonse price sits very close to
its market price is **not** automatically a good deal. It means your
deposit is financing most of the building — with all that implies for
recovery if things go wrong.

## The conversion rate is the price of money embedded in your deposit

The standard tool for comparing jeonse and wolse (월세, monthly rent) is
the **deposit-to-rent conversion rate**:

> Conversion rate = additional annual rent ÷ reduction in deposit

If cutting the deposit by ₩300M raises rent by ₩1,000,000/month —
₩12,000,000/year — the conversion rate is **4%**. Handing the landlord
₩300M more "earns" you ₩12M of avoided rent annually. So if your own
after-tax return on ₩300M exceeds 4%, reducing the deposit and paying rent
is the capital-efficient choice; if you can only safely earn 2–3%, the
larger deposit wins.

Note the legal layer: when converting deposit into rent within an existing
lease, the Housing Lease Protection Act caps the applicable rate at the
lower of a presidential-decree rate or the Bank of Korea base rate plus a
decreed spread. That statutory cap is tenant protection — it is *not* the
market conversion rate, which forms separately by region, housing type,
and supply.

## Compare jeonse and wolse with opportunity cost included

For anyone with meaningful assets — and certainly for a company CEO — the
deposit belongs in the asset-allocation column, not the housing-expense
column.

Compare a ₩500M jeonse against ₩100M deposit + ₩1.5M/month rent. Choosing
wolse frees ₩400M; annual rent is ₩18M, so the implied conversion rate on
that ₩400M is 4.5%. Now the decision is a one-line test:

- Deploy the freed ₩400M at 6% after tax → ₩24M/year. Pay ₩18M rent,
  keep ~₩6M. **Wolse wins.**
- Park it in deposits at 3% → ₩12M/year against ₩18M rent. **Jeonse
  wins.**

> Annual rent **<** after-tax return on the freed deposit → wolse
> Annual rent **>** after-tax return on the freed deposit → jeonse

One discipline: do not plug an 8% equity-market "expected" return into
this formula as if it were certain. The deposit is money you *will* need
back on a fixed date — benchmark it against assets of comparable
liquidity and risk, not against your most optimistic portfolio.

## Borrowed deposits change the benchmark to your loan rate

If the deposit comes from a jeonse loan rather than your own capital, the
comparison simplifies. Borrow ₩300M at 4.5% and your financing cost is
₩13.5M/year; if removing that ₩300M from the deposit costs ₩12M/year in
extra rent, then **on interest alone, wolse is cheaper** — before adding
guarantee fees and loan costs, which widen the gap.

Two caveats keep this honest. Policy and guarantee-backed jeonse loans
sometimes price below market — use your *actual* rate. And every borrowed
won feeds your DSR, shaping future mortgage and credit-loan capacity; for
foreign borrowers, the income, status, and guarantee-institution layers
covered in [our loans guide](/loans/can-foreigners-get-a-loan-in-korea/)
apply on top. Run payment scenarios in the
[loan calculator](/tools/loan-calculator/) before committing.

## The dominant risk is not price risk — it is counterparty credit

Because the deposit "comes back at the end," it feels like cash. It is
not. It is a **claim against your landlord**.

Suppose the home is worth ₩600M with a ₩100M senior mortgage and your
₩400M deposit — ₩100M of apparent cushion. If a market downturn drops the
auction price to ₩450M, and senior claims plus execution costs are paid
first, your recovery shrinks fast. So the number to compute is not the
jeonse-to-price ratio but:

> **Real collateral cushion = conservative disposal value − senior liens −
> senior tenants' deposits and other priority claims**

That figure needs to exceed your deposit with room to spare.

Understand also the machine behind **reverse jeonse** (역전세): many
landlords repay departing tenants with the *next* tenant's deposit. When
jeonse prices fall ₩100M, the landlord must produce ₩100M of new cash to
bridge the gap. No cash → delayed return; multiple debts → you are now a
creditor in a property auction.

## Opposing power and priority rights are filings, not contract clauses

Under the Housing Lease Protection Act, an ordinary tenant gains
**opposing power** (대항력) against third parties from the day *after*
taking actual delivery of the home and completing resident registration —
move-in reporting counts as resident registration. **Priority repayment**
(우선변제권) additionally requires a **fixed date** (확정일자) on the
contract, obtainable at community centers, courts, and registries.

For foreigners the mechanics route through the immigration system:
alien registration and **stay-address change reporting** stand in for
resident registration. Which creates a specifically foreign failure mode —
physically moving in but processing the stay-address change late. With
hundreds of millions of won at stake, a few days of administrative lag is
not a small thing: protection runs from the day after the requirements are
met, and an intervening lien beats you. File the address change and get
the fixed date **immediately**, together, at move-in.

## A return guarantee transfers landlord credit risk to an institution

A jeonse deposit-return guarantee (전세보증금반환보증) works like this: if
the landlord fails to return the deposit at the end, the guarantee
institution pays you within the covered scope and then pursues the
landlord. Priced correctly, the fee is not an annoyance — it is the cost
of moving a single, concentrated, hundreds-of-millions-of-won credit
exposure off your balance sheet.

The institutional landscape matters enormously for foreigners:

- **HUG** (Korea Housing & Urban Guarantee): the standard return
  guarantee covers deposits up to **₩700M in the capital region and ₩500M
  elsewhere**, with application generally required before half the lease
  term has passed. Critically, HUG **explicitly accepts foreign and
  corporate tenants** — foreigners submit extra documents such as the
  alien-registration certificate.
- **HF** (Korea Housing Finance Corporation): its Jeonse Jikim guarantee
  currently requires **all tenants to be Korean nationals**. Do not treat
  HF and HUG products as interchangeable.
- **SGI** and private guarantees apply their own underwriting — match the
  product to your nationality, status, housing type, and deposit before
  signing anything.

And guarantees are underwritten on the **property**, not on you: deposits
over the cap, excessive senior liens, deposit-to-value ratios beyond the
standard, or registry defects can block enrollment. Multi-household
buildings (다가구) are the hardest case, because other tenants' deposits
and fixed dates in the same building affect your recovery — HUG requires
disclosure of the building's other lease contracts and fixed-date records
for exactly this reason. The correct sequence is therefore: **confirm
guarantee insurability first, then sign** — not the reverse.

## Corporate leases are capital allocation, not housing policy

Companies — including foreign-invested ones — often lease housing for
executives and staff. HUG's standard guarantee does include corporate
tenants (with corporate documentation and contract-form review), but the
finance runs differently from a household's.

A ₩500M corporate deposit converts ₩500M of cash into a long-duration
receivable. It never hits the income statement as an expense, but working
capital drops all the same — and if the company borrows at 5%, that
deposit silently costs ₩25M a year in capital charges. Compare
low-deposit wolse arrangements using the company's WACC or actual
borrowing rate, not just the monthly rent line. For a CEO, jeonse is less
a tax question than a
[capital-allocation question](/business/registering-a-business-in-korea-as-a-foreigner/).

## Short stays: expensive-looking wolse is often the rational choice

For a D-2 student or a one-to-two-year assignee, running a huge jeonse
deposit is rarely efficient. Add up the remittance and FX costs of moving
the deposit in, brokerage fees, moving costs, and the risk of a delayed
return, and paying higher monthly rent while keeping capital liquid is
frequently the better trade.

There is also a currency layer settled residents forget: convert
USD 300,000 to won for a deposit and back to dollars two years later, and
your housing cost now includes the KRW/USD move over the period — the
double-conversion problem from our
[money-management guide](/cost-of-living/managing-money-in-korea-fx-remittance-cards/).
(Run the monthly side of the budget in the

## Long-term settlers should put buying in the same equation

Once Korea is home for the long haul — F-4, F-5, F-6 — the jeonse-vs-wolse
frame becomes too narrow. When jeonse prices reach 70–80% of purchase
prices, buying with additional equity plus a mortgage joins the comparison
set. Purchase carries acquisition tax, property tax, transaction costs,
and price risk; but years of rent, and the opportunity cost of an enormous
deposit, are costs too. For households, LTV, DSR, spousal income, and
household ownership status all interact — a topic that deserves its own
guide in the family-finance series.

The full comparison, for any structure, is one formula:

> **Housing cost = nominal rent + financing cost + opportunity cost of
> capital + transaction costs + expected credit loss**

Leave the deposit's opportunity cost and credit risk out of that formula
and jeonse always looks cheaper than wolse. Put them in and the answer
depends — on your return on capital, your loan rate, your horizon, and
your landlord's balance sheet.

Korean jeonse is a lease and a loan at the same time. The tenant is both
the resident of the home and a creditor who has financed a large part of
it. What deserves scrutiny before signing is not the few hundred thousand
won of monthly difference — it is the **yield and recoverability of the
loan you are about to make.**
