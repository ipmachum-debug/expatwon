The extended work premium and the night work premium attach to separate grounds, and where both apply to the same hour both are added: 100% base, plus 50% for extended work, plus 50% for night work. Payroll that treats the two as alternatives and pays 150% underpays every night shift, and a payslip showing a single "overtime" line will not reveal it. Note the window as well — night work runs from 22:00 to 06:00, not to 05:00, and the last hour is the one most often dropped.'
  - question: 'I worked ten hours on a holiday. Is that 250%?'
    answer: 'Not on that reasoning. Holiday work is regulated on its own two-tier basis: the first eight hours at 150% and the part beyond eight hours at 200%. Adding a further 50% for extended work on top of the beyond-eight-hours tier double-counts, because the statute has already set the higher rate for exactly those hours. Where the extra hours fall between 22:00 and 06:00, however, the night premium does apply on top — and that is the route by which holiday work beyond eight hours reaches 250%.'
  - question: 'My contract says my salary includes all overtime. Does that end the matter?'

In an office with access records, PC logs and a working hours system, the premise usually fails. Premium pay in Korea is not one rate with exceptions. It is three separate
grounds — extended work, night work, holiday work — that combine according to
rules people routinely get wrong in both directions: paying too little for a
night shift, and too much for a long holiday.

Underneath all of them sits ordinary wage, which is not base pay and which
moved in 2024.

## Extended work: 50% added

Article 56 requires at least
[50% of ordinary wage](/tracked/overtime-premium-rate/) added for statutory
extended work.

At an hourly ordinary wage of ₩15,000, two hours of weekday extended work:

```
base       ₩15,000 × 2          = ₩30,000
premium    ₩15,000 × 2 × 50%    = ₩15,000
                                  ────────
                                   ₩45,000
```

Or, as it is normally computed, ₩15,000 × 2 × 150%.

## Night work: 22:00 to 06:00

The night window is
[22:00 to 06:00](/tracked/night-work-time-window/) the following day, and it
is stated in the statute rather than derived.

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">22:00 to 05:00 is the single most common written error</p>
  <p>It appears in a great deal of English-language material about working in
  Korea. It drops an hour of premium from every night worked, and across a
  roster that is not a rounding difference.</p>
</div>

Night work attracts its own 50%. If the hour is inside statutory hours, that
is 150%. If the hour is **also** extended work, both grounds apply:

```
base                    100%
extended work          + 50%
night work             + 50%
                        ────
                        200%
```

Two hours of extended work after 22:00, at ₩15,000:

```
₩15,000 × 2 × 200%  =  ₩60,000
```

## Holiday work has its own two tiers

Holiday work is regulated separately under Article 56(2), at
[50% within eight hours and 100% beyond](/tracked/holiday-work-premium-rate/).

Which produces the full table:

| Work | Total |
|---|---:|
| Ordinary hours | 100% |
| Extended work | 150% |
| Night work | 150% |
| Extended + night | 200% |
| Holiday, within 8 hours | 150% |
| Holiday, beyond 8 hours | 200% |
| Holiday within 8 hours + night | 200% |
| Holiday beyond 8 hours + night | 250% |

## Do not add an extended premium to holiday work

Ten hours worked on a holiday:

```
first 8 hours     150%
beyond 8 hours    200%
```

Reasoning that the week also passed 40 hours and adding a further 50% to reach
250% does not match how Article 56 handles holiday work. The statute has
already fixed the higher rate for the hours beyond eight — that *is* the
treatment of the excess.

<div class="callout callout-note">
  <p class="callout-title">250% is reachable, by the other route</p>
  <p>If those extra two hours fall between 22:00 and 06:00, the night premium
  applies on top and the figure is 250%. Same number, different reason — and
  the reason is what decides whether it is correct.</p>
</div>

The [overtime pay calculator](/tools/overtime-pay-calculator/) takes the hours
by ground and applies these rates, and checks the cap separately from the pay.

## Ordinary wage is the multiplicand, and it is not base pay

Every rate above is a multiple of ordinary wage, so the scope of ordinary wage
decides more money than any individual rate does.

An en banc Supreme Court decision in 2024 **discarded the fixedness
requirement**, leaving
[regularity and uniformity](/tracked/ordinary-wage-requirements-count/) as the
test. Position allowances, qualification allowances and certain bonuses paid
regularly and uniformly as consideration for contractual work therefore fall to
be reassessed.

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">Guidance written before 2024 states a test that no longer holds</p>
  <p>And nothing on its face says so. An employer computing premiums from base
  pay alone is not automatically correct — it may simply be applying the older
  rule. If your pay has regular monthly allowances outside base pay, that is
  the question to ask.</p>
</div>

## 209 is a common divisor, not a universal one

For a monthly-paid employee on a 40-hour week with eight paid weekly holiday
hours, the hourly ordinary wage is normally the monthly ordinary wage over
[209 conversion hours](/tracked/monthly-standard-hours/):

```
₩3,135,000 ÷ 209  =  ₩15,000
```

But the conversion figure follows the contract. Different contractual hours or
a different paid holiday structure give a different divisor, and using 209
anyway produces an hourly rate that is **too low** — which then understates
every premium computed from it. Where the 209 comes from is in
[Korea's minimum wage and the 209-hour month](/employment/minimum-wage-and-weekly-holiday-pay-korea/).

## Comprehensive wage and fixed overtime are different things

They are used interchangeably in practice and they are not the same.

| | Comprehensive wage | Fixed overtime |
|---|---|---|
| Premise | Measuring working hours is genuinely impractical | Hours are measurable and have been measured |
| Form | Premiums folded into a single amount | A stated number of hours with a stated amount |
| Example | "Salary includes all premiums" | Base ₩3,000,000 + 20 hours' extended work ₩450,000 |

A genuine comprehensive wage arrangement is exceptional. Whether one is
effective is assessed on the whole picture — whether the nature of the work
makes hours genuinely hard to compute, whether there was an actual agreement,
and whether it disadvantages the employee.

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">Writing "comprehensive wage" in the contract does not create one</p>
  <p>In an ordinary office with fixed start and finish times, electronic access
  records, PC session logs and extended hours that are in fact tallied, the
  premise that hours cannot be measured is difficult to sustain. A clause
  asserting that the salary includes every premium is not, on its own, a reason
  to stop calculating.</p>
</div>

## Fixed overtime is a floor, not a ceiling

A contract paying 20 hours of extended work a month:

<div class="steps">
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">1</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Worked 12 hours.</strong> The employer may pay the agreed 20 hours' worth under the arrangement.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">2</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Worked 30 hours.</strong> The fixed 20, plus the statutory premium on the extra 10, falls to be examined.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">3</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Where the statutory figure exceeds what was paid</strong>, the difference is unpaid wages.</div></div>
</div>

So fixed overtime is not an agreement that hours beyond the stated number are
unpaid. And it does not remove the duty to record hours — without the record,
nobody can tell which of the three cases above applies.

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">And none of it touches the 52-hour cap</p>
  <p>Putting 40 hours of extended work into a monthly salary does not authorise
  40 hours of extended work. The weekly limit is still 12. Pay arrangements and
  hours limits are managed separately — the cap side is in
  <a href="/employment/working-hours-and-the-52-hour-week-korea/">the 52-hour week, and what it does not buy</a>.</p>
</div>

## Proving the hours

A claim for unpaid premiums turns on establishing the hours actually worked.
Usable records include attendance and access logs, PC login and logout times,
work messaging, email timestamps, rosters, work reports, and late-night taxi
and meal claims.

Being in the building is not the test on its own, though. What matters is
whether the time was spent working, or waiting for work, under the employer's
direction — which is the same standard the morning guide applies to the cap.

## The sequence

```
ordinary headcount
  → contractual hours, and hours actually worked
  → which hours are extended, night, holiday
  → ordinary wage, on the current test
  → hourly ordinary wage, on the right divisor
  → statutory premium for each ground
  → less fixed overtime already paid
  → the difference
```

Unpaid premiums are a wage claim: raise it with the employer, then with the
local labour office, and the Ministry's counselling line is **1350**. How the
components should have appeared in the first place is in
[reading your Korean payslip](/employment/reading-your-korean-payslip/), and
what the contract should have said about hours and fixed overtime is in
[your Korean employment contract](/employment/your-korean-employment-contract-what-to-check/).

The sentence to stop accepting is "we are a comprehensive wage company, so
there is no overtime pay." Whether such an arrangement exists, whether it is
effective, whether the hours can be measured, whether the amount falls short of
the statutory figure, and whether the cap was respected are five separate
questions with five separate answers.
