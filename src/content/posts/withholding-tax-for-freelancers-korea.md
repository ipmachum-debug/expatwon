You hire a freelance designer for ₩2,000,000. The work is done, the invoice
arrives, you transfer ₩1,934,000 and hold back ₩66,000 for tax.

The freelancer has been paid. Your obligations have just started.

That ₩66,000 is not a discount, a commission or extra margin — it is the
recipient's tax, which the payer has to report and remit. And the payment
creates income-reporting duties that are separate from paying the tax.

A business can deduct 3.3% perfectly and still miss its reporting. It can file
the payment statement on time and miss the tax deadline. Or it can apply 3.3%
to someone who should have been an employee, which creates problems that
outlive the tax question entirely.

**The first question is not how much to deduct. It is whether you are required
to deduct anything from this particular payment.**

## Three obligations, three deadlines

For ordinary qualifying personal-service business income paid by a Korean
withholding agent:

| Obligation | General rule |
|---|---|
| Withhold | When the income is paid |
| Report and remit national withholding tax | By the 10th of the following month |
| Submit the simplified payment statement | By the end of the following month |

The individual local income tax has to go through its own local filing. An
approved semiannual withholding filer pays on a different schedule — **and the
monthly statement deadline does not move with it.**

The common failure is treating withholding, paying and reporting as one act.
They are three.

## Who has to withhold

The duty falls on the payer making a payment inside the statutory rules — the
withholding agent, 원천징수의무자. The NTS includes corporations, individual
business operators and certain other payers, but the duty depends on the
income category.

One exception matters more than the rest: the NTS explains that **an ordinary
private individual not operating a business is not required to withhold
business-income tax** merely because they paid someone business income.

So:

- A Korean company paying a qualifying independent instructor generally
  withholds.
- A **registered sole proprietor** paying a qualifying personal-service
  provider can also be a withholding agent — not being incorporated does not
  exempt you.
- A genuine non-business individual paying for a personal service falls inside
  that exception.

In the third case the recipient still has taxable income. **A person can owe
tax on income even when the payer had no duty to withhold it.**

## 3.3% belongs to a specific kind of income

| Component | Rate |
|---|---:|
| National income tax | 3.0% |
| Individual local income tax | 0.3% |
| **Combined** | **3.3%** |

The NTS describes this business-income withholding as applying to prescribed
income from VAT-exempt personal services and certain medical and
health-related services. For the monthly statement system it describes
independent personal-service earners as people who provide services
continuously and independently, without an employment relationship or business
facilities, paid for the results of their work.

That covers particular prescribed categories. It does not establish that
everyone paid for a service is a 3.3% case. The payment may instead be
employment income, occasional other income, an ordinary VAT-taxable business
service, or a payment to a non-resident under different provisions.

Get the classification wrong and the payer files the wrong return, the wrong
statement, and reports the recipient's income incorrectly.

## The arithmetic, on ₩2,000,000

```
₩2,000,000 × 3%   = ₩60,000   national
₩2,000,000 × 0.3% =  ₩6,000   local
                    ₩66,000   withheld
₩2,000,000 − ₩66,000 = ₩1,934,000 transferred
```

The recipient got ₩1,934,000 in cash. The gross service compensation is
₩2,000,000. The difference is owed to two tax authorities.

Do not treat the ₩66,000 as an extra cost on top of the ₩2 million because it
leaves the bank separately. The ₩2 million is the compensation; it is simply
split between the recipient and the tax authorities.

## The withheld money is not yours

Ten qualifying payments of ₩2 million:

| | Total |
|---|---:|
| Gross contractor payments | ₩20,000,000 |
| Net transfers | ₩19,340,000 |
| National tax withheld | ₩600,000 |
| Local tax withheld | ₩60,000 |
| **Combined withholding** | **₩660,000** |

The total cash needed is still ₩20 million. The operational failure happens
when the net transfers go out through the bank and the withheld ₩660,000 is
never reserved against its deadlines. For a few weeks the account looks like
it has spare money in it. It does not.

**Withheld tax is a liability to settle, not a reduction in what the service
cost.**

## "Freelancer" is a way of working, not a tax class

| Actual arrangement | What to examine |
|---|---|
| Continuing independent personal services in prescribed categories | Business-income withholding, commonly 3.3% with local tax |
| Qualifying occasional lecture or advisory payment | Other income; 8.8% may apply under specified conditions |
| Genuine employment relationship | Employment-income withholding and payroll settlement |
| Ordinary VAT-taxable business service | Invoice and VAT rules — not automatic 3.3% |
| Payment to a foreign non-resident | Source rules and treaty provisions |

Three designers can all call themselves freelancers: one working
independently for several companies inside the prescribed categories, one
running a registered VAT-taxable business, one working under daily
supervision. The word is identical and the withholding procedure is not. The
payer has to look at the actual contractual and working arrangement.

## One-off work can be other income instead

The NTS treats certain occasional personal-service payments — qualifying
lectures, advisory work — as other income (기타소득). For specified
other-income payments the calculation can allow **deemed necessary expenses of
60%** of the gross, leaving 40% as the income amount, with a 20% national rate
on that. Twenty per cent of forty per cent is an effective 8% on the gross,
and the local component produces the familiar 8.8%.

On a ₩500,000 qualifying lecture fee:

```
₩500,000 × 60% = ₩300,000   deemed expenses
₩500,000 − ₩300,000 = ₩200,000   other-income amount
₩200,000 × 20% = ₩40,000   national
                  ₩4,000   local
                 ₩44,000   withheld  →  ₩456,000 net
```

Processed incorrectly as 3.3% business income, the withholding would have been
₩16,500. The gap is ₩27,500, and it is a consequence of the classification —
not a rate the payer may pick because the recipient prefers it.

⚠️ 8.8% is not a universal rate for every occasional payment or every kind of
other income.

## A small payment is not automatically tax-free

There is a minimum taxable amount for certain other income. On a qualifying
occasional service with 60% deemed expenses, a gross ₩125,000 leaves an
other-income amount of ₩50,000, and under the applicable rule no income tax is
imposed on that example. Above ₩125,000 the same category moves past it.

That is not a general ₩125,000 exemption for service fees. And a separate rule
cuts the other way: the NTS explains that for relevant payments **from 1 July
2024, withholding on resident personal-service business income applies even
where the calculated national amount is under ₩1,000.** So the ordinary
small-withholding non-collection rule should not be applied to 3.3%
personal-service payments.

**Small payments need correct classification, not assumed exemption.**

## Withholding 3.3% does not make someone a contractor

Someone works at your premises every weekday. You set the schedule, supervise
the tasks, supply the equipment and direct how the work is done. They are paid
a fixed monthly amount. The contract says freelancer and you withhold 3.3%.

None of that settles it. The real relationship is assessed under the labour
and tax rules, and a contract title or a withholding rate is not conclusive.
Where it is genuinely employment, employment-income withholding, payroll
duties, labour law and social insurance follow — and the problem cannot be
fixed by getting the worker to agree the rate is 3.3%. For a foreign worker,
immigration permission is a separate test again: a correct tax filing does not
authorise work outside a status of stay. That territory is
[freelancing in Korea legally](/business/freelancing-in-korea-legally/).

For a company hiring contractors the immediate lesson is narrower: **do not
classify payroll by payment rate.**

## A registered business is not automatically a 3.3% contractor

Buying website development from a registered business that issues a tax
invoice is not a reason to deduct 3.3% because an individual did the work. An
ordinary purchase of taxable business services runs through invoices and VAT
reporting instead.

Equally, a business registration number does not settle the question on its
own — some registered operators provide services that fall under particular
withholding rules. Before applying 3.3%, establish who is legally providing
the service, whether the recipient is an individual or an entity, what the
service actually is, whether it falls in a prescribed withholding category,
whether VAT invoicing applies, and the recipient's tax status.

A tax invoice and a personal-service withholding receipt are not
interchangeable, and the same transaction should not be run as both without
establishing which is correct.

## Nationality does not set the rate

A foreign national living in Korea may be a Korean tax resident, determined
under the tax rules — a passport and a residence card are not conclusive.
Where a foreign resident receives qualifying personal-service business income,
the ordinary resident rules generally apply, and holding a foreign passport
does not produce a different rate.

A payment to a **non-resident** is a different analysis: Korean-source rules,
the non-resident withholding provisions and any treaty. A contract with a
foreign consultant does not fall automatically under the resident 3.3%
procedure, and where the work is performed matters. An overseas bank account
does not establish non-residence either.

**Do not substitute nationality or bank location for residence and source
analysis** — the test is in [income tax for foreigners: resident or
not](/business/income-tax-for-foreigners-resident-or-not/).

## The clock starts at payment, not at delivery

Withholding generally happens when the income is paid; certain categories have
special deemed-payment rules. For ordinary personal-service business income
outside those exceptions, the **actual payment date** drives the reporting
period.

Work finished in October, statement received 28 October, payment made 5
November: the withholding belongs to November. That is not the same as
assuming every October service is an October withholding.

Keep both dates — the period the service relates to, and the date it was paid.
They do different jobs. For certain year-end-settled business-income
categories the NTS sets statutory deemed-payment dates; handle those
separately rather than applying them to everyone.

## The tax: by the 10th

Withholding tax on qualifying business income is ordinarily reported and paid
by the 10th of the following month, on the 원천징수이행상황신고서. A payment
made on 20 October 2026 is due 10 November 2026.

The individual local income tax special withholding goes through its own local
filing and payment. **A single national payment does not settle the local
component.** Holidays and statutory rules can shift actual dates, so check the
calendar for the period.

## Semiannual approval exists, and is narrower than it sounds

Korea allows approved semiannual withholding for qualifying agents — the NTS
identifies a system for certain businesses with no more than 20 regular
employees on the relevant prior-year calculation. It is **approval-based**,
applied for in June or December for the following half-year, and decided by
the competent tax office. An approved agent generally pays by the 10th of the
month after the half-year period.

What it does not do is make the reporting semiannual. The monthly simplified
statements for resident personal-service business income keep their own
timetable. **Approval to pay tax semiannually is not permission to skip
monthly income reporting** — and that distinction is exactly what a business
using an accountant twice a year is most likely to miss.

## The statement: by the end of the month

The form is 간이지급명세서(거주자의 사업소득), and the NTS states it is
generally submitted monthly, by the last day of the month following payment.

For an October 2026 payment:

```
national withholding tax   →  10 November 2026
simplified statement       →  30 November 2026
```

Two deadlines, two purposes. The first reports and remits tax. The second
reports income information about the recipient. **Filing the statement does
not pay the tax, and paying the tax does not file the statement** — a business
can complete one and miss the other without noticing.

## One payment, three pieces of work

A ₩3,000,000 payment in October: ₩90,000 national, ₩9,000 local, ₩2,901,000
transferred.

| Task | What it carries |
|---|---|
| National withholding return and payment | ₩90,000 |
| Local income tax special withholding | ₩9,000 |
| Monthly simplified payment statement | The recipient, and ₩3,000,000 gross |

The statement is not a second charge — it reports information. The withholding
return is not a list of bank transfers. The figures should reconcile, but the
documents are not substitutes for each other. Checking that ₩2,901,000 left
the account verifies none of the three.

## The March statement has an exception people miss

Businesses long associated contractor payments with an annual business-income
payment statement due 10 March. That deadline still matters where an annual
statement is required.

But the current NTS guidance exempts a payer from the separate annual resident
business-income statement **where all the required monthly simplified
statements were submitted** — which is the point of the monthly system. The
exception to the exception is certain year-end-settled categories: the NTS
names insurance agents, door-to-door salespeople and beverage delivery
workers, for which both monthly information and the year-end-settlement
statement may be needed.

So the question is not "do I file one annual statement in March". It is: which
category did I pay, did I submit the monthly statements, and does the
exemption apply? **Do not duplicate or omit the annual statement because a
software checklist still runs on the old schedule.**

## Three contractors, one October

| Recipient | Gross | National | Local | Net |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| A | ₩2,000,000 | ₩60,000 | ₩6,000 | ₩1,934,000 |
| B | ₩1,200,000 | ₩36,000 | ₩3,600 | ₩1,160,400 |
| C | ₩3,000,000 | ₩90,000 | ₩9,000 | ₩2,901,000 |
| **Total** | **₩6,200,000** | **₩186,000** | **₩18,600** | **₩5,995,400** |

₩204,600 of combined withholding against ₩5,995,400 transferred. National tax
to 10 November, statements to 30 November, local on its own process.

Reconcile the recipients' gross against the payment ledger. Report only the
net transfers and the statements understate the payments; report ₩6,200,000
gross while remitting withholding computed on a different set of transactions
and nothing ties out. **One ledger should support both the tax filing and the
recipient-level information.**

## What to record

For ordinary resident personal-service business income: the recipient's legal
identity and taxpayer information, the service type and income classification,
the service period where relevant, the actual payment date, the gross amount,
national and local withholding, and the resulting net transfer.

Collect identifying information only through a lawful process and keep it to
the people who need it — a reporting obligation does not justify unrelated
sensitive data, and identifying numbers do not belong in unsecured
spreadsheets. Wrong identification hurts both sides: your statement fails to
match the actual recipient, and the recipient then cannot reconcile the income
against their own records.

## The receipt you owe them

A withholding agent paying business income subject to withholding must issue
the business-income withholding receipt, 사업소득 원천징수영수증. It is not the
employment-income receipt.

A freelancer should not be handed an employment receipt because your software
has a payroll module, and an employee should not be handed a business-income
receipt because someone used a 3.3% calculation. **The document follows the
income classification.** The recipient needs it for their annual return and to
check reported income — how to read one is [your Korean withholding tax
receipt, explained](/business/korean-withholding-tax-receipt-explained/).

## The transfer must reconcile to the gross

Recording only what left the bank is the most common accounting error. On a
₩2 million service payment the transfer is ₩1,934,000; if the contractor
expense ledger also says ₩1,934,000, the gross compensation is understated.

Conceptually: a ₩2,000,000 service expense, ₩1,934,000 payable to the
contractor, ₩60,000 national and ₩6,000 local withholding liabilities. That is
an illustration, not a mandatory journal format — the recognition point and
account structure follow the circumstances and your accounting policy.

Paying the taxes later settles those liabilities. It does not buy more
services. **If the expense record, the contractor statement and the tax
payment cannot be reconciled to one gross figure, the problem is the records.**

## When the wrong amount was withheld

Suppose ₩66,000 should have been withheld and the full ₩2 million went out
instead. Paying the contractor everything does not extinguish the obligation —
the NTS explains that withholding agents can be liable for tax that should
have been withheld and paid. What follows depends on the transaction, the
contract and the rules, including whether the contract allows recovery from
the recipient.

Withholding correctly but remitting too little is a different problem. So is
reporting the wrong income classification. Before fixing anything, establish
what should legally have been withheld, what actually was, what reached the
recipient, what was reported and remitted, what statement was filed, and which
corrections are required.

Correcting the bank payment may not fix the tax records. Correcting the return
may not fix the statement.

## Two kinds of penalty, on two different things

**Unpaid tax.** The NTS penalises a withholding agent that fails to pay
required withholding tax by the deadline or pays short, on a calculation
involving an initial percentage, time-based components and statutory limits.
Its guidance was **updated for rules applicable from 1 July 2026**, and the
amount can turn on the dates, on whether a notice or collection step has
happened, and on the national and local provisions. Do not copy an older
formula into a current filing without checking its effective date. Local
special withholding has its own penalty rules.

**Missing statements.** For resident personal-service business income the NTS
identifies a general failure-to-submit penalty of **0.25%** of the affected
payment, reduced to **0.125%** where a qualifying late statement arrives
within one month, with further rules for incomplete or incorrect statements.
On ₩10 million of unreported payments that is ₩25,000, or ₩12,500 with the
reduction.

The NTS also has rules against applying certain penalties twice where annual
and monthly statements overlap, and the annual exemption above bears on it. So
do not stack every possible penalty — which one applies depends on what
information was required, what was filed, and which relief provisions are
available.

**Deducting the tax correctly is not enough. It has to reach the authority.**

## Where the payer's job ends

After withholding and reporting correctly, you may be asked whether the
freelancer gets a refund in May. You cannot know. Their final tax depends on
their income, expenses, other income, deductions and credits across every
payer — you only see your own payments. Withholding ₩330,000 from someone who
earned ₩10 million through you says nothing about the nine other clients.

Provide accurate records. Do not promise a refund. Whether they are filing at
all is [May filing in Korea: who has to, and what it
costs](/business/may-global-income-tax-filing-for-foreigners/), and if they
run their own business the income side is [income tax for foreign sole
proprietors](/business/korean-income-tax-for-foreign-sole-proprietors/).

## One payment, five records that must agree

The contract says ₩2,000,000. The payment approval says ₩2,000,000. The bank
transfer says ₩1,934,000. The withholding calculation says ₩60,000 and
₩6,000. The payment statement says gross ₩2,000,000.

Each describes a different part of one payment. If the ledger says ₩2,000,000
and the statement says ₩1,934,000, they do not reconcile. If the statement
reports two payments of ₩2,000,000 against one actual payment, the recipient's
income is overstated. If ₩66,000 was withheld and ₩60,000 remitted, the local
obligation is still open. A system worth using makes those mismatches visible.

## Duplicates distort someone else's tax return

A company pays a freelancer ₩2,000,000 once. An administrator registers it
manually; later an integration submits the same payment again. The freelancer
earned ₩2,000,000 — but the tax authority may now hold two income records, and
the recipient's annual reconciliation gets harder for no reason.

The fix is not blocking every transaction with the same amount and recipient:
a freelancer can legitimately receive two ₩2,000,000 payments in one month.
The system has to distinguish **duplicate processing of one payment from two
real payments**, which is what transaction identifiers and reporting status are
for. A correction should be linked to what it corrects, not entered as a second
income event.

**Report every real payment once. Do not report every administrative action as
a payment.**

## Wrong identifiers, right arithmetic

Enter an incorrect taxpayer identifier and the amount and the withholding can
still be arithmetically perfect while the authority cannot match the payment
to the intended person. The NTS lists incorrect or missing recipient
information among the things that make a statement incomplete.

Do not resolve it by inventing an identifier or assigning the payment to
someone else. Verify the correct details and use the applicable correction
procedure, with the privacy obligations in mind. For a foreign recipient,
names that differ across the contract, the bank and the tax system make this
harder — establish the legal identity and the right identifier before
reporting.

## A foreign company paying someone in Korea

Not the same procedure. The examples here assume a Korean withholding agent
paying a resident. Where a foreign company without a straightforward Korean
withholding arrangement pays someone working from Korea, the recipient may
still have Korean tax obligations — but it does not follow that the overseas
payer simply applies the Korean 3.3% mechanism.

The payer's legal position, the recipient's residence, the nature and source
of the income and any treaty all bear on it. A Korean business paying a
non-resident contractor faces another set of rules again, and a payment for
consulting is not automatically treated like a payment for software licensing
or IP rights. The commercial description in the contract may not be enough to
determine the treatment.

**3.3% is not a universal international freelancer tax.**

## The monthly sequence

**Before the first payment** — confirm the relationship and income
classification, whether it falls in a withholding category, and the
recipient's identity and reporting details.

**At approval** — fix the gross amount and the actual payment date, and
calculate national and local withholding on the correct rules.

**On payment** — transfer the net, record the tax liabilities, issue the
withholding receipt.

**After** — reconcile gross, net and withholding; remit national withholding
by its deadline; complete the local obligations; file the monthly statement by
its deadline.

**At year end** — check every monthly statement went in, decide whether an
annual statement is required or exempt, and confirm each real payment was
reported once.

The order matters because it keeps apart three things that casual conversation
runs together: deducting the tax, paying the tax, and reporting the income.

## Five questions for whoever files on your behalf

Outsourcing the filing does not outsource the responsibility.

**Which income category did you use?** Employment, business, other — not "they
are a freelancer".

**Why does that rate apply?** The answer should be about the classification.

**Who files and pays the local component?** Both, or only the national return?

**Who submits the monthly simplified statements?** Name the party.

**What completion proof can I see?** Keep the filing confirmations.

None of that requires the owner to compute a return. It establishes who is
accountable for which obligation — because a company can pay for accounting
every month and still find that one required filing sat outside the
engagement. For the employment-side equivalents, see [hiring your first
employee in Korea](/employment/hiring-your-first-employee-in-korea/).

## The bottom line

The 3.3% calculation is the small part. The real question is who has a legal
duty to withhold, and from which kind of income.

A Korean payer handling qualifying resident personal-service business income
generally withholds 3% national and 0.3% local tax. That does not make every
contractor, registered business or foreign consultant the same case —
employment income, occasional other income, VAT-taxable services and

Then the duties continue. The tax must be reported and paid. Recipient-level
information must be submitted on its own timetable. The two deadlines are
different, an approved semiannual arrangement does not touch the monthly
reporting, and a correct bank transfer proves none of it.

**The payer's responsibility does not end when the freelancer has the money.
It ends when the payment has been classified, withheld, remitted, reported and
documented.** What happens to the recipient's final tax afterwards is theirs.
Knowing where that line falls is what stops one freelance payment turning into
several separate compliance problems.
