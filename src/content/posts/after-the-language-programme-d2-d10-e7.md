Finishing a language programme is not the end of a visa. It is the end of
an **activity** — and the status you hold permits that activity and no
other.

There is no single follow-on visa. A degree means D-2. Job-seeking, if you
qualify, means D-10. An actual job in an eligible occupation means E-7 or
another work status. They are three different structures, and — the part
that costs people a semester — **they start at three different times.**

<p class="hand">Course end date and stay expiry date are two different dates. So is the start of the next thing.</p>

## Completion does not invalidate anything

Your permitted stay runs to the date on your card. The course ending does
not change it.

What it does change is what you may lawfully do. D-4 permits language
training. Once that has finished, starting a degree or a job on the same
status is not an option — and under the Immigration Act **a change of
status is a matter of prior permission**, so it has to be granted before
the new activity starts.

```
Finishing the course  →  status stays valid to its expiry
Starting the next thing  →  needs its own status, in advance
```

## The three routes, side by side

| Route | What it is for | The precondition | Start preparing |
|---|---|---|---|
| **D-2** | Junior college, bachelor's, master's, doctorate | Admission and a certificate of admission | While still studying |
| **D-10** | Job-seeking in E-1 to E-7 fields | Degree and points requirements | Several months before completion |
| **E-7** | A specific professional occupation | Eligible occupation, contract, occupation-specific criteria | During hiring negotiations |
| **Leave and reapply** | When in-country change is unavailable or timing breaks | The mission's visa requirements | Before the current stay expires |

The most natural of these is D-4 to D-2. D-10 and E-7 depend far more on
qualifications you hold independently than on having completed a language
course.

## D-4 to D-2: prepared during the course, not after it

A change to D-2 requires **admission first**. The sub-class follows the
programme — D-2-2 for a bachelor's, D-2-3 for a master's, D-2-4 for a
doctorate.

Documents typically include the passport, the registration card, the
change-of-status application, the certificate of admission, proof of
tuition payment, final academic records, proof of residence and financial
evidence.

<div class="callout callout-note">
  <p class="callout-title">Your language-course record follows you</p>
  <p>2026 university guidance for D-4 holders also asks for enrolment or
  completion certificates from the language institute, along with grade and
  attendance records for the whole course. Some universities ask for a written
  explanation where the previous term's attendance fell below 70%. The
  attendance you kept in the language year is not left behind when the
  programme ends.</p>
</div>

**No rule requires the same university.** Finishing a language course in
Seoul and entering a university in Busan, or the reverse, is ordinary. What
matters is meeting the new university's admission requirements and the D-2
conditions — not institutional continuity.

The Korean level generally indicated for Korean-track entry is level 3,
with level 4 for graduation, though departments raise it — see
[TOPIK levels and what they open](/study/topik-levels-and-what-they-open/).

## The attendance question, stated correctly

Attendance during D-4 matters for later assessments. That direction is
right.

**"Below 80% means an automatic refusal of D-2, D-10 or E-7" is not.**
There is no single national cut-off of that kind. Real 2026 university
guidance asks for full-course attendance certificates and handles a term
below 70% with a written explanation — which shows attendance is assessment
material, not that one number decides everything.

What immigration is actually looking at across your record:

- whether the training activity genuinely took place
- whether the purpose of stay was pursued in good faith
- whether there was unauthorised work or false documentation
- whether reporting duties on address and stay period were met

So attendance is best understood as **evidence of how you held the previous
status**, not as a score carried forward into the next one.

## D-10 is not the default next step

This is the most common misreading in the whole sequence.

General job-seeking D-10 is for people seeking work or training in the
fields covered by E-1 through E-7, and as a rule it requires a **bachelor's
degree or above** — a Korean junior-college degree counts among recognised
qualifications — together with the **points requirements**.

Which means:

- Already held a bachelor's or master's before coming, then studied Korean
  on D-4? **D-10 is worth examining.**
- Finished secondary school, then did a language course? **Completing the
  course does not create D-10 eligibility.**

There is also a **points exemption** route for people who obtained a Korean
junior-college degree or above within the last three years and meet a
Korean proficiency standard such as TOPIK level 4 or above.

For D-10 the operative chain is not attendance. It is:

```
degree
  → eligible job-seeking field
  → points, or the exemption
  → a job-seeking plan
```

## D-10's ceiling changed in October 2025

A lot of published guidance still says six-month grants to a two-year
maximum. That was the position **before 29 October 2025**.

The Ministry of Justice reformed general job-seeking D-10 with effect from
that date: **up to one year per grant, to a cumulative maximum of three
years.**

A longer search window did not remove the entry conditions. Education, the
job-seeking field, the plan and the applicant's own circumstances are all
still assessed.

## E-7 is not a contract plus a form

An offer from a Korean employer during your course does not convert into
E-7 by itself.

The occupation has to be one E-7 covers, and you have to meet **that
occupation's** criteria on education, experience and salary. The employer
prepares the contract and business documentation, and depending on the
occupation a recommendation letter, an employment utilisation plan or
further employer conditions may apply.

**"A bachelor's in a related field, or five years' experience" is not the
formula for E-7.** Criteria differ by occupation, and there are separate,
easier structures for graduates of Korean universities — government
guidance for foreign students describes routes where a Korean bachelor's
degree or above does not require the same demonstration of relatedness
between major and occupation. Nor do employer conditions such as national
employment ratios or recommendation letters apply uniformly across every
E-7 occupation.

### Count backwards from the first day of work

If the contract says 1 September and permission arrives on 10 September,
working from the first is not something the contract fixes. **D-4 is not a
professional work status**, so the permitted change has to exist before the
E-7 activity begins.

Settle the occupation code, education and experience, salary, the
employer's foreign-hiring position and any recommendation letter **while
the contract is being drafted**, and set the start date against the
permission timeline. That is the one moment when the date is still easy to
move.

## The gap between expiry and the first day of term

The failure mode in D-2 transfers is the space between the course ending
and the degree starting.

Your D-4 expires in mid-August and the degree begins in September? "I have
been admitted, so I am fine until September" is not how it works.
**Admission and permission to stay are separate administrations.**

The reverse is also wrong: with a D-4 valid to late September, starting a
degree in early September on D-4 is not appropriate either — that is a
different activity.

Work the calendar backwards:

```
current D-4 expiry
  → university application window
  → final admission
  → tuition paid
  → certificate of admission issued
  → D-2 change application
  → permission granted
  → degree programme starts
```

Universities say this repeatedly in their own guidance: the change should
be completed **before the semester starts**.

## In-country change or leave and reapply

| | Change inside Korea | Leave and apply at a mission |
|---|---|---|
| Continuity of stay | Preserved | Interrupted |
| Flights and living costs | Lower | Additional |
| Existing registration | Continues | Re-entry procedure needed |
| Time to fix documents | Bounded by your current stay | Can be rebuilt from abroad |
| Where you wait | In Korea | At the mission |
| When it fits | In-country change available, file complete | Change unavailable, or the timing needs it |

Neither is automatically better. **Not every combination of statuses can be
changed in-country** — some require leaving and obtaining a new visa
abroad. Moving from D-4 into a degree programme is generally a route that
can be handled domestically.

So the first question is not "which is easier" but **"is an in-country
change available for this particular pairing"**. Ask before you plan around
either.

## Two things people get wrong after applying

**A receipt is not a status.** Submitting the application does not grant
the new one. Confirm the permission has been issued, and that your
registration information has been updated, before starting the study or the
job.

**Leaving while it is pending is its own problem.** A trip home in the
break before term, or a business trip before a start date, has to be
checked against the pending application — the processing state and whether
re-entry is possible are not automatic. Do not plan the flight and the
application as separate matters.

## The record does not reset

Problems from the D-4 period are not cleared by applying for something new.

A fine for late address reporting, unauthorised part-time work, a false
address or false documents, an overstay — all of it can surface in the
change assessment. There is at least one administrative appeal case where
false residence documentation submitted with a D-10 change led to a
departure order and entry restrictions.

Which is the real argument for treating the language year as
administrative history rather than as a gap year:
[keeping D-4 status](/study/alien-registration-for-language-students/).

## A working timeline

Not a legal rule — margin, built deliberately.

| When | What |
|---|---|
| 3 months after arrival | Decide the direction: degree, job-seeking, or home |
| Around 6 months | Confirm the requirements — TOPIK, degree, experience, admission |
| 4–6 months before completion | University applications, or enter the job market |
| 2–3 months before completion | Admission, job-seeking eligibility or a contract, concretely |
| Before your current stay expires | Apply for the change, or leave and apply at a mission |

## What actually prevents a gap

The stable long-term shape is:

```
D-4 language study
  → TOPIK, plus a clean attendance and residence record
  → D-2 admission, or D-10 eligibility
  → employment contract
  → E-7 or another appropriate work status
  → F-2 and longer-term options once the conditions are met
```

The thing that prevents a gap is not finding the next visa before your
current one expires. It is putting **four dates on one page** — your
current expiry, the start of the next activity, which status is available,
and what it requires — and making sure they do not overlap.

Almost everyone plans the first. The gap happens in the second.
