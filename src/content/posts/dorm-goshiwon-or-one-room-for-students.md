Three options, and they are usually compared on price and size. Compare
them on the deposit first, because that is what decides which ones are
available to you at all.

## Side by side

| | Dormitory | Goshiwon | One-room |
|---|---|---|---|
| Deposit | None | Often none, sometimes small | Substantial |
| Billing | Per term or month | Monthly | Monthly + utilities |
| Utilities | Usually included | Included | Separate |
| Furniture | Provided | Provided | Sometimes bare |
| Kitchen | Shared or none | Shared | Own |
| Bathroom | Shared or shared-suite | Often shared | Own |
| Space | Small, shared room common | Very small | A real room |
| Notice to leave | Term-bound | About a month | Contract term |
| Curfew / rules | Sometimes | Rarely | None |
| Proof of address | Confirmation letter | Contract, if reputable | Lease |
| Fits 3 months | Yes | Yes | Usually not |
| Fits 6 months | Yes | Yes | Conditionally |
| Fits 12 months | Check continuity | Liveability becomes the issue | Yes |

**This is a comparison of funding structures, not a price list.** Actual
amounts move with the city, the university and the building, which is why
the figures live in the tracked series rather than in this paragraph.

## The dormitory is the answer to a specific problem

It solves day-one cash, and it solves it completely: no deposit, no agency
fee, no negotiating in a language you are still learning, and a document
for your registration appointment that arrives without argument.

Its weakness is duration. Not every university guarantees a dormitory place
to language students at all, and where places exist they are limited —
constrained by capacity, by gender, by term, and by how the school handles
vacations. Some dormitories require moving out between terms.

**So the first question is not "is the dormitory cheap". It is "can I live
there continuously for six or twelve months".** A first-term place is
common; a guaranteed fourth-term place is not.

2026 examples from regional universities indicate roughly
**₩130,000–200,000 a month for a twin room**. Some schools bill by term
instead — ₩1,800,000 for six months appears in the same listings — and some
include meals, which makes a headline figure hard to compare until you know
what is inside it. The band is tracked at
[dormitory monthly cost](/tracked/student-dormitory-monthly-cost/).

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">Ask these two in writing, before you fly</p>
  <p><strong>Is a place guaranteed for the first term, and on what basis for
  later terms?</strong> "Usually available" is not something you can budget
  against. And <strong>what is included</strong> — meals, utilities, internet,
  and whether the fee is per term or per month. A dormitory figure that
  excludes meals is not comparable to one that includes them.</p>
</div>

The realistic plan for a year is often: dormitory for the first term while
you learn the city and save the deposit, then move privately. That is a
better plan than assuming continuity and discovering otherwise in week
nine of term one.

## The goshiwon is a trade, and you should know which side you are on

A goshiwon room is very small. Usually windowless or close to it, shared
kitchen, often shared bathroom, walls that carry sound. In exchange: no
deposit, all-inclusive monthly billing, and you can leave at a month's
notice.

That trade is excellent for some people and unlivable for others, and the
dividing line is not resilience.

```
Out all day, sleep there   →  it works
Study at home, need light  →  it does not
```

The failure mode is seasonal. A room with no daylight is one thing in
September and another in January, when it is dark at five and you are
indoors. If you know you need daylight and quiet to function, the goshiwon
saving is not a saving.

## The one-room, and what the deposit really is

A Korean rental deposit is not security against damage in the sense a
foreign renter expects. It is a lump sum held by the landlord for the term
of the tenancy, and its size tracks local property values, not your rent.

Which means:

- It is **large**, particularly in the capital region. Deposits of
  **₩5,000,000 or ₩10,000,000** are commonly quoted in districts students
  look in — and that is a common quote, not a national price. The figure
  moves with the district and the building, and can be well below or far
  above those.
- It is **not spendable** while you live there.
- It is the same whether you stay six months or two years.
- Getting it back is a process with its own risks.

And the rent is not the only recurring cost: maintenance charges, and
electricity, gas and water billed on usage, sit on top, plus an agency fee
at the front if you go through a broker. What the maintenance charge
actually covers is in
[utilities and maintenance fees](/cost-of-living/utilities-and-maintenance-fees-korea/).

The mechanics, and how the deposit interacts with monthly rent, are in
[jeonse and wolse explained](/cost-of-living/jeonse-vs-wolse-korean-rental-system/).

  <p>A deposit is the largest sum most students will hand to a stranger in
  Korea, and recovering one from an unwilling landlord — or from a property
  with prior claims on it — is slow and sometimes unsuccessful. What to check

Also budget for the exit, not only the entry: the deposit returns when the
next tenant's deposit arrives, which may be after you have left the
country. Keep the Korean bank account open until the money has actually
landed.

## All three need one thing

Whichever you choose, **you need a document proving you live there.**

Without it the alien registration appointment does not succeed, and
without registration the bank account, the phone contract and the
insurance all stall behind it. A dormitory issues a confirmation. A
reputable goshiwon and a proper landlord will provide a contract or
written confirmation.

What fails is the informal arrangement — a friend's spare room, a
handshake sublet, a place taken through a group chat. It may be a fine
place to live and it is not a place you can register from.

The dependency chain this sits in is in
[keeping D-4 status](/study/alien-registration-for-language-students/).

## A sequence that works

<div class="steps">
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">1</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Ask about the dormitory first</strong> — first term guaranteed? later terms on what basis? what is included? If a first-term place exists, take it. It removes the deposit from your day-one cash at the moment you can least afford it.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">2</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Use the first term to look.</strong> See rooms in person, learn which neighbourhoods around the campus are actually convenient, and find out what deposits really are there rather than what a city average says.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">3</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Then choose goshiwon or one-room</strong> with the deposit you have saved, the Korean you have gained, and a classmate who has already done it.</div></div>
</div>

Doing all of this from abroad, in month zero, with no Korean and no
ability to see the room, is how students end up in a twelve-month contract
on a place they would not have chosen in person.
