A sole proprietor's account problem is a reporting obligation. A company's is
structural, and it starts from one sentence.

**The company is a separate legal person.** Holding 100% of the shares does not
change that. Company money is not the director's money, and every movement of it
to a person needs a reason that survives being asked about.

## 1. Taking sales into a personal account breaks the accounts

Company revenue received into a director's personal account, mixed with living
expenses, cannot be reconstructed into proper corporate accounting afterwards.
Not "is harder to" — cannot, reliably, once enough months have passed.

The company's sales and costs run through accounts in the company's name. That is
the baseline, and everything below is about the exceptions.

Which of the two structures you are in was decided earlier —
[sole proprietor vs corporation in Korea](/business/sole-proprietor-vs-corporation-korea/) —
and it decides whether the rest of this page applies to you at all.

## 2. A transfer to a director is not automatically embezzlement

It is worth saying plainly, because the fear is common and the fear pushes
people into doing nothing, which is worse.

Money moves from a company to its representative for ordinary reasons all the
time: salary, bonus, dividend, expense settlement, repaying money the director
lent the company in the first place, a legitimate loan.

The problem is not the transfer. The problem is a transfer whose legal and
accounting cause cannot be stated.

Where a director takes company funds unrelated to the business and does not
settle for a long period, it can be treated as a **provisional payment** or a
loan to the director — and depending on the facts, tax, company law and criminal
questions can arrive together.

## 3. Deemed interest is not a flat 4.6%

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">"Director's balance × 4.6%" is not the calculation</p>
  <p>Where a company is treated as having lent money to a related party such as
  its representative for non-business purposes, the arm's length interest is
  built on the <strong>weighted average borrowing rate</strong> structure under the
  Corporate Tax Act first. The overdraft rate applies where the statutory
  conditions or an election bring it in — it is a route to the answer, not the
  answer.</p>
</div>

The overdraft rate itself is currently
[4.6% a year](/tracked/deemed-interest-current-account-rate/), and it is worth
tracking because the figure is set by regulation and does move.

What does not move is the rule about when it is used. A company with substantial
borrowing of its own may compute a materially different figure, and applying
4.6% as a universal formula can be wrong in either direction.

## 4. Deemed interest is the small half of the problem

A business-unrelated provisional payment does more than generate interest income
for the company.

It can also cause **disallowance of a portion of the company's own interest
expense** on its borrowings. And where the money is never recovered, or in
substance becomes the director's personal consumption, it can be reclassified —
as remuneration to the director, with the income tax and insurance consequences
that attach to that.

So the practical sequence for an owner-manager who needs money out of the
company is to decide the legal character **first**: salary, bonus, dividend, or a
properly constituted loan. Each has a different cost and a different set of
paperwork, and all four are cheaper than the fifth option of deciding nothing.

What the salary route costs the company on the way out is the same arithmetic as
any employee's —
[what your first employee actually costs](/employment/what-your-first-employee-actually-costs/).

## 5. Opening the account: more documents, and more of them for foreigners

A bank opening a sole proprietor's account will generally want the
representative's identification, the business registration certificate and
something evidencing the place of business.

A company adds to that: the corporate registration certificate, the corporate
seal and its certificate, the articles of incorporation, the shareholder
register, and beneficial ownership verification. A foreign representative or
foreign corporate shareholders can bring further requirements around residence
status and overseas company and shareholder documentation.

## 6. The real obstacle for a new company is the limited account

Banks verify the purpose of financial transactions when opening a chequing
account, as a control against accounts opened to be sold on.

A newly incorporated company has no sales and no trading history, so it often
cannot evidence a transaction purpose — and the account is opened as a
**limited-transaction account**.

Since May 2024 the ordinary banking-sector daily limits on such an account are:

| Channel | Daily limit |
|---|---|
| Branch counter | [₩3,000,000](/tracked/limited-account-counter-limit/) |
| ATM | ₩1,000,000 |
| Electronic banking | [₩1,000,000](/tracked/limited-account-electronic-limit/) |

Internet-only banks and individual institutions can set their own figures, so
these are the general banking-sector levels rather than an absolute ceiling on
every account in Korea.

For a company paying wages, rent and materials, ₩1,000,000 a day through
electronic banking is binding from the first month. It is worth knowing before
incorporation rather than on the day the first invoice falls due.

## 7. Lifting the limit is about evidence that the business is moving

When a business asks to convert to an unrestricted account, the bank can require
objective material showing an actual business purpose.

In practice that means things like the lease, electronic tax invoices, sales
contracts, customer agreements, payroll records, VAT filings, and evidence of the
premises.

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">No single document releases it</p>
  <p>This is a customer due diligence and transaction-purpose review, not a
  procedure where submitting one tax invoice triggers an automatic release. Which
  is why a new company should start accumulating evidence from its first
  transaction — the restriction lifts when there is a record to show, and the
  record only exists if someone kept it.</p>
</div>

## 8. What the whole structure is for

| | Personal account | Sole proprietor's business-use account | Company account |
|---|---|---|---|
| Whose money | The individual's | The proprietor's own | The company's |
| Separate legal person | No | No | **Yes** |
| NTS business-use account report | Not applicable | Required of double-entry obligors | A separate regime |
| Personal spending | Free | Separate it | Not company funds |
| Owner withdrawals | Free | Proprietor's drawing | Needs a legal cause |
| Provisional payment risk | None | Not the corporate concept | **Real** |
| Limited-transaction account | Possible | Possible | Common for new companies |
| Central risk | — | Mixing business and household | Mixing company and director |

Separating business accounts in Korea is not about having the word "business"
printed somewhere.

It is about making the bank transactions, the tax invoices, the card records, the
payroll and the ledger describe one flow of money. Once they do, the economic
cause of any transaction can be explained — to a tax audit, or to a bank asking
where funds came from. Once they do not, total revenue and actual costs have to
be reconstructed from several accounts, by someone, later.

For a sole proprietor that means an obligation and a habit — covered in
[business vs personal bank accounts in Korea](/business/business-account-vs-personal-account-in-korea/).
For a company it goes one step further: the company's money and the director's
money have to be separate in law, not merely tidy. And that stays true at the
end, where the balance in a company account is not the director's to take home —
[the deadlines after the last day](/business/closing-a-business-the-deadlines-after-the-last-day/).
