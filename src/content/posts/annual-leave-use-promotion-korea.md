faq:
  - question: 'My company emailed everyone in December saying to use up their leave. Does that remove my unused-leave pay?'
    answer: 'Almost certainly not. Encouraging staff to take leave and running the statutory use-promotion procedure are different things. The procedure has two stages with fixed timing — a written notice to each employee of their unused days, keyed to six months before the leave period ends, and, if the employee does not nominate dates within ten days, a written designation by the employer no later than two months before it ends. A single all-staff email late in the year satisfies neither the individual notice requirement nor the schedule, so the duty to pay is not removed.'
  - question: 'Is a KakaoTalk message valid, or does it have to be paper?'
    answer: 'Neither answer is safe as a rule about the medium. Article 61 requires the promotion to be in writing, so a manager saying "use your leave" in a meeting does not satisfy it. Whether an email or a message in a company system meets the written requirement depends on how it was actually delivered — whether it went to the employee individually, whether it stated their own unused days, and whether they could read and keep it. "KakaoTalk is always invalid, email is always valid" is not the test; whether each employee was properly notified in writing of their own position is.'
  - question: 'I am in my first year. Does the six-month notice apply to me?'
    answer: 'No, and this is where employers most often invalidate their own procedure. First-year leave has its own shorter timetable: the first written request is keyed to three months before the first year ends, and if you do not nominate dates within ten days, the employer must designate them in writing by one month before the year ends. Days that accrue after the first notice run on a further compressed schedule. An employer applying the general six-month and two-month dates to a first-year employee has not validly promoted that leave, and the pay remains owing.'
  - question: 'They designated my leave dates but then made me work those days. What happens?'
    answer: 'Then the promotion has not done what it needs to do. Article 60 provides that leave does not simply lapse where the employee was unable to use it through the employer’s fault, so a company that designates dates on paper and then requires attendance, or that refuses leave requests while running the paperwork, is in a different position from one that genuinely made the time available. Most of Korean annual leave is arithmetic. This part is not: it is a
procedure that decides **who owes money**, and it either happened properly or
it did not.

Article 61 lets an employer that has completed the statutory use-promotion
steps escape the duty to pay for leave the employee then failed to take. That
is a substantial legal consequence attached to a set of dates and a delivery
requirement — which is why "we told everyone to use their leave" is not the
same sentence.

## The general procedure, in two stages

For an employee past their first year, on ordinary annual leave:

| Stage | What the Act requires |
|---|---|
| First | Within **10 days** of the point **six months** before the leave period ends, the employer notifies the employee **in writing** of the days they have not used, and asks them to fix when they will take them |
| Employee | **10 days** from that request to notify the employer of the dates |
| Second | If the employee does not, the employer **designates the dates in writing** by **two months** before the leave period ends |

The anchor is
[six months before expiry](/tracked/annual-leave-promotion-deadlines/) for the
first notice.

Both stages, both deadlines, and the written requirement. Miss one and the
procedure has not been completed — which means the pay obligation was never
removed.

## The first-year timetable is different, and shorter

The up-to-eleven days accrued during a first year all expire together at the
end of that year —
[one shared deadline](/tracked/first-year-leave-expiry-rule/) — so a promotion
schedule built around a six-month runway does not fit them.

  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">2</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Employee replies within 10 days</strong> with the dates they will take.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">3</div>

    <div class="step-body"><strong>Days accruing after that first request</strong> run on a further compressed schedule of their own.</div></div>

  unused-leave pay is still owed — usually discovered at the point someone
  leaves and asks.</p>

## "In writing" is a delivery question, not a brand question

Article 61 requires the promotion to be **in writing**. A team leader saying
"everyone use up your leave" does not meet it.

What it does not do is settle the status of any particular medium. Whether an
email, or a notice inside a company HR system, satisfies the written
requirement turns on how it was actually delivered:

- did it go to the employee **individually**, rather than to a group
- did it state **that employee's own** unused days
- could the employee read it, and keep it

<div class="callout callout-note">
  <p class="callout-title">Do not reason from the app name</p>
  <p>"KakaoTalk is automatically invalid, email is automatically valid" is not
  the test, and repeating it produces confident wrong answers in both
  directions. The question is whether each employee was properly notified in
  writing of their own position, and that can fail by email and be satisfied
  by a system notification.</p>
</div>

## Completing the paperwork is not the same as providing the leave

A company that designates leave dates and then requires the employee to work
them has not made the time available. Nor has one that refuses leave requests
while running the promotion process on paper.

Article 60 provides that leave does not simply lapse where the employee could
not use it **through the employer's fault**. So the promotion machinery does
not operate in isolation from what actually happened.

The purpose of the procedure is to produce genuine opportunities to rest. Where
the record shows the opposite, that is a separate legal question and not one
the notices answer by themselves.

## If you are on the receiving end

<div class="steps">
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">1</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Keep the notice.</strong> Date, medium, and whether it named your own unused days — those three decide most of it.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">2</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Reply within the 10 days</strong> with dates. Replying is better than being designated, and it removes the argument entirely.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">3</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Check which timetable you are on.</strong> First year or not — an employer using the wrong one has not validly promoted your leave.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">4</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>If you were made to work a designated day</strong>, record it at the time, alongside the designation notice.</div></div>
