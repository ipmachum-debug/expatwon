A check card and a credit card look like the same object and do the same
thing at the till. They are issued on completely different logic.

A **check card** settles immediately against money you already hold. The
issuer takes no risk, so the questions are about identity and the
account behind it.

A **credit card** is the card company paying your merchant now and
collecting from you later. That is credit, and it is assessed like
credit — residence status, remaining stay, employment, income, domestic
bureau data and existing debt.

Which is why an alien registration card gets you the first and not the
second.

## Side by side

| | Check card | Credit card |
|---|---|---|
| Mechanism | Settles from your balance, immediately | Issuer pays now, you repay on a set date |
| Residence status | Broadly available once identity and an account are in order | Stability of status is an assessment input |
| Minimum stay | **No nationwide legal minimum** | **No nationwide legal minimum** — issuers set their own |
| Income evidence | Not generally required | Central |
| NICE / KCB data | Limited effect on issuance | A main input |
| Settlement account | Required | Required |
| Post-paid transit | Separately assessed | Depends on the product |
| Age | By product | Adult in principle, with narrow exceptions |

## The account comes first, and its condition follows the card

A check card is normally applied for alongside, or just after, opening
the bank account it draws on. With an alien registration card or domestic
residence report card and a properly opened account, most banks will
issue one.

Worth bringing:

- Alien registration card or domestic residence report card
- Passport
- A Korean mobile number in your own name
- Your Korean address
- The purpose evidence the account itself needs — employment contract and
  certificate for a salary account, enrolment certificate for a student

That last line is the one that actually decides the day. If the bank
cannot establish the purpose of the account, it may open a **limited
account** — and a check card drawn on a limited account inherits those
caps. Sort that out first:
[opening a Korean bank account](/banking/how-to-open-a-bank-account-in-korea-as-a-foreigner/)
and [limited accounts explained](/banking/korean-limited-accounts-hando-gyejwa-explained/).

## Post-paid transit is credit, and it is screened on its own

Here is the part almost no English guide gets right.

An ordinary check card needs no credit line — it spends your own balance.
**Post-paid transit does not work that way.** You tap through buses and
subways for a period, the fares accumulate, and the total is collected
from your account afterwards. Between the ride and the collection, the
card company is owed money.

That is why the function is age-regulated at all: the Credit Specialized
Finance Business Act Enforcement Decree was amended in June 2019 to lower
the minimum age for adding post-paid transit to a check card from 18 to
**12**.

  <p class="callout-title">"Check card approved, transit declined" is normal</p>
  <p>Issuers apply their own criteria to the transit function — identity,

  the card. Treat them as two approvals, and ask about the transit
  function explicitly rather than assuming it came with the card. If
  collection fails, the function is suspended and late charges can apply
  to the unpaid fares.</p>

## A credit card application is a small unsecured loan review

Not a payment-method request. The issuer is advancing funds between your
purchase and your statement date, and it reviews accordingly:

  <div class="check-row ok"><span class="mark"></span><span><strong>Employment and its stability</strong> — employer, tenure, contract type</span></div>
  <div class="check-row ok"><span class="mark"></span><span><strong>Income and disposable income</strong></span></div>
  <div class="check-row ok"><span class="mark"></span><span><strong>Length of your domestic financial history</strong>, and NICE / KCB bureau data</span></div>
  <div class="check-row ok"><span class="mark"></span><span><strong>Existing loans, card debt and any arrears</strong></span></div>
  <div class="check-row ok"><span class="mark"></span><span><strong>Assets</strong>, and the issuer's own application scoring on top of the bureau score</span></div>
</div>

Which is why two people holding the same visa get different answers.

## Two myths worth naming

**"There is a list of card-eligible visas."** There is not. F-5, F-6,
F-2 and F-4 often assess as more settled, and an E-7 holder with a Korean
salary and enough tenure is a normal applicant — but "F-5 yes, E-7
conditional, D-2 no" is not a rule anywhere. A long-term status with no
income, heavy debt or arrears is declined; a shorter status with a stable
Korean salary and a clean record may not be.

**"You need NICE 700."** No issuer publishes a threshold, and card
companies run their own application scoring alongside the bureau score.
Some application screens block below a set score — that is one issuer's
configuration, not a national standard.

<p class="hand">If a site tells you the exact score or the exact visa, it is guessing. Nobody publishes those numbers.</p>

## What actually goes in the file

For an employed applicant:

| Document | What it evidences |
|---|---|
| Employment certificate | That you work there now |
| Employment contract | Terms and duration |
| Withholding tax receipt | Last year's income, officially |
| Income certificate | The same, from the tax office |
| Payslips | Current pay level |
| Salary transfer history | That the money actually arrives |
| Health insurance record | Enrolment and contributions paid |

Recently arrived, with no prior-year withholding receipt or income
certificate? The contract, the employment certificate and a few months of
salary transfers usually carry the file. **How many months, and which
documents count, differ by issuer** — so ask before you apply rather than
after a decline.

## Where income cannot be evidenced

No salary does not mean no possibility. Issuers may weigh deposits,
savings and property as repayment capacity, and some institutions offer
cards secured against a deposit or savings balance.

Two cautions. This is **not a standard product you can demand** — whether
it exists, the deposit required, the limit ratio and the security
mechanics all vary by institution. And it is not a conversion rate:
depositing ₩10,000,000 does not produce a ₩10,000,000 limit.

## Age follows the ordinary rules

Credit cards are for adults in principle, with narrow exceptions in
issuers' criteria — such as applicants of 18 with verifiable employment.
Check cards can go lower by product, sometimes with a legal
representative's consent. **Being foreign adds no separate age rule.**
For an adult applicant, the ARC, the account and identity verification
decide far more than age does.

Starting with your main bank's card affiliate has a real rationale: the
group can already see the salary landing and the account behaving, so
there is less about you that has to be asserted rather than observed.
It is not a guarantee of approval.

**A decline at one issuer is not a verdict on Korea.** Criteria and
required evidence differ. But firing applications at several issuers in a
short window is worse than talking to one that is likely to recognise
your status and income — and for foreign applicants, a branch or a
foreign-customer desk, where you can explain employment, income and
remaining stay in person, often beats an online form.

## Which one, and when

Newly arrived, with little Korean income history: **the check card is the
answer**, and it covers almost everything — living costs, online
shopping, utilities.

Once you have worked here a while and the record exists: a credit card
adds instalments, a real limit and card benefits. It also adds a payment
date to manage, and arrears on it feed straight back into your domestic
score.

The thing that decides the outcome is never one visa name. It is
**stability of stay, income you can evidence in Korea, length of domestic
financial history, bureau data, and existing debt** — combined. The
practical route is to run the check card and the account cleanly first,
then extend into credit once there is something for an issuer to read.

How that record is actually built — and how long it takes — is set out in
[the Korean credit score for foreigners](/loans/korean-credit-score-for-foreigners-nice-kcb/).
