There is no separate foreign tax system and no foreign deduction table.

A foreign employee receiving Korean employment income goes through the same
January settlement as everyone else: the company compares what it withheld
monthly against the year's actual liability, and collects or refunds the
difference.

The differences that do exist come from four places — **residence status,
family living abroad, housing conditions, and whether the 19% flat rate was
elected.** Not from nationality.

## The calendar is the same

The National Tax Service runs the foreign employee settlement on the same
schedule and the same procedure as everyone else's.

Where the company uses the bulk provision service, it registers its employee
list with the tax office and the employee consents in Hometax to the scope
of data being handed over. In the most recent cycle that consent window ran
from early December to mid-January, with the company collecting the
simplified data afterwards.

<div class="callout callout-note">
  <p class="callout-title">The dates are re-announced every year</p>
  <p>Do not plan from last year's window. The National Tax Service publishes
  the schedule each cycle, and the sensible habit is to settle dependants,
  housing, rent and the flat-rate question in <strong>December</strong> rather
  than gathering evidence in January.</p>
</div>

## Not every foreign employee files the same documents

A common overstatement is that a foreign employee must submit proof of alien
registration every year.

The guidance asks for it from **newly hired foreign employees** and those
whose **dependants have changed** — not as a universal annual requirement.

| Stage | What is involved |
|---|---|
| Base settlement | Employee's deduction and credit return |
| Simplified data | Hometax data, or consent to bulk provision |
| New hire, or changed dependants | Proof of alien registration |
| Medical missed by the simplified service | Receipts from the hospital or pharmacy |
| Education | Payment certificates from qualifying Korean institutions |
| Donations | Receipts from qualifying recipients |
| Dependants abroad | Relationship, income and actual support |
| Electing 19% | Foreign employee flat-rate application |

## Residence is the first fork, and it is a big one

A foreign employee who is a **tax resident** can, in principle, access the
same structure a Korean employee does — the basic deduction, credit card
usage, medical, insurance, education and donation credits, the child credit.

A **non-resident** cannot. The comparison is stark:

| | Foreign resident | Foreign non-resident |
|---|---|---|
| Own personal deduction | Yes | Yes |
| Spouse and dependants | Yes, on conditions | No |
| Credit card usage | Yes, on conditions | No |
| Health and employment insurance premiums | Yes | No |
| Medical and insurance credits | Yes | No |
| Child tax credit | Yes | No |
| Housing rental loan principal and interest | Yes, on conditions | No |
| Long-term mortgage interest | Yes, on conditions | No |
| Monthly rent credit | Yes, on conditions | No |
| Housing subscription savings | Restricted, with an exception | No |

So the first question in a foreign employee's settlement is not "are they
foreign". It is **"were they a Korean tax resident for that tax period"** —
and that is a question about their life, answered in
[income tax for foreigners: resident or not](/business/income-tax-for-foreigners-resident-or-not/).

## The housing rule changed in 2021, and the old sentence is still circulating

This is the most consequential correction in the whole guide.

Older material explains that foreign nationals cannot claim housing
deductions because they cannot be a head of household under the resident
registration system. **That stopped being the position in 2021.**

A qualifying foreign resident may claim:

<div class="steps">
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">1</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Repayment of principal and interest on a housing rental loan</strong> — deducted at 40% of the repayment, subject to an annual ceiling of ₩4,000,000 combined with housing savings.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">2</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Interest on a long-term mortgage</strong>, on the ordinary conditions.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">3</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>The monthly rent tax credit</strong> — see below, because it has a condition specific to foreign residents.</div></div>
</div>

  <p class="callout-title">"Foreigners cannot claim housing" is now the error, not the rule</p>
  <p>Following pre-2021 guidance means handing back deductions you were

## The rent credit turns on your registered stay address

For a foreign resident the monthly rent tax credit runs on a condition
Korean employees never meet, because it does not apply to them.

What matters is **not** the address on a resident registration extract. It
is your registered place of stay under the Immigration Act — and the decree
requires the address on the lease and the registered place of stay to
**match**.

```
where you actually live
  = the address on the lease
  = your reported place of stay with immigration
```

Which ties this deduction directly to something else with its own deadline:
report a change of stay address within **15 days** of moving, or these three
fall out of alignment. That obligation is in
[keeping D-4 status](/study/alien-registration-for-language-students/), and
it applies well beyond students.

Transferring the rent by bank transfer is not by itself enough — total
salary, not owning a home, the property's value and size, and whose name is
on the contract all have to be satisfied too.

## Housing subscription savings: still restricted, but no longer closed

Keep this one separate from the deductions above.

Because a foreign national cannot be a head of household in the ordinary
sense, claiming housing subscription savings as a non-home-owning head of
household is generally not available.

**From contributions made in 2025, an exception opened.** A foreign employee
who is a Korean resident, has total salary of **₩70,000,000 or below**, is
the **spouse of a head of household owning no home**, and contributes to a
qualifying housing savings account in their own name, may deduct **40% of
contributions up to ₩3,000,000 a year**.

So the accurate statement is neither "foreigners can" nor "foreigners
cannot":

| | |
|---|---|
| General position | Still restricted |
| Spouse of a non-home-owning head of household, salary ₩70m or below | Available, on the 2025 rules |

Tracked at
[the housing savings exception](/tracked/housing-savings-foreign-spouse-rule/).

## Family abroad can be claimed. Money spent abroad usually cannot

These two get confused constantly, and they point in opposite directions.

### Dependants living abroad — yes

A spouse in your home country can qualify for the basic deduction where the
income test and the rest are met. There is no requirement that dependants
live in Korea.

For parents or children abroad, you additionally have to show **actual
support** — the guidance gives remittances of living expenses as the
example.

Documents issued by a foreign government need an **apostille** where that
country is party to the convention, or **consular confirmation** at the
Korean mission where it is not.

```
relationship  +  age and other conditions  +  income  +  actual support
```

Build that as one file, not four separate scrambles in January.

### Money spent abroad — generally no

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">Three things that are not deductible, however good the receipt</p>
  <p><strong>Education fees for a child at a school abroad</strong> are not
  eligible for the education credit for a foreign employee.
  <strong>Medical expenses paid to a foreign medical institution</strong> are
  not eligible for the medical credit. And <strong>credit card spending
  abroad</strong> does not count toward the card usage deduction.</p>
</div>

None of these are cases of Hometax failing to collect something. They are
outside the deduction, and the distinction matters:

| | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Missing from the simplified service** | Get the certificate from the institution and submit it |
| **Not deductible** | No document helps |

## Electing 19% changes what the paperwork is for

Elect the flat rate and the exemptions, deductions, reductions and credits
are in principle not applied to that employment income. So medical,
education, card and rent documents make no difference to the calculation,
however many are submitted.

What you submit instead is the **foreign employee flat-rate application**,
attached to your deduction and credit return and given to the employer as
withholding agent — or to the district tax office if you are electing on an
annual return.

Which method actually costs less, and why the crossover is much higher than
usually quoted, is in
[the 19% flat rate: when it beats the brackets](/business/the-19-percent-flat-rate-when-it-wins/).

## The order HR should work in

Not receipts first.

  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">1</div>
    <div class="step-body">Resident or non-resident, for that tax period</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">2</div>
    <div class="step-body">Progressive or flat rate — decided, not assumed</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">3</div>
    <div class="step-body">If progressive: dependants → housing → rent → cards → insurance, medical, education → donations → evidence for family abroad</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">4</div>
    <div class="step-body">Reconcile the simplified data against what was submitted directly</div></div>
</div>

Working this way stops the commonest error, which is claiming something on
the strength of a receipt for a payment that was never deductible.

## The simplified service is not an approval

Its value is that it collects from hospitals, card companies, insurers and
financial institutions automatically. It does **not** confirm that a
deduction's legal conditions are met.

A dependant's medical expenses can appear without that person qualifying as
a dependant. Housing data can appear without the ownership, income, address
and loan conditions being satisfied. The employee still has to check.

The reverse holds too: a legitimate item missing from the simplified data
can be claimed by obtaining the receipt or payment certificate from the
institution and submitting it to the company. The guidance says so
explicitly for insurance, medical, education, donation and card-related
items.

## January is not the last chance

Missing a deduction in the January settlement does not extinguish it. It can
be picked up in the following May filing period, or through a claim for
correction where the conditions are met.

The asymmetry is worth internalising: claiming something you were **not**
entitled to can mean additional tax later, with penalties attached.

Which is why the objective for a foreign employee is not maximising the
refund. It is **being able to evidence every deduction claimed** — and the
evidence for dependants abroad in particular takes weeks to assemble from
another country.

## The whole thing, in order

```
tax residence
  → progressive or 19%
  → dependant eligibility
  → housing conditions
  → qualifying Korean expenditure
  → simplified data
  → missing evidence
  → company settlement
```

The largest single risk is not missing a receipt. It is **using guidance
written before 2021**, concluding that a foreign employee cannot claim
housing, and quietly giving up deductions that have been available for
years.

Nationality is not what makes a foreign settlement different. What makes it
different is whether residence, household, registered stay address, actual
spending and the elected method all line up with each other.
