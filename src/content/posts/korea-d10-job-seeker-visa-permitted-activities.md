**D-10 gives you time to look for qualifying work. It is not an open work permit.**

A foreign student finishes a degree at a Korean university but has not yet secured a job. A company offers a trial internship. A different employer suggests that the graduate start working immediately and switch to E-7 after the probation period. A friend says D-10 now lasts three years, so there should be plenty of time to do both.

Each statement contains a possible trap. D-10 is a job-seeking or qualifying preparation status, with categories, eligibility requirements and activity limits. A permitted internship is not automatically the same as unrestricted paid employment. A future E-7 application does not retroactively authorize work that was not permitted when it began. And the three-year policy improvement introduced in late 2025 must be read according to **who qualifies for that benefit**.

The useful way to understand D-10 is as a bridge: it can keep eligible applicants lawfully in Korea while they pursue a qualifying next step, but the bridge has its own rules.

## What D-10 is designed for

Korea's government-run Study in Korea employment guidance describes a change to job-seeking or start-up preparation status for eligible people who seek jobs at domestic companies or organizations, participate in qualifying short-term internships, or prepare intellectual-property or technology-based start-up activities through relevant arrangements.

This should not be translated as “any foreigner without a job can take D-10.” The applicable requirements consider prior or present status, educational background, career or qualification criteria and the particular D-10 route. Some categories are designed for professional job-seeking; others involve qualifying technical start-up preparation. A student graduating from a Korean university and an overseas professional entering under a special talent program may not be assessed under the same conditions.

The government's guidance lists examples of eligible applicants including qualifying graduates or prospective graduates of domestic junior colleges and universities and certain degree holders pursuing intellectual-property-based start-ups. Additional special categories and point-system conditions must be checked in the current immigration manual.

Before collecting papers, identify the precise D-10 subtype. **D-10-1** is commonly associated with job seeking; other D-10 arrangements may relate to start-up preparation. Their permitted activities and supporting evidence are not interchangeable.

## The October 29, 2025 reform: what changed

A major source of obsolete advice is the former description that D-10 job-seekers could stay only up to **two years**, with renewals in **six-month** increments. In an official October 2025 Ministry of Justice announcement, Korea announced improvements effective **October 29, 2025**: a move from six-month renewal increments to **one year at a time**, and an expansion of the maximum job-seeking period from **two to three years** for the reform's intended beneficiaries.

The same reform changed internship arrangements: the former total internship-duration ceiling of one year was removed, while the maximum internship period at a **single company** increased from six months to **one year** under the announced scheme. Ministry communications frame these measures in the context of supporting graduates of Korean universities and the recruitment of overseas talent.

Do not copy “every D-10 holder receives three years, one year at a time” into a visa application or an employment contract. Specific eligibility, transition and review conditions still apply. The published policy change is a maximum framework for qualifying cases, not a promise of three years of automatic permission with no extension application.

## Job hunting is allowed; ordinary work needs its own basis

D-10 is intended to facilitate job search. Activities such as applying for jobs, attending interviews, corresponding with prospective employers and preparing evidence for a qualifying employment-status change fit its purpose.

But accepting a normal paid job is a different legal step. A company cannot safely say, “Start next Monday; we will file E-7 after your first paycheck,” and assume the D-10 label authorizes that arrangement. A proposed job must be examined under the relevant employment-status rules and immigration procedure before work begins.

In some cases, an applicant may qualify to change directly from D-10 to an employment status such as E-7 after obtaining an appropriate contract and satisfying occupation, qualifications, pay and employer-related criteria. The timing of approval and whether any separate authorization is needed are central questions.

The employer's willingness to hire is not the same thing as the immigration authority's permission to perform the work. A business manager and a candidate can both face serious problems when they treat paperwork as something to fix after the fact.

## Internships are permitted only within the relevant scheme

Internship activity is one of the most misunderstood elements of D-10. Government guidance recognizes certain short-term internships or training-related activity during job seeking. The 2025 reform expanded the framework for eligible cases, including the longer single-company period noted above.

That does **not** mean any business can employ a D-10 holder indefinitely under the title “intern.” Labels do not replace substance. The nature of the work, duration, remuneration, supervising organization and approval or reporting obligations can determine how immigration classifies the activity.

Before an internship begins, the applicant and employer should confirm the status-specific requirements through HiKorea or the competent office. Record the company's details, activity description, dates, compensation or training allowance, and the legal basis for participation. Do not assume that calling money a stipend eliminates employment questions.

Employers should also avoid designing a permanent operational position as serial internships merely to delay a proper employment-status change. The announced removal of a total duration cap for qualifying internships does not erase other immigration, labor or workplace obligations.

## Part-time work is a separate question

Students on D-2 frequently hear about permitted part-time work under particular authorization rules. It is a mistake to carry that permission over automatically after changing to D-10. Different statuses have different activity rights and approval conditions.

A prospective employer might offer café shifts, food delivery, tutoring or short-term office help. Whether any such activity is permitted while holding D-10 cannot be resolved solely by saying the applicant “needs living expenses.” The underlying visa's permitted scope and any available separate authorization must be verified.

Do not infer that a payment received in cash or through a friend's account is exempt from immigration law. The analysis concerns what activity is performed and whether the foreigner is allowed to perform it, not merely how remuneration is labeled.

If income is necessary while job hunting, check the precise lawful options before accepting shifts. The immigration consequences of unauthorized work can outlast the immediate earnings and affect future changes of status.

## Graduating from D-2: plan the transition early

A degree does not silently extend student status after the conditions for D-2 change. A student who is approaching graduation should decide whether the next legitimate route is qualifying employment, D-10 job seeking, further study or departure.

The government Study in Korea guidance identifies documentation for certain D-2-to-D-10 applicants: an application form, passport, residence card, education or transcript evidence and a job-seeking activity plan, with other papers where applicable. The exact current list may differ by applicant type and location.

The job-seeking plan deserves attention. A vague statement that you “want work in Korea” does not tell an immigration reviewer what qualifying occupation is being pursued, which employers or sectors are targeted, or how you intend to use the stay. Present a genuine plan consistent with your background and the eligible professional activities.

Do not exaggerate pending job offers or invent interview invitations. If you have a conditional offer, describe what is still conditional. Credibility is more valuable than optimistic paperwork.

## The D-10 to E-7 transition is a new legal test

D-10 may give applicants time to locate an employer, but an eventual **E-7** change has distinct requirements. The occupation needs to fit an eligible classification; the applicant may have to prove qualifications, degree or experience; and the employer must meet its own relevant conditions.

The government Study in Korea employment page describes a change from D-2 or D-10 to qualifying employment status after an appropriate employment contract. It also lists employer documentation and occupation-specific supporting records. Some of its numerical examples or older tables may need reconciliation with more recent ministry notices; use the current E-7 manual for publication-day requirements.

A helpful sequence is: confirm occupation code; check credentials and relevant degree; screen employer eligibility; verify current salary conditions; sign an accurate employment agreement subject to lawful authorization; file the status change; begin activity at the legally permitted time.

For a small or newly formed employer, immigration compliance can be a separate challenge even if the candidate is excellent. The employer should prepare its registration, workforce and business records early rather than promising a visa based purely on enthusiasm.

## Special talent programs do not rewrite every D-10 case

The October 2025 Ministry announcement also described special treatment for selected overseas talented graduates, including point-system or financial-requirement exemptions for certain high-ranking-university applicants, and targeted benefits for some young graduates in specified regional or technology categories.

These benefits are conditional on the announced profile and requirements. A person who studied abroad cannot assume that every Korean-university graduate rule applies to them, nor that a government list of eligible universities remains unchanged forever.

A candidate should first confirm which policy track applies: ordinary D-10 point-based eligibility, the Korean-graduate route, start-up preparation or a specified special talent measure. Mixing advantages from different routes can produce an invalid application.

If a recruiter says a university ranking automatically waives E-7 requirements, ask for the exact official policy and effective date. Some announced exemptions are limited to identified categories and may operate within a pilot period.

## What about start-up preparation?

The government guidance also describes D-10 uses for qualifying technology or intellectual-property-driven entrepreneurial preparation, such as patent, utility model or design-related work and participation in the OASIS start-up immigration support scheme.

That is not a general right to operate any retail or trading business as though a D-8 investment status had already been approved. Activities designed to prepare a qualifying start-up should be distinguished from running a commercial company and earning ordinary business income.

An applicant considering a start-up route should assemble the proposed technology or IP evidence, a coherent business preparation plan and the correct training or support-program documentation. Do not file a job-seeker application that disguises an already operating business.

Once the company reaches the operating stage, verify the appropriate status and corporate or investment requirements before commencing activities outside the D-10 permission.

## Financial and residence practicalities

A job seeker may have a valid residence status but no regular Korean salary. That can affect rent deposits, bank services and credit applications. D-10 authorization should not be represented to a lender as proof of a future job; a signed but unapproved employment change is likewise not current payroll evidence.

Build a realistic living-cost runway. Include housing, health insurance where applicable, travel, job-search expenses and the costs of future application steps. The official maximum D-10 period is not a budget. A person may receive less time than the published maximum or change status much sooner.

Keep your registered address accurate and maintain timely immigration filings. If you leave Korea temporarily, separate the rules on re-entry from the rules on D-10 extensions; a trip does not necessarily restart the job-seeking allowance or remove an expiry date.

## An employer's checklist before inviting a D-10 candidate to start

Confirm the candidate's exact D-10 category and currently authorized period. Describe the proposed activity honestly: interview, trial, internship, ordinary employment or start-up work. Identify the permission basis. Check the relevant occupation and salary framework if conversion to E-7 is intended. File any required application before the activity begins. Preserve clear agreements and correspondence.

If the company is using an internship pathway, determine the permitted length at that employer and any reporting or approval obligations. If it needs an employee to fill an operational vacancy, prepare the employment-status application rather than extending an internship label beyond its legal function.

## Bottom line

D-10 can be a valuable buffer between study, professional employment and qualifying start-up preparation. The October 2025 reforms made the framework more accommodating for specified applicants, including extended job-search and internship parameters. They did **not** turn D-10 into an unrestricted work visa.

Before accepting an offer, answer the questions in order: **Which D-10 route do I hold? What activity is actually proposed? What permission is needed before starting? When must I file for the next status?** That sequence protects both the job seeker and the employer.
