The day you hire your first employee, the legal character of your
business changes. A one-person company becomes an employer, and with that
come duties on contracts, social insurance, withholding, working hours,
holidays and severance.

Being a foreign owner changes none of this. A sole proprietorship or
company you set up in Korea, employing someone in Korea, is inside Korean
labour law.

Hiring a *foreign* employee adds one step in front of everything else:
before the working conditions matter, you have to confirm that the
person's residence status permits the job you are hiring them for.

So the first hire is not a decision about salary. It is the moment you
build a system — contract, four insurances, payroll register,
withholding, attendance records, severance — and each part has to agree
with the others.

## The contract, even for one person

A recurring belief is that the Labor Standards Act does not reach
workplaces with fewer than five employees. It mostly does. Written
statement of conditions, wage payment, rest breaks, the paid weekly
holiday and advance notice of dismissal all apply below five.

Article 17 requires the employer to state the working conditions when
concluding the contract, and to hand the employee a written document
setting out the components of pay, how it is calculated and how it is
paid, along with working hours, holidays and annual leave. Electronic
documents count.

At minimum the contract should make these unambiguous:

- Contract term
- Place of work and duties
- Contractual working days and hours
- Rest breaks
- The components of pay
- How pay is calculated and the payment date
- Weekly holiday and other holidays
- Annual paid leave and other conditions

Writing it is not the whole duty. **You must actually give the employee a
copy.** Failing to state and deliver the conditions in writing carries a
fine of up to ₩5,000,000. A single part-time employee working a few hours
a day is not an exception.

## The four insurances, and what they really cost

Hiring triggers registration of the workplace and the employee for
National Pension, health insurance, employment insurance and industrial
accident insurance. The four can be reported together, and the ordinary
deadline for reporting an employee's acquisition of coverage is the 15th
of the month following the month the event occurred — so a 10 August
start is reportable by 15 September. In practice, file immediately:
payroll and premium calculations depend on it.

The 2026 rates, and who pays:

| Scheme | Total rate | Employee | Employer |
|---|---|---|---|
| National Pension | 9.5% | 4.75% | 4.75% |
| Health insurance | 7.19% | 3.595% | 3.595% |
| Long-term care | 13.14% of the health premium | half | half |
| Employment insurance (unemployment) | 1.8% | 0.9% | 0.9% |
| Employment stability & skills (under 150 staff) | 0.25% | — | 0.25% |
| Industrial accident | 1.47% average, by industry | — | all of it |

The industrial accident rate is the one that moves most. It is set across
28 industry classes plus a commuting-accident component, so office work
sits well below the 1.47% average and construction or manufacturing well
above it. Look up your own class rather than budgeting on the average.

## Short hours do not switch the insurances off

Employees working under 15 hours a week are treated differently for some
coverages and for severance. That is not the same as "no insurance at
all."

National Pension and health insurance each have their own exclusion tests
based on monthly hours, days worked and the nature of the employment
relationship. Employment insurance has its own exceptions based on
contractual hours and continuous service. And industrial accident
insurance is not generally excluded merely because the hours are short.

For a foreign employee there is an additional layer: National Pension
coverage can turn on nationality, any bilateral social security
agreement, and reciprocity. Design the salary the same way you would for
a Korean employee, then check pension applicability and residence status
separately.

## Minimum wage 2026: ₩10,320 an hour

The 2026 minimum wage is **₩10,320 per hour**, up 2.9% from ₩10,030.

| | Amount |
|---|---|
| Hourly | ₩10,320 |
| Daily (8 hours) | ₩82,560 |
| Monthly (209 hours) | ₩2,156,880 |

The 209-hour figure is 40 contractual hours a week plus the 8 paid
weekly-holiday hours, annualised. It applies to every workplace including
those under five employees, and an employee cannot agree to less. Paying
below it exposes the employer to up to three years' imprisonment or a
fine of up to ₩20,000,000.

Paying a monthly salary does not exempt you from the check. You divide
the pay that counts toward the minimum wage by the monthly reference
hours and compare the resulting hourly figure. Which is why a contract
that says only "₩2,500,000 per month" is weaker than one that separates
base pay from each allowance and states what each is for.

The 90% probation rate is real but narrow. Three conditions must hold

## The weekly holiday allowance follows hours, not headcount

Weekly holiday pay is not a five-or-more rule either. It applies below
five.

An employee whose contractual hours average 15 or more a week over four
weeks, and who works all their scheduled days in a given week, earns paid
weekly holiday. For a 40-hour employee that is normally 8 hours. For
part-timers it is proportional:

> 20 hours ÷ 40 hours × 8 hours = **4 hours**
> 4 × ₩10,320 = **₩41,280** for that week

Whether the entitlement arises, and the exact formula, depend on the
work pattern, the scheduled days and whether the employment relationship
continues — so do not compute it from clock-in totals alone.

## "All-inclusive pay" does not delete statutory allowances

Small employers often write a contract folding overtime and other
allowances into the base salary. A line saying "inclusive of all
allowances" does not extinguish the obligations.

For such an arrangement to hold up, the base pay and the allowances have
to be distinguishable, the expected overtime hours identifiable, and
actual hours verifiable. Where the amount paid falls short of the
statutory allowances actually earned, the difference remains payable.

This matters more once you pass five employees, because the premium rates
for overtime, night and holiday work start to apply. If growth is
plausible, separate attendance records and pay components from the first
hire rather than untangling them later.

## Severance starts at one year

An employee with one year or more of continuous service whose contractual
hours average 15 or more a week over four weeks is entitled to severance
under the Employee Retirement Benefit Security Act. The conventional
severance scheme pays at least 30 days of average wages per year of
service — which is why the payroll model above accrues roughly one
twelfth of annual pay each month.

Two things employers get wrong here. **Splitting severance into monthly
salary and labelling it as such does not discharge the obligation** —
the statutory requirements of a retirement benefit scheme have to be met.
And severance can be run as a defined benefit or defined contribution
retirement pension rather than the lump-sum scheme; the choice is
structural and worth making before the first anniversary, not after.

On separation, wages and severance are settled **within 14 days** of the
event, extendable by agreement in particular circumstances.

## What changes at five employees

Headcount is not "who showed up today." It is calculated under the Labor
Standards Act from the aggregate number of workers used over a period
against the number of operating days.

| | Under 5 | 5 or more |
|---|---|---|
| Written employment contract | Applies | Applies |
| Minimum wage | Applies | Applies |
| Paid weekly holiday | Applies | Applies |
| Rest breaks | Applies | Applies |
| Advance notice of dismissal | Applies | Applies |
| Severance pay | Applies | Applies |
| Overtime / night / holiday premiums | **Not applied** | Applies |
| Statutory annual paid leave | **Not applied** | Applies |
| General restriction on dismissal | Partly excluded | Applies |
| Unfair dismissal relief at the Labor Relations Commission | **Not available** | Available |

Going from four employees to five is not an incremental hire. It is the
point at which overtime becomes a priced obligation, leave becomes a
statutory entitlement, and dismissal acquires a forum for challenge.
Budget the fifth hire with that in mind.

## Foreign employees: status before contract

Before drafting the contract, confirm that the candidate's residence
status permits the intended work.

E-7 is tied to an approved occupation and workplace; where the actual job
or the employer differs, an immigration step may have to come first.
F-series statuses do not all carry the same scope — F-4 in particular
restricts certain simple-labour occupations, so "it's an F visa" is not a
finding. And your own business registration being in order does not
authorise you to employ someone whose status does not cover the work.

If you are still deciding how to structure the business itself, the
liability and tax comparison is in
[sole proprietor versus corporation](/business/sole-proprietor-vs-corporation-korea/).
Where the person you are hiring is currently invoicing you as a
contractor, read
[freelancing in Korea legally](/business/freelancing-in-korea-legally/)
first — misclassification is decided on how the work is actually
directed, not on what the agreement is called.

## Paying salary starts a tax process too

Paying wages makes you a withholding agent. Income tax and local income
tax are withheld and remitted on schedule, and the employee's share of
the insurances is deducted while yours is added.

## Why the records matter more than the number

With one or two employees it is tempting to run everything from a
spreadsheet and bank transfers. The cost of that appears at separation or
in a dispute.

If the contract, the clock-in records, the payslips, the transfer
history, the overtime log, the leave record and the insurance filings do
not agree with each other, the exercise becomes recalculating several
years of wages from whichever record is most credible — and the employer
holding the weaker paper trail loses that argument.

There is no lighter labour-law regime for foreign owners. The moment you
hire one person you are an employer, managing contract, wages, social
insurance, tax and severance simultaneously.

Which makes the important question at the first hire not *how much*.
It is what that pay is made of, which hours it covers, and whether you
can prove both on the same basis every month.
