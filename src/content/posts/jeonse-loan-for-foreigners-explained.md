Start with the sentence you will hear most often: *"foreigners can't get a
jeonse loan in Korea."* As a description of the current market, it is
simply wrong — banks run foreigner-specific jeonse loan products today,
with published eligibility lists and published caps. But the opposite
assumption — that you can walk into any branch and use the same products
Koreans use, on the same terms — is not accurate either.

A foreigner's jeonse loan executes only when several things line up at
once: the bank's own credit screen, a guarantee institution willing to
underwrite you, verifiable Korean income, your residence status and its
remaining period, and the legal state of the property itself. This guide
walks through how those pieces actually interact — including the parts
that most English-language explanations get backwards.

One framing error to clear immediately: "if the guarantor approves, the
bank must lend." HF — the Korea Housing Finance Corporation — states
plainly that meeting its guarantee requirements does not oblige the bank
to lend; the final credit decision runs through the bank's own scoring
and internal rules. The guarantor absorbs part of the bank's credit
risk. It does not replace the bank's judgment.

## A jeonse loan is not a mortgage in disguise

In a mortgage, the bank takes the house as collateral. In a jeonse loan,
you do not own the house — so the bank cannot.

What you do own is a legal claim: the right to get your deposit back
from the landlord when the lease ends (the deposit-return claim,
임차보증금반환채권). The bank secures the loan against *that*, by taking a
pledge (질권) over the claim or an assignment (채권양도) of it. Where a
guarantee institution — SGI, HF, or HUG — joins the structure, it
underwrites a defined slice of the credit risk on top.

So a jeonse loan is really four things bolted together:

**your repayment capacity + the deposit-return claim + the guarantee +
the property's title condition.**

Weakness in any one of the four can sink the whole structure, which is
why the approval process feels more involved than a simple credit loan.
If the jeonse system itself is still unfamiliar, read
[how jeonse and wolse actually work](/cost-of-living/jeonse-vs-wolse-korean-rental-system/)
first — a jeonse deposit is, in substance, credit you extend to the
landlord, and the loan finances that credit.

## What you need first is not the ARC — it is a Korean financial record

Explanations of foreigner jeonse loans usually list three conditions:
ARC, income, visa. Directionally fine — but none of these is a uniform
legal requirement, and the real products are far more specific.

Take the clearest published example. KB Kookmin Bank's **WELCOME PLUS
jeonse loan**, running as of 2026, targets foreign residents and
overseas Koreans and requires, among other things:

- **Korean income you can document for three months or more**;
- eligibility for an **SGI (Seoul Guarantee Insurance) credit insurance
  certificate** — the guarantee layer of this product;
- **visa validity of at least three months** from the loan date.

Note what that last line does to a popular myth: there is no industry
rule that a foreigner needs a year or more of remaining stay. A public,
mainstream product draws the line at three months. A short remaining
period does have consequences — the bank may match the loan term to it
conservatively and weigh your renewal prospects — and other banks or
guarantors can be stricter. But "12 months minimum" is not a rule of
the market; it is folklore.

## The SGI-backed route is the clearest path on the market

Of the structures a foreigner can actually access today, the most
clearly documented is the **SGI-backed bank product**.

KB's foreigner product lends against an SGI credit insurance
certificate, up to **₩200,000,000**, with the executed amount set by the
smallest of three published caps:

1. the **₩200M product maximum**;
2. **80% of the jeonse deposit**;
3. senior liens plus the jeonse loan together staying within **80% of
   the home's market value**.

And then a fourth, unpublished cap sits on top: the bank's own credit
assessment of you — income, existing debt, credit file. Whichever of the
four numbers is smallest is your actual loan.

Run the example: on a ₩300M deposit, 80% is ₩240M — but the product
stops at ₩200M first. If the bank then reads your income and debts and
concludes it will lend ₩160M, your limit is ₩160M. The visa never
entered that final arithmetic.

## Visa-by-visa verdict tables are mostly wrong

The most common error in English-language content on this topic is the
tidy table: *F visas — yes; E-7 — conditional; D-2 students — no.*

The published eligibility list of KB's SGI-backed foreigner product, as
of 2026, includes not only **E-7, F-2, F-4, F-5 and F-6** but also
**D-2 (study), D-3 (training), D-7 (intra-company transfer), D-8
(investment), D-9 (trade management) and D-10 (job seeking)** — plus
A-1 through A-3, E-1 through E-5, F-1 and H-2. **E-9** is notably absent
from the current published list.

So a table declaring "students: impossible" contradicts a mainstream
bank's published terms. The accurate mental model:

- The **visa decides whether you can enter a given product at all** —
  each product publishes its own list.
- Within an eligible status, **the financial file decides the outcome**.
  An E-7 engineer with ₩100M of declared salary, five years at one
  employer and a real [NICE/KCB credit file](/loans/korean-credit-score-for-foreigners-nice-kcb/)
  and an E-7 arrival with three months in Korea and no declared income
  are entirely different borrowers to the same product.
- Symmetrically, D-2 is not legally barred — but a student without
  documentable Korean income will usually fail the income screen, which
  is a different rejection than "your visa is ineligible."

## HF, HUG and SGI are not three flavors of the same thing

Grouping the three guarantee institutions as "the jeonse guarantee
options" hides differences that matter specifically to foreigners.

**HF (Korea Housing Finance Corporation)** provides jeonse *loan*
guarantees to banks — currently for deposits up to **₩700M in the
capital region / ₩500M elsewhere**, with a general guarantee ceiling of
**₩400M** — and states that its guarantee never obliges the bank to
lend. But do not confuse HF's loan guarantee with HF's *return*
guarantee:

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">⚠️ Watch out</p>
  <p>HF's deposit-<em>return</em> guarantee (Jeonse Jikim, 전세지킴보증)
  currently requires <strong>all tenants on the lease to be Korean
  nationals</strong>. A foreigner can sit inside an HF-guaranteed loan
  structure and still be unable to use HF's return protection. For
  return guarantees, foreigners generally look to HUG — as covered in
  <a href="/cost-of-living/rental-deposit-protection-korea/">the deposit-protection guide</a>.</p>
</div>

**HUG (Korea Housing & Urban Guarantee Corporation)** runs a combined
product — the jeonse safe-loan guarantee (전세금안심대출보증) — that wraps
a repayment guarantee on the loan *and* a return guarantee on your
deposit into one structure. Its loan-side special guarantee currently
runs at an **80% ratio in the capital region and regulated areas, 90%
elsewhere**. HUG accepts foreign tenants in ways HF's return product
does not — but "HUG accepts foreigners" is not a blanket statement
either; eligibility runs product by product and bank by bank.

**SGI (Seoul Guarantee Insurance)** is the commercial insurer behind
the foreigner-specific bank products described above — currently the
most clearly documented door for a foreign tenant.

The efficient order for a foreigner, then: start from **banks'
foreigner-specific SGI-backed products**, and check HUG and HF
product-by-product from there — not the other way around.

## Income means recognized income — again

Everything in [how Korean lenders read foreign borrowers](/loans/can-foreigners-get-a-loan-in-korea/)
applies here, compressed: the bank lends against income it can verify
through Korean records — withholding receipts, NTS income certificates,
employment certificates, health-insurance enrollment records, salary
deposit history. KB's foreigner product asks for three months of
domestic income evidence, plus your registration certificate, the
fixed-date lease contract, and proof you have paid at least 5% of the
deposit as the contract payment.

Cash salary with minimal tax filing creates a gap between what you earn
and what the bank recognizes. Freelancers on 3.3% withholding are read
through their filed returns, not their bank inflows — the mechanics are
in [the freelancing guide](/business/freelancing-in-korea-legally/).
For a jeonse loan, the recognized figure is the one that survives.

## The landlord: consent is not always required, cooperation always is

The loan's security is a pledge over — or assignment of — your
deposit-return claim, which pulls the landlord into the process whether
they like it or not. At lease end, the portion of the deposit tied to
the loan flows under the bank's rights, not simply back to you.

The legal fine print differs by structure: under a *notice-type*
assignment, the landlord's active consent may not be strictly required —
receiving the notice can suffice. But HUG's combined guarantee, for
example, expects the landlord to receive the assignment notice (or sign
a consent), and to answer contract-verification contact. A landlord who
won't take the registered mail or return the verification call can
stall the loan in practice, whatever the theory says.

  <p class="callout-title">💡 Tip</p>
  <p>Before signing, ask the landlord the precise question, not the vague

## Put a financing-contingency clause in the contract

Because a foreigner's product menu is narrower, the sequence
*sign first, arrange the loan later* is riskier for you than for a
local. SGI can decline the certificate; the property's senior liens can
breach the 80% cap; the income screen can fail. If any of that happens
after you have paid the contract deposit, that money is exposed.

The mitigation is a special clause (특약) in the lease to this effect:

> If, without intent or gross negligence on the tenant's part, the
> jeonse loan or the associated institutional guarantee cannot be
> approved, the landlord agrees to return the received contract payment
> and rescind the lease.

It does not guarantee approval — nothing does — but it converts
"financing failed" from a lost deposit into an unwound contract.

## The loan is not deposit protection

Loan approval does not make your deposit safe. These are two different
instruments: the loan **finances** the deposit; a return guarantee
**transfers the risk** of not getting it back. Even with the bank's
pledge sitting on the claim, a deposit in an over-leveraged property
(the *kkangtong* scenario) is still exposed — the pledge secures the
bank's position against you, not your position against the landlord.

So the tenant-side homework from
[the deposit-protection guide](/cost-of-living/rental-deposit-protection-korea/)
still applies in full: check the registry for senior liens, run the
deposit against the home's value, move in and report your address
promptly (for a foreigner, the stay-place change report on your
registration), get the fixed date on the contract — that combination is
what builds opposing power and priority repayment under the Housing
Lease Protection Act — and evaluate a HUG return guarantee separately.

## Maturity management: three calendars, one problem

Foreigner jeonse loans typically run in 1–2 year terms matched to the
lease. KB's product sets the initial term at three months to two years,
extendable to a **maximum of ten years** — which is emphatically not a
promise of ten years. At every renewal the bank can re-verify your
status, income, credit, the lease renewal itself, and the guarantor's
willingness to extend.

An E-7 borrower who changed jobs, or whose extension is pending, is a
different risk at renewal than at origination. Even an F-5 holder with
vanished income and new delinquencies has no automatic rollover. The
practical rule: your **visa expiry, lease maturity and loan maturity are
one problem, not three** — put them in one calendar and start checking
all three at least two to three months before the earliest date.

## The bottom line

A foreigner's jeonse loan is not an impossible product. It is a product
where the guarantor's underwriting, your residence status, your
recognized Korean income, and the bank's ability to safely secure the
deposit-return claim must all pass at once — and where the decisive
question is never just *"what visa do I hold?"* It is: **does a
currently offered guarantee structure accept my status, how much of my
income does Korea recognize, and can the bank safely secure this
particular home's deposit claim?** Answer those three before you sign,
and the loan stops being a gamble.
