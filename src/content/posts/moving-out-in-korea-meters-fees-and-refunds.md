Moving day in Korea is not a day for carrying boxes. It is an administrative day
on which several contracts end, several meters stop, and one payment you have
been making for someone else becomes reclaimable.

The parts that cost money if skipped all sit in the same few hours.

## 1. Start by stopping the meters

Water, electricity and gas billed on a monthly cycle will not line up with the
day you actually leave.

So the first job on moving day is to read each meter and photograph it. Try to
get three things visible or verifiable in the image:

- the meter reading
- the meter number
- the time the photograph was taken

That is what lets you and the landlord — or you and the incoming tenant — draw a
clean line between two periods of use.

<div class="callout callout-note">
  <p class="callout-title">You do not inherit the last tenant's bills</p>
  <p>Electricity, water and gas are settled against the user or contract holder
  for the period of their use; a new tenant does not automatically take on a
  previous tenant's debt. What does happen, often, is that the reading and the
  change of contract holder are not cleanly separated at handover — and then it
  becomes an argument about who used what and when. The photographs are what stop
  the argument, not a rule about succession.</p>
</div>

## 2. Water is settled locally, so there is no one national procedure

Water supply is run by each local authority's waterworks, which means there is no
single nationwide phone process for it.

In Seoul, the Arisu moving settlement service handles the charge up to the point
the user changes. As of 2026 the procedure has been improved so that cancelling
the card auto-debit can be handled at the same time.

Wherever you are, the same four steps run in the same order:

<div class="steps">
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">1</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Final meter reading</strong>, photographed.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">2</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Moving settlement</strong> with the local waterworks.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">3</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Cancel the auto-debit</strong>, or it keeps running against the old address.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">4</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Register in your name</strong> at the new address.</div></div>
</div>

Step 3 is the one people discover two months later on a card statement.

## 3. Electricity: first work out who bills you

The final reading matters here too, but there is a question in front of it.

**Individually metered housing** — a detached house or a unit billed directly —
is settled with KEPCO or the body managing the electricity supply contract,
along with the change of contract holder.

**Apartments where electricity is inside the management fee** are settled by the
management office as part of the interim management settlement.

Getting this the wrong way round produces either a duplicate settlement or none
at all, so establish which one applies before making any calls.

## 4. Gas needs an appointment, not a phone call on the day

Gas is not only a meter reading. Disconnecting the cooker, capping the
intermediate valve and the related safety work generally require a service visit.

Seoul City Gas asks a departing customer to notify the customer centre of the
moving date and a contact number in advance, book a service manager visit, have
the cooker and intermediate valve disconnected and capped, and then have the
final consumption read and the charge settled.

At the new address, check the meter and whether the previous user left anything
unpaid, then handle the supply contract and the change of name.

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">Same-day may not exist where you live</p>
  <p>Availability depends on the local gas company, and some cannot take a
  same-day booking at all. Several days' notice is the safe default. A moving day
  that ends with the gas not disconnected is a moving day that has to have someone
  come back to it.</p>
</div>

## 5. Ask for an interim settlement, not the final bill

In an apartment or officetel, the move-out date almost never coincides with the
regular billing date.

Ask the management office for an **interim management fee settlement as at the
move-out date**. It separates out general management, electricity, water,
heating, parking and other usage charges for the period you actually occupied.

Then present it when the deposit is being returned. Unpaid management fees are
the most frequently cited reason for holding back part of a deposit, and a
settlement statement removes the reason before it is raised.

## 6. The long-term repair reserve is not your cost

This is the money most tenants leave behind.

The long-term repair reserve in multi-family housing funds replacement and repair
of major building systems, and **in principle the owner bears it**.

In practice it sits on the management bill and the tenant pays it every month
along with everything else.

When the lease ends, the tenant can claim back from the owner what they paid on
the owner's behalf. And you can evidence it: ask the management office for a
payment certificate, which the management body is required to issue.

<div class="steps">
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">1</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Request the payment certificate</strong> from the management office before you leave.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">2</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Total the amount</strong> across the whole tenancy, not just the last year.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">3</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Claim it from the owner</strong> — it is a claim against them, not the management office.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">4</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Settle it with the deposit</strong>, in the same transfer, so nothing is left outstanding.</div></div>
</div>

Over a two-year tenancy this is not a trivial sum, and nobody will offer it.

## 7. Internet and TV do not follow you

An internet or IPTV line does not transfer to the new address by itself. You
apply to the carrier for a change of installation location and book an engineer
visit.

There can be a relocation installation charge, and weekend or evening slots can
carry extra cost — which is a reason to book one to two weeks ahead rather than
to discover the earliest slot is after your moving date.

The case worth knowing about is where the new building has no line from your
carrier, or installation is technically impossible. Depending on the terms,
reduction or waiver of the early termination discount clawback can be available.

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">"Inconvenient to install" is not "cannot be installed"</p>
  <p>Not seeing a cable, or a building owner preferring a different wiring
  arrangement, does not produce an automatic waiver. What counts is whether the
  carrier determined on a site survey that the service genuinely cannot be
  provided — whether an alternative technology works, whether the building's
  wiring supports installation. If you are going to ask for a waiver, keep the new
  lease, the relocation reference number, the engineer visit record and whatever
  the carrier said about why installation failed. The contract mechanics are in
  <a href="/cost-of-living/moving-leaving-and-ending-a-korean-internet-contract/">moving, leaving and ending a Korean internet contract</a>.</p>
</div>

## What the old place owes you

| Item | Where it comes from | What to get |
|---|---|---|
| Water, electricity, gas | Usage to the move-out date | Meter photographs, settlement |
| Management fees | Interim settlement, not the last bill | Itemised settlement statement |
| Long-term repair reserve | Paid for the owner all along | Payment certificate, then a claim |
| Deposit | The lease | Return, net of a settlement you can check |
| Internet relocation | The carrier's terms | Reference number and visit record |

None of it is automatic and none of it is offered. Every row is something a
departing tenant has to ask for on a day when they are busy with something else —
which is why it is worth having the list before the van arrives.

The other half of a Korean move is not about money at all. It is about which
address is registered where, and by when — and for a foreign resident, one of
those deadlines carries a criminal penalty rather than an administrative one.
