When something goes wrong at work in Korea, the first task is not gathering
evidence. It is **classifying the case**, because unpaid wages and unfair
dismissal go to different institutions, follow different procedures, and run on
different clocks.

Get that wrong and you can win the argument you started while quietly losing the
one you did not.

<p class="hand">Nationality is not an input here. Article 6 says so directly.</p>

## Article 6 first

The Labor Standards Act prohibits discriminatory treatment of working
conditions on grounds of nationality. Where a person is an employee, wages,
severance and dismissal are judged by Korean labour law — E-7, E-9, F-2, F-4 and
F-6 alike.

What differs for a foreign worker is not the standard. It is that a labour
problem and an immigration problem arrive together, which is the subject of the
afternoon guide.

## Unpaid wages: the labour office

Unpaid salary, overtime, night and holiday premiums, unused leave pay, severance
and final settlement all go to the **local labour office** as a complaint to a
labour inspector.

The inspector investigates both sides and compares the contract, the wage
records, attendance records, bank transfers and the employer's account. Where
arrears are confirmed, the employer can be directed to pay, and a breach of the
Act can lead into criminal process.

### Complaint and accusation are not the same thing

| | Aim |
|---|---|
| Complaint (진정) | Get the money — investigation and a direction to pay |
| Accusation (고소) | Punish the breach — criminal treatment of the employer |

Starting with a criminal accusation is not required. In practice a complaint,
confirmation of the arrears, and a direction to pay is the ordinary path, with
criminal or civil steps following if payment still does not come.

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">But do not let it sit</p>
  <p>Limitation periods and the progress of any criminal process differ case by
  case, and the substitute payment route below has a deadline of its own that
  runs from leaving rather than from the arrears.</p>
</div>

## Evidence decides wage cases

Employers dispute wage claims with three sentences: you did not work those
hours, nobody instructed that overtime, we already paid you in cash. Each is
answered by records rather than recollection.

<div class="steps">
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">1</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Contract and payslips</strong> — the agreed terms and what was actually stated as paid.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">2</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Bank records</strong> — what arrived, and when. The strongest answer to "we paid you in cash".</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">3</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Attendance and access records, rosters, work schedules.</strong></div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">4</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Messages and email</strong> showing overtime being instructed, and the work being done.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">5</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Termination or dismissal notice</strong>, and any severance calculation.</div></div>
</div>

Gather these while still employed if you can. Access to systems tends to end on
the same day the dispute begins.

## Confirmation of unpaid wages is the document that unlocks the rest

Where an inspector confirms arrears, a **confirmation of unpaid wages and the
employer** can be issued. It is not a counselling note — it evidences the
employer and the amount for what comes next.

```
arrears confirmed
  → confirmation issued
  → simplified substitute payment, or civil enforcement
```

## The substitute payment is a backstop, not a guarantee

Under the Wage Claim Guarantee Act the state can pay confirmed arrears the
employer has not settled.

For a former employee using the labour office route, the complaint must as a
rule be filed within
[one year of the day after leaving](/tracked/substitute-payment-claim-period/).

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">Two ways people lose this</p>
  <p>Waiting to see whether the employer eventually pays, until the year has
  gone. And assuming the state covers everything — the scheme pays specified
  wages up to a ceiling, so a large arrears figure is not fully underwritten.
  Separate routes exist through a court judgment or order, and certain employees
  still in work can qualify on their own conditions, so "I am still employed,
  therefore nothing applies" is also wrong.</p>
</div>

## Unfair dismissal: a different body, and a hard deadline

Whether a dismissal was justified goes to the **Labor Relations Commission**,
and the application must be made within
[three months](/tracked/unfair-dismissal-relief-deadline/) of the dismissal.

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">Put the date in a calendar the day you are dismissed</p>
  <p>This is not a wage complaint, where facts can be assembled at a considered
  pace. Three months runs from the dismissal — not from the day you learn a
  remedy exists, and not from the day the wage complaint concludes.</p>
</div>

## But check the headcount before you plan around it

The Labor Standards Act applies principally at workplaces with
[five or more ordinary employees](/tracked/annual-leave-minimum-workplace-size/),
and the Commission's unfair dismissal relief sits inside that framework.

So the sequence is not "dismissed → Commission". It is:

```
headcount at the workplace
  → does the Act's dismissal framework apply?
  → which remedy is actually available
```

Below the threshold, advance notice obligations and civil claims arising from
the termination can still be considered — and unpaid wages remain a separate
matter that does not turn on the same question.

## Written notice is a requirement, not a formality

Article 27 requires the employer to give the **grounds and the date in
writing**, and ties the effect of the dismissal to that notice.

So "don't come in tomorrow", delivered verbally, is not a clean dismissal.
Whether an electronic message satisfies the requirement in a given case depends
on its content and how it was delivered — the question being whether the
employee could clearly ascertain the grounds and the timing.

## Notice pay is not a price for dismissing you

[Thirty days' notice, or thirty days' ordinary wages in lieu](/tracked/dismissal-notice-period/),
satisfies Article 26. It says nothing about whether there was a justifiable
reason.

```
30 days' pay received   ≠   the dismissal was lawful
```

Accepting it concedes nothing, and it is not a settlement of the dismissal
question. Believing otherwise is the most common reason the three-month window
passes unused.

## What the Commission can order

Where a dismissal is found unfair, the ordinary remedies are **reinstatement**
and payment of **wages equivalent** to what would have been earned during the
dismissal period.

An employee who does not want to return can instead be awarded a monetary
payment of not less than that wage equivalent. And where reinstatement has
become impossible — a contract term ending, or retirement age reached — the
Commission can still rule on the dismissal and order payment.

## The two tracks, side by side

| | Unpaid wages | Unfair dismissal |
|---|---|---|
| Body | Local labour office | Labor Relations Commission |
| Question | Was the money paid | Was the dismissal justified |
| Procedure | Complaint or accusation | Application for relief |
| Investigated by | Labour inspector | Commission investigation and hearing |
| Key evidence | Contract, pay, attendance | Dismissal notice, disciplinary and appraisal records |
| Outcome | Confirmation, direction to pay, criminal process | Reinstatement, wage equivalent, monetary award |
| Deadline | Substitute payment route: 1 year from leaving | **3 months from the dismissal** |
| Threshold | — | 5 or more ordinary employees |
