Your manager criticises your work in a meeting. Uncomfortable, and not illegal.
The next day he says foreign employees should not question Korean supervisors.
Your name comes off the team chat. Colleagues are told not to help you. You are
sent on personal errands, with a hint about what happens if you refuse.

Somewhere between the first day and the fourth, this stopped being management.
Korean law has a name for where it landed — 직장 내 괴롭힘 — but the law does
not ask how it felt. It asks three questions, and then a fourth one that comes
before all of them.

## The threshold that comes first

The harassment articles generally apply to workplaces regularly employing
{{fig:workplace-harassment-employer-threshold}} workers. Below that line the
specific framework generally does not reach, and a complaint built on it meets
an applicability problem before anyone considers the conduct.

Two things people get wrong here. The count is of workers *regularly*
employed, under the Act's own method — not the number who happened to be
rostered the day of the incident, so fluctuating staffing needs checking
rather than assuming. And a separate, higher threshold governs the duty to
prepare and file employment rules, 취업규칙, which must address harassment
prevention. A company of seven has no rulebook duty and still cannot ignore a
report.

Below five, the conduct itself may still be unlawful under some other
provision — violence, threats, sexual harassment, nationality discrimination,
unpaid wages, unlawful dismissal. The route changes; the protection does not
vanish.

## Superiority is not only a job title

A department head has positional authority. So, in practice, does a senior
colleague who controls shifts, training or access to information, or a group
of coworkers acting against an isolated one. The Ministry's guidance treats
both positional and relational superiority as relevant.

Language dependence belongs in that assessment. A worker who relies on a
supervisor to understand safety instructions and workplace rules is in a
relationship the supervisor can exploit — by withholding what the worker needs
to do the job safely, or by using the gap to humiliate.

But being foreign does not establish the element on its own, and sharing a job
title with the other person does not rule it out. The facts decide.

## Where legitimate supervision ends

| Situation | The question an investigation asks |
| --- | --- |
| Feedback on a defective product | Ordinarily a management function |
| Public humiliation with personal insults | Did the manner exceed acceptable limits? |
| Repeated removal from work communications | Was relational power used to worsen the environment? |
| A manager's private errands | Was there any legitimate connection to the job? |
| Threats involving immigration status | Was dependence used to coerce? |
| Reassignment after a documented safety incident | Was there a genuine, proportionate business reason? |

No row decides itself. An instruction can be entirely work-related and still
become inappropriate in how it is delivered or enforced, and a single severe
incident can warrant investigation where a pattern is absent. What the law
does not do is convert every offensive remark into a statutory violation
because the recipient was offended.

## Build the timeline, not the adjectives

The quality of the evidence matters more than the length of the complaint.
Keep a factual record: date, approximate time, place, who was present, what
was said, and — this is the part people omit — what actually changed at work
afterwards. Removal from a channel, a reassignment, a cancelled shift.

| Date | Event | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 5 Oct | Nationality-related insults in a meeting | Notes, witnesses |
| 8 Oct | Instructions for unrelated personal errands | Messages |
| 13 Oct | Excluded from shift information | Team-chat history |
| 19 Oct | Reassignment threatened after a complaint | Messages, schedule change |
| 22 Oct | Written complaint submitted | Submission record |

Preserve conversations whole rather than cropping them into the most damning
line, and do not edit a screenshot. On recording: taking part in a conversation
you record is legally different from intercepting one between other people, so
do not assume a general right to record the workplace — and never obtain
evidence by getting into someone else's account or records. Evidence gathered
unlawfully becomes the employer's case rather than yours.

## Who may report, and what the employer then owes

Anyone who becomes aware of workplace harassment may report it — the reporter
does not have to be the person harassed. And the employer's duty arises on
becoming aware, not on receipt of a particular form, so a company that already
knows cannot wait for paperwork.

| Stage | The employer's obligation |
| --- | --- |
| Report received | Investigate objectively, without delay |
| During the investigation | Protect the affected worker where necessary |
| Harassment confirmed | Act on the perpetrator; take appropriate measures for the worker on request |
| Throughout | No retaliation; confidentiality from those involved |

A written complaint is still worth making, because it fixes what was reported
and when. Keep it factual: the conduct, why it went beyond normal supervision,
and a request for an objective investigation and protection from retaliation.
If the complaint would have to go to the person accused, ask whether there is
another designated recipient.

## Protection must not be the punishment

Protective measures during an investigation can include a change of workplace
or paid leave — but they must not be imposed on the affected worker against
their wishes.

This matters because the path of least resistance for a company is to move the
person who complained rather than the person complained about. Asking not to
be in direct contact with a supervisor during an investigation is one thing. A
transfer to a worse position, presented as protection, is another, and it is
the shape retaliation usually takes.

## Retaliation is criminal, and it is not your money

Dismissal or unfavourable treatment because a worker reported harassment or
was the affected worker carries up to three years' imprisonment or a fine of
up to {{fig:harassment-retaliation-criminal-fine}}.

Read that as a sanction on the responsible party. It is not compensation, and
a worker who plans around receiving it has misread the provision — anything
you recover needs its own legal basis and its own assessment. The employer's
own harassment and a failure to investigate carry separate administrative
fines, lower than this one and also not payable to you.

Nor does every later personnel action prove retaliation. The reason for the
action, and whether it connects to the report, is what gets examined.

## When to go outside the company

Where an employer fails to investigate, fails to take the required measures,
or retaliates — and where the employer is the one accused — a complaint to the
competent local labour office is the route, through the Labor Portal or in
person.

Say both halves when you go. Not "the workplace is unpleasant", but: *I
reported repeated harassment to HR on 22 October; no objective investigation
has started, and my assignment changed after the report.* The office assesses
the conduct and the employer's duties, and the second half is often the
stronger of the two. If what you need is the mechanics of taking an employer
to the labour office,
[reporting your employer as a foreign worker](/employment/reporting-your-employer-as-a-foreign-worker/)
covers that ground.

## When it is not a harassment case at all

Sexual conduct at work engages the Equal Employment Opportunity Act, with its
own definitions, duties and remedies — it is not simply another example of
bullying. Assault, stalking, threats and coercion are criminal matters. If you
are in immediate danger the number is 112, and 119 for a medical emergency. An
internal HR process is not the right sole response to violence.

Nationality discrimination is also its own ground. A requirement to use a
shared language for safety instructions can have a legitimate purpose; insults
aimed at where you are from do not acquire one by being attached to it. The
two complaints can coexist, but they are not interchangeable.

## The visa threat

An employer may say that reporting will cost you your status. A harassment
report does not, by itself, establish an immigration violation.

What is true is that your residence category and work authorisation are a
separate legal track, and some categories are employer-specific with their own
permission or notification requirements that an employment dispute does not
suspend. So preserve the threat if one is made, and confirm your own position
with immigration on 1345 rather than with a coworker's summary of the rules.
Do not resign or start work elsewhere on the strength of either.

Resigning is not a precondition for complaining, and it does not by itself
secure unemployment benefit, a new status or damages. If your status is tied
to the employer, get the immigration position straight before any irreversible
step, and keep your contract, payslips and complaint records.

<p class="hand">The strongest complaint explains what happened, why it exceeded supervision, and what the employer did after it knew.</p>
