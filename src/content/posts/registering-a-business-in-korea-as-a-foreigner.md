The first distinction a foreigner starting a business in Korea must make is
between two questions that sound identical and are not: *can I register a
business*, and *does my current visa allow me to actually run it?*

The National Tax Service's business registration is a **tax** procedure —
it registers you as a business operator. Your status of stay is an
**immigration** matter — it defines which income-generating activities you
may lawfully perform in Korea. A freshly printed business registration
certificate does not legalize business activity your visa does not permit.

Get the order right and everything downstream gets easier: confirm the
immigration side against your actual business plan first, then choose the
structure (sole proprietor vs corporation), then move through registration,
banking, tax, and financing. Invert that order and problems compound.

## Which visas allow business activity — and which need permission first

**F-5 (permanent residency)** carries the fewest restrictions on business
activity. **F-6 (marriage-based)** is also generally broad on employment
and income-generating activity. **F-2** and **F-4** can support business
activity in defined circumstances, but the permitted scope depends on the
specific sub-status — verify, don't assume.

The work-visa group is different in kind. **E-7** authorizes stay on the
basis of an approved occupation and an employment relationship; starting
your own business on the side can fall outside your permitted activity, so
check status-change or extra-activity permission (체류자격 외 활동)
*before* acting, not after.

**D-2 students** are clearer still: the Ministry of Justice restricts
student employment and income-generating activity to approved part-time
work and separately permitted activities. Registering a business and
running an online shop or a restaurant is not the same category as an
approved part-time job. **D-10** is a job-seeking status with defined
startup-preparation allowances — not a general business visa.

So checkpoint number one is not the tax office. It is whether your current
status of stay permits the actual management activity you are planning.

## D-8 is not "make a corporation, get a visa"

For a foreigner investing in a Korean corporation and managing it directly,
the flagship status is **D-8**. In the standard D-8-1 structure, the
baseline foreign-direct-investment requirement is an investment of at
least **₩100,000,000** with ownership of at least **10% of voting shares** —
Invest KOREA's guidance describes the D-8 route for foreigners bringing in
₩100M+ from abroad to establish and run a Korean corporation.

The key subtlety: ₩100M does not mean "have ₩100M sitting in a Korean
account." The money has to trace through the FDI process — foreign
investment notification, remittance or importation of the funds from
abroad, incorporation, business registration, and foreign-invested-company
registration. D-8 screening can ask for remittance confirmations,
foreign-currency purchase certificates, corporate registry extracts, the
business registration certificate, records of how the capital was used,
and the office lease. Moving ₩100M of money you already held in Korea into
a corporate account does not, by itself, complete the FDI requirement.

## D-9 sole proprietorships are a ₩300M story, not ₩100M

Here is the error most foreign-startup articles make. The thresholds differ
by structure:

| Structure | Representative investment threshold |
| --- | --- |
| D-8-1 — invest in a Korean corporation | Foreign investment ≥ ₩100M + in principle ≥ 10% voting shares |
| D-9 — independent foreign-owned sole proprietorship | Foreign investment ≥ ₩300M |
| D-8-3 — joint sole proprietorship with a Korean partner | Separate joint-investment and co-representative requirements |

Invest KOREA's guidance is explicit that a foreigner operating a private
business that is *not* a joint venture with a domestic business falls under
D-9 when the investment is ₩300M or more.

And the flip side matters just as much: these amounts are **visa/FDI
thresholds, not a general minimum capital for foreign-owned businesses.**
An F-5 or F-6 holder opening a small business has no reason to apply the
D-8 or D-9 figures to themselves — they need no investment visa.

## Sole proprietor vs corporation: liability and funding split before tax does

For a small service business or one with modest early revenue, a sole
proprietorship (개인사업자) is simple — to open, to account for, and to
close. Profit and loss flow directly to you, and so does **liability**: the
business's obligations are personally yours.

A corporation (법인) is a separate legal person. Shareholders' liability is
in principle limited to their contribution, company assets separate from
the CEO's personal assets, and the structure cleanly supports investment,
share transfers, corporate-name financing, and dealings with overseas
affiliates. Invest KOREA frames the trade the same way: sole
proprietorships are procedurally simple but fundraising-constrained;
corporations are more complex to establish but stronger on financing and
the separation of ownership and management.

Tax differs too — sole-proprietor profit runs through personal
comprehensive income tax, corporate profit through corporate tax — but
resist the folk rule "above revenue X, a corporation always saves tax."
Taking corporate profit out as salary or dividends triggers a second,
personal-level tax; using corporate money personally creates 가지급금
(provisional payments) and deemed-bonus problems. The conversion decision
is really three joint questions: how much profit will stay retained in the
company, how large will future investment and borrowing be, and how much
must you personally extract as income?

## Registration is an administrative step — not a financial identity

The NTS requires business registration per place of business within 20 days
of starting (application before starting is allowed). Sole proprietors file
the application with the lease agreement and any required licenses, plus —
for foreigners — an ARC or passport copy. Corporations file incorporation
plus business-registration forms, the corporate-name lease, and the
shareholder/investor schedule.

But a registration certificate does not make a bank treat you as an
established business. The tax office and the bank are answering different
questions — which brings us to the actual first financial hurdle.

## The real banking hurdle is AML and purpose verification

A new foreign-owned business's first financial problem is usually not the
loan. It is the **account**.

Under KYC and anti-money-laundering obligations, banks can verify the real
representative, beneficial owners, business substance, transaction
purpose, expected volumes, and source of funds. A foreign CEO, or a company
expecting frequent overseas transfers, should expect more verification, not
less. Walking in with only the registration certificate and corporate seal
certificate and expecting a full-function account is the standard rookie
error — the bank may probe the actual premises, the shareholder and
beneficial-ownership structure, the business purpose, projected revenue,
and contracts. Newly formed companies with zero revenue, shared-office
addresses with vague activity, or repeated large inbound foreign transfers
get the most scrutiny.

The same [limited-account system](/banking/how-to-open-a-bank-account-in-korea-as-a-foreigner/)
that applies to personal accounts applies here, and the lifting logic is
identical: **substance, not time.** "Use it a few months and it unlocks" is
not how it works. What works is evidence of a real business — tax
invoices, contracts, settlement inflows, VAT filings, the office lease,
payroll records. A pre-revenue company substitutes supply contracts,
purchase orders, licenses, the lease, and capital-injection records. If the
bank asks for a site visit, the point is not hastily mounting a sign — the
premises need real workspace, equipment, inventory, or staff consistent
with the registered business. Manufacturing fake contracts to escape the
limit is the one move worse than staying limited.

## A business loan is not a right that comes with registration

The registration certificate is an operating document, not a credit file.
What a lender needs is a **repayment source**.

For sole proprietors: the owner's KCB/NICE credit file, business tenure,
declared income, VAT-reported and card revenue, existing debt, and tax
compliance. For corporations: financial statements, revenue, operating
cash flow, leverage, tax payment history, and collateral. A new
corporation has almost no credit history of its own, so the CEO's personal
credit and guarantee weigh heavily — which is one more reason to
[build your personal Korean credit base first](/loans/can-foreigners-get-a-loan-in-korea/).

For foreign CEOs one factor is added: stay stability. An imminent visa
expiry, or an unclear basis for continuing to run the business in Korea,
gives the lender a management-continuity question to resolve — though, as
with personal loans, no industry-wide "months remaining" cutoff exists.
What underwriting ultimately wants is a structure in which **the business
continues and the cash that repays the loan is generated in Korea.**

## Policy funds and guarantee institutions don't decide on "foreign CEO" alone

Whether a foreign-owned business can use regional credit-guarantee
foundations or small-business agency (소상공인시장진흥공단) programs
depends on each program's own eligibility and guarantee criteria. Being
registered does not qualify you for every program; being foreign does not
disqualify you from all of them. Conditions vary by location, industry,
tenure, headcount, revenue, arrears, and the CEO's residence status — and
program names and limits change, which is why this guide deliberately does
not hard-code any. Judge against the institution's current notice at the
time you apply.

## Leasing preserves a new company's cash — but not the CEO's exposure

Foreign CEOs commonly lease vehicles or equipment in the corporate name
right after incorporation, and the core benefit is real: tens of millions
of won stay liquid as working capital instead of converting into assets on
day one.

But a thin-credit new corporation should expect the lessor to want a larger
deposit, CEO credit support, or extra documentation — a corporate contract
does not sever the financing from the CEO's personal credit. And for cars,
skip the "lease payments are fully deductible" folklore: company-vehicle
costs face depreciation-equivalent caps, business-use ratios, and
business-use insurance requirements, all covered in our
[car lease guide](/cars/car-lease-in-korea-for-foreigners-real-cost/).
Classify business leasing correctly: a financing tool that preserves early
cash while using the asset — not a tax product.

## Separate FDI money, company money, and your money from day one

If you built the company on notified foreign investment, the post-founding
flows deserve stricter discipline than a domestic founder needs. When
notified investment capital, operating revenue, and the CEO's living
expenses mix in one account, explaining the capital's origin and the
accounting later becomes genuinely difficult.

Every payment from company to CEO needs a defined character — salary,
dividend, expense reimbursement, or loan. Every payment from CEO to
company likewise — additional capital, temporary advance (가수금), or
loan. And when a foreign-invested company later repatriates dividends or
recovers its investment, the original FDI notification and remittance
records come back into play. Separating the corporate and personal
accounts from the start is not bookkeeping tidiness; it is **building the
base file that every future bank, tax, and FX review will read.**

## As the business grows, the reasons to incorporate change

Early on, a sole proprietorship's low overhead wins. As staff grow, larger
counterparties sign, outside investment arrives, or borrowing scales, the
weight shifts to liability separation and funding capacity — at which
point conversion to a corporation is a financial restructuring, not a tax
trick.

A few stage-specific cautions:

- **Long-term settlers** should design visa and business together: a D-8
  operator building business results and residence history toward
  long-term status is running a different financial structure than an F-6
  holder co-running a business with a Korean spouse.
- **Do not borrow a spouse's credit** by adding them as a nominal
  co-representative or co-proprietor. Co-proprietors take on real income,
  tax, and debt consequences in proportion to their stake. Keep family
  relationships and business contracts distinct.
- **Students and short-term residents** should be the most conservative of
  all: being able to generate income and being permitted to run a business
  are different questions. Confirm the lawful scope first, manage the
  basics — account, cards, remittance costs — inside it, and expand into
  business finance, property, leasing, and loans only after reaching a
  long-term settlement stage.

What Korean financial institutions demand from a foreign-owned business is
not complicated: **who actually runs it, where the money came from, what
business generates how much cash, and whether that cash can service the
debt.** The visa, the registration certificate, the corporate registry,
the bank account, the tax filings, and the loan look like independent
administrative events — but in a real credit review they are read as one
continuous record. Keeping that record unbroken from day one is the whole
strategy.
