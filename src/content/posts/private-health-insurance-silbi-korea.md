The claim that long-stay foreigners cannot buy Korean private actual-loss
medical insurance (실손의료보험, "silbi") is simply wrong. A foreigner
with an alien-registration number is identifiable to Korean insurers,
and companies operate foreigner verification and contract-management
systems — KB Insurance, for one, documents verifying foreign customers
by the name and number on the ARC or domestic residence-report card.

One correction before anything else, though: **there is no statutory
"three requirements" — ARC + NHIS + the right visa — that guarantees
silbi acceptance.** Alien registration matters for identification, and
NHIS status shapes how claims pay, but final acceptance is each
insurer's underwriting call across age, health history, occupation, and
stay circumstances.

And the timing matters in 2026: the **5th generation of silbi launched
on May 6, 2026**. New contracts are written on 5th-gen terms; the large
population of existing 4th-gen holders — many foreigners among them —
manage their current contracts and the non-covered surcharge system.
This guide covers both, plus the parts that are specifically foreign:
identity matching, NHIS interplay, and cross-border edges.

## Gate one is not nationality — it is being identifiable as a policyholder

At contract time the insurer verifies the real name, birth date,
alien-registration or residence-report number, address, and contact
details of the policyholder and insured. The foreigner-specific failure
mode is name mismatch: KB's guidance requires the **uppercase English
name and registration number exactly as printed on the ARC**, and warns
that verification can fail where the lawful stay period has lapsed or
the Ministry of Justice database cannot confirm the person.

If your passport, ARC, telecom, and bank records spell your name three
different ways, fix that before the insurance application — it is the
same [identity-consistency problem](/banking/how-to-open-a-bank-account-in-korea-as-a-foreigner/)
that trips foreigners at banks, transplanted to insurance.

## NHIS is not a substitute for silbi — it is silbi's reference point

Silbi does not pay medical bills independently. The structure is
layered: NHIS pays the covered (급여) portion first; silbi then
reimburses, per policy terms, your actual out-of-pocket — the covered
co-payment plus eligible non-covered (비급여) costs. The insurance
association describes silbi in exactly those terms: a complement to
NHIS co-payments and non-covered costs.

Two precise statements follow:

- Maintaining normal NHIS status — employee, regional, or dependent —
  is central to managing medical costs. (Sort your
  [NHIS position first](/insurance/nhis-korean-health-insurance-for-foreigners/);
  silbi comes second.)
- But "no NHIS, no silbi contract" is *not* the law. Contract
  acceptance and claim mechanics are separate questions.

The practically sharper issue is treatment that bypasses NHIS. Where
care was not covered by NHIS or proper benefit procedures were not
followed, silbi terms have applied a separate, lower reimbursement
ratio to the whole cost rather than the normal covered/non-covered
split — a recent Supreme Court case confirms a clause paying **40% of
actual costs** in that situation. Note the correction to a common
error: this is not a universal "50%" rule; the ratio lives in each
generation's and product's terms, with 40% the prominently documented
structure.

## There is no visa-based rate card

In practice, long-settled statuses (F-5, F-6, F-4) often make contract
administration simpler, and E-7 professionals or D-8 investors with
normal registration, residence, and income are routine candidates. But
formalizing that into a table —

> F-5 = auto-approved / E-7 = conditional / D-2·E-9 = declined

— has no basis in any published underwriting standard. Insurers assess
age, medical history, current treatment, occupational risk, and
identity verifiability per product, and reserve the right to decline —
KB's current silbi products state exactly that. Likewise, "your visa
must have 12+ months remaining" is not an industry-wide legal
threshold. The genuine boundary case is short-term visitors (C-3 and
similar) without alien registration: for them, travel insurance or
visitor medical products are the realistic instruments, not long-term
domestic silbi.

## 4th generation: covered and non-covered deliberately separated

The 4th generation (sold from July 2021) split the product: the base
contract covers 급여, a rider covers 비급여, with representative
self-pay ratios set at **20% for covered** and **30% for non-covered**
costs at introduction.

Simple-ratio illustrations: ₩1,000,000 of eligible covered co-payment →
roughly ₩800,000 reimbursed; ₩1,000,000 of eligible non-covered costs →
roughly ₩700,000. Outpatient care carries per-visit minimum deductibles
by hospital type, so never just multiply every bill by 80%/70%. The 3rd
generation's "big three" non-covered items — manual therapy,
non-covered injections, MRI — sit inside the 4th gen's managed
non-covered rider.

## The number that matters most on 4th gen: last year's non-covered claims

The non-covered premium differential began applying in **July 2024**,
keyed not to your hospital bills but to **non-covered claims the
insurer paid you in the prior year**:

| Prior-year non-covered claims paid | Next renewal, non-covered premium |
| --- | ---: |
| ₩0 | Discount |
| Under ₩1,000,000 | Unchanged |
| ₩1,000,000–1,500,000 | +100% |
| ₩1,500,000–3,000,000 | +200% |
| ₩3,000,000 or more | +300% |

Read the +300% correctly: a ₩15,000/month non-covered rider premium in
the top band becomes roughly **4× the base** for that portion (100%
base + 300% surcharge) — not ₩15,000 × 3 as a total. And two
mitigations: the surcharge touches **only the non-covered rider
premium**, not the whole policy, and the band is recalculated **every
year** on the trailing twelve months — it is not a permanent mark.
Serious-illness special-calculation categories and long-term-care
grades 1–2 are exempt from the differential.

The expat-specific lesson: if you use manual therapy, non-covered
injections, or MRI freely "because insurance pays," compute the true
TCO including next year's premium — the reimbursement and the surcharge
are one system.

## 2026 new enrollees calculate on the 5th generation

Since **May 6, 2026**, new silbi sales are 5th generation — so writing
a "4th-gen enrollment strategy" for new buyers is out of date the day
it is published. The 5th gen re-partitions non-covered care into
**severe versus non-severe**, tightening self-pay on non-severe
non-covered services while strengthening protection for severe illness,
and the FSC projects premiums about **30% below** 4th-gen levels.

For existing 4th-gen holders the live question is whether to keep the
current contract (with its surcharge mechanics) — a
generation-comparison decision that deserves its own follow-up guide.

## Underwriting cares about your medical history, not your passport

Silbi is a high-loss-ratio product, which makes health disclosure the
real gate. Recent examination findings, hospitalizations and surgeries,
diagnoses and medications all feed acceptance decisions — KB's current
simplified-issue silbi, for instance, asks about re-examination
findings within 3 months, hospitalization/surgery within 2 years, and
cancer history within 5 years, and still reserves final refusal to
company standards.

Foreigners get no disclosure discount. If a question covers treatment
you received **in your home country**, it must be answered truthfully —
omitting material history because translation is inconvenient sets up
contract rescission or claim disputes later. Answering the application
accurately is worth far more than saving a few thousand won of premium.

## NHIS subscriber types are not silbi payout grades

Employee subscriber, regional subscriber, and dependent are NHIS
*contribution* categories. They do not map to different silbi
reimbursement rates (80%/70%/50% folklore). What silbi asks is whether
the treatment ran through normal NHIS benefit procedures and what you
actually paid. An F-6 spouse covered as a dependent and a
self-paying regional subscriber with normal NHIS benefits are, for
silbi claim purposes, judged by the same policy terms. The rational
order: settle the household's
[NHIS structure](/insurance/nhis-korean-health-insurance-for-foreigners/)
first, then layer silbi on top.

## Claims run on documents — Korean ones

The claim file is about proving actual medical costs: claim form, ID,
hospital payment receipts, plus — by amount and condition —
prescriptions, diagnosis certificates, and itemized bills, per the
insurance association's standard list.

Two foreigner-specific clarifications. Korean insurers assess claims on
**Korean-language medical records** — you generally do not need an
English diagnosis certificate for a domestic silbi claim (that document
is for your home-country insurer, a different errand). And use your own
Korean bank account for payouts, with your
[registered identity consistent across bank and insurer](/banking/how-to-open-a-bank-account-in-korea-as-a-foreigner/)
— name mismatches at payout are avoidable errors.

## Departure and visa changes: manage the contract, don't abandon it

A status change (E-7 → F-5) neither upgrades your silbi automatically
nor voids it. What does demand a review: long departures, ARC
cancellation, or loss of NHIS status — at which point continuing to pay
premiums may no longer make economic sense, because silbi fundamentally
reimburses treatment at **domestic** medical institutions, and industry
guidance confirms 2nd–4th generation products do not cover overseas
medical costs. Anyone relocating abroad long-term should re-divide the
roles of Korean silbi, international health insurance, and home-country
coverage before leaving — not after the first foreign hospital bill.

## The three-layer design for a foreign resident

The clean mental model for medical-cost defense in Korea:

1. **NHIS** — the state layer carries baseline covered-care risk.
2. **Silbi** — reimburses actual residual costs: covered co-payments
   and eligible non-covered care.
3. **Lump-sum diagnosis policies** (cancer, stroke, cardiac) — defend
   income interruption and living costs, not hospital bills.

Silbi is layer two, no more and no less. So the first question in any
foreigner's silbi decision is not "which visa do you hold?" but four
better ones: *how long will you live in Korea, what is your NHIS
status, what is your health history, and how much non-covered care do
you actually use?* The insurer is pricing verifiable identity, health
risk, and contract continuity — and you should be pricing **the medical
costs that remain after NHIS**. Where those two numbers meet is your
silbi decision.
