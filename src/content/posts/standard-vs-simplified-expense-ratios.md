You earned ₩40 million freelancing. Clients withheld 3.3% from qualifying
payments, you kept the bank statements, and you never organised the expenses.

Now it is May. How does Korea work out your taxable business income if you
cannot establish every cost through proper books?

Through prescribed estimates: **단순경비율** (simple expense rate) and
**기준경비율** (standard expense rate). They sound alike and they are not
interchangeable.

The simple rate treats a percentage of revenue as your expenses. The standard
rate makes you evidence the major costs — purchases, business rent, labour —
and covers the rest with a much smaller percentage. Two people with identical
revenue can end up with very different estimated income depending on method,
industry code and documents.

And one thing neither method does: **being eligible for an estimate does not
remove the obligation to keep records.** Bookkeeping penalties and lost
benefits survive it.

So the question is not which percentage gives the biggest refund. It is
**which calculation is legally applicable, and whether proper books would have
given a truer result.**

## Three things to settle first

| Question | What it decides |
|---|---|
| Simple or standard rate? | How estimated income is calculated |
| Simplified or double-entry books? | Your bookkeeping obligation |
| Which industry code and income year? | Which thresholds and published rates apply |

🔴 **There is no universal expense rate for freelancers.** The percentage comes
from the industry code and the tax year. Every percentage in this guide is
invented for the arithmetic — the real one has to come from the National Tax
Service schedule for your code and year.

## Three ways to treat expenses

With proper records, the calculation is ordinary:

```
gross revenue − allowable necessary expenses = business income
₩50,000,000 − ₩32,000,000 = ₩18,000,000
```

Without adequate books, Korea has prescribed estimated methods (추계): the
**simple rate**, where a percentage of qualifying revenue estimates expenses,
and the **standard rate**, where specified major expenses are evidenced and a
percentage estimates the rest.

These are not a menu. A taxpayer with proper records generally uses them; one
whose income is estimated under the statutory rules falls under whichever
method the law and the business circumstances produce. **Not the lowest
result on offer.**

## The simple rate

```
estimated income = revenue − (revenue × simple expense rate)
```

On ₩40 million at an assumed 68%:

| | Illustrative |
|---|---:|
| Gross revenue | ₩40,000,000 |
| Assumed rate | 68% |
| Estimated expenses | ₩27,200,000 |
| **Estimated business income** | **₩12,800,000** |

The ₩27.2 million is not established by producing receipts for it. It comes
from the prescribed rate — that is the defining feature of the method.

⚠️ And ₩12.8 million is not the taxable base. Business income is one stage of
the comprehensive calculation; other income, deductions, credits and tax
already paid all still apply.

### A 68% rate is not a 68% refund

This is the mix-up worth stopping. The rate estimates **expenses**. It does
not compute a refund.

On that ₩40 million, 3.3% withholding came to ₩1,320,000. Then:

```
final liability ₩900,000   →  refund ₩420,000
final liability ₩1,600,000 →  pay ₩280,000
```

Same expense-rate calculation, opposite outcomes. Those liabilities are
assumptions, not figures derived from ₩12.8 million alone.

**The rate determines estimated business income. The return determines the
tax.**

## The standard rate

Three major categories carry evidence: **매입비용** (qualifying purchases),
**임차료** (qualifying business-asset rent), **인건비** (qualifying labour).
The prescribed rate then estimates everything else.

```
estimated income = revenue − verified major expenses − (revenue × standard rate)
```

On ₩80m revenue with ₩24m purchases, ₩9m rent, ₩12m labour and an assumed 12%:

| | Illustrative |
|---|---:|
| Gross revenue | ₩80,000,000 |
| Verified purchases | −₩24,000,000 |
| Verified rent | −₩9,000,000 |
| Verified labour | −₩12,000,000 |
| Other expenses at 12% | −₩9,600,000 |
| **Estimated business income** | **₩25,400,000** |

That assumes all three major amounts satisfy the eligibility and documentation
rules. There is also a statutory comparison calculation that can cap the
result — below.

### What changes is what you must prove

Under the simple rate the percentage *is* how expenses are recognised. It
would be wrong to apply the full rate and then deduct receipts on top.

Under the standard rate the major costs come out separately and the percentage
covers the rest. But you cannot add advertising, transport, subscriptions and
every other operating payment as major expenses because receipts exist — the
categories have boundaries.

**A receipt proves a payment happened. It does not prove the payment belongs
in the major-expense calculation.**

### Purchases are narrower than "things I bought"

매입비용 covers qualifying purchases of goods and certain specified items —
merchandise, materials, supplies, particular purchased energy where the
definition is met, and certain outsourced processing under the relevant rules.

A food retailer buying packaged goods for resale is inside it. A consultant
buying a ₩2 million laptop is not automatically: **a fixed-asset acquisition
is a different thing from buying goods for resale**, and the treatment follows
the asset's nature.

The English word "purchase" reads far wider than the category does.

### Rent has to qualify

Qualifying rent for business-use fixed assets — commercial premises, machinery
or equipment used in operations.

A personal apartment's rent does not become business rent because someone
works from home sometimes, and mixed use needs the actual business use
examined. Labelling a bank transfer "rent" establishes nothing; the asset and
the business relationship do, with contracts, invoices and payment records
behind them.

Pay rent and keep no evidence and the standard calculation turns out worse
than expected.

### Labour is not owner's draw

Employee wages and other qualifying labour payments, with payroll withholding
and payment statements as the evidence. A business paying ₩30 million in
qualifying wages with proper records has a major expense.

🔴 A sole proprietor moving money to their own account does not. An owner's
withdrawal is not wages, and calling the monthly transfer "salary" does not
convert it. A contractor payment also has to be classified correctly — not
every outsourced fee is labour under the major-expense definition. The
employee-or-contractor line is in [freelancing in Korea
legally](/business/freelancing-in-korea-legally/).

## The industry code decides the percentage

Korea publishes rates by industry — 업종코드 — in an annual schedule. The 2025
income-year detail was published on 29 April 2026, following the rate
notification.

A translator's rate is not a retailer's, an accommodation operator's or a
manufacturer's. Businesses that sound alike in English can hold different tax
codes, and two people who both call their work "online services" — one
teaching, one retailing — may differ in registered activity, income
classification and code.

🔴 **You cannot search for "average freelancer expense ratio Korea" and use the
first percentage you find.** Start from the actual registered or reportable
activity, not the job title on a profile.

## The thresholds, and the second gate

| Industry group | Simple rate if prior-year revenue below | Double-entry books from |
|---|---:|---:|
| A — retail, wholesale and specified activities | ₩60 million | ₩300 million |
| B — manufacturing, accommodation, restaurants, information, others | ₩36 million | ₩150 million |
| C — professional, scientific, educational, specified services | ₩24 million | ₩75 million |

These are not tiers of one number, and informal descriptions do not place you
in a group — the statutory classifications and special exceptions do. Certain
personal-service activities sit differently again.

⚠️ **They are not tax-free amounts.** Someone under ₩24 million still owes
income tax. The figures govern eligibility for an estimated method.

### Prior-year revenue is not the only test

Reaching the relevant **current-year** double-entry threshold excludes simple-rate
treatment. So a Group C business with a small prior year but ₩75 million this
year may lose access despite the low prior figure. Check both.

### New businesses

No prior year means the ordinary threshold cannot apply the same way, and
special treatment exists — but eligibility still runs on current-year revenue
and the statutory exclusions.

So neither "I registered in 2025, so the simple rate is automatic" nor "I am a
sole proprietor, so double-entry from day one" holds. It depends on the
industry, commencement, revenue and special rules. And for foreign
entrepreneurs, the registered activity still has to match the immigration
authorisation — tax registration and permission to trade are separate
questions.

### Professionals are excluded regardless

Specified professional occupations — certain legal, accounting, medical and
other regulated activities — carry double-entry obligations whatever the
revenue, and are excluded from simple-rate treatment. A tiny first year does
not change it.

Further exclusions cover certain cash-receipt registration failures and
repeated improper refusals to issue card or cash receipts. **Low revenue alone
does not guarantee the simple rate** — rule out the exclusions first.

### The ₩40 million split for personal services

For qualifying personal-service business income on codes beginning **940**, the
2025 notification splits the simple rate into a basic rate up to ₩40 million of
revenue and an excess rate above it.

With invented rates of 70% and 60% on ₩50 million:

```
₩40,000,000 × 70% = ₩28,000,000
₩10,000,000 × 60% =  ₩6,000,000
                     ₩34,000,000  estimated expenses
₩50,000,000 − ₩34,000,000 = ₩16,000,000
```

🔴 This is **not** a ₩40 million tax-free threshold, and not a rule that
freelancers under ₩40 million qualify for the simple rate. Eligibility has to
be satisfied already; only then does the rate split matter.

## The comparison calculation

Most short explanations omit this, and it changes the answer.

A standard-rate taxpayer has two calculations:

```
direct      revenue − major expenses − (revenue × standard rate)
comparison  [revenue − (revenue × simple rate)] × multiplier
```

**The smaller is used.** For 2025 income the multipliers are **2.8** for
simplified-bookkeeping taxpayers and **3.4** for double-entry taxpayers — the
year's figures, not constants. And double-entry taxpayers use **one-half** of
the standard rate in the direct calculation.

On ₩50m revenue, ₩3m major expenses, an assumed 6% standard rate, an assumed
70% simple rate and the 2.8 multiplier:

| Calculation | Result |
|---|---:|
| Direct | ₩44,000,000 |
| Comparison | ₩42,000,000 |
| **Used** | **₩42,000,000** |

🔴 **The comparison is not permission to use the simple rate.** The simple rate
appears only as an input to a formula inside the standard method. The same
business may still have failed to keep required books, with the penalties that
follow.

### The halving, shown

On ₩100m revenue with ₩40m major expenses and an assumed 10% published rate:

```
full rate   ₩100m − ₩40m − ₩10m = ₩50,000,000
half rate   ₩100m − ₩40m −  ₩5m = ₩55,000,000
```

That shows only the halving — the comparison calculation still has to run
alongside it. More to the point, a double-entry taxpayer cannot treat
estimated filing as a harmless alternative to the books: the NTS describes
serious penalties where someone required to use double-entry books files on an
estimate without them.

## Which way the estimate cuts

**When the estimate is generous.** ₩40m revenue, an assumed 68% rate, and
actual allowable expenses of only ₩19m:

| Method | Business income |
|---|---:|
| Actual books, ₩19m expenses | ₩21,000,000 |
| Simple rate at 68% | ₩12,800,000 |

The estimate is lower. The government is not claiming you spent 68% — it is a
statutory estimation method, and that is why qualifying taxpayers find it
attractive. It still does not make it available to you.

**When it is not.** Same revenue and rate, actual expenses of ₩33m:

| Method | Business income |
|---|---:|
| Actual books, ₩33m expenses | ₩7,000,000 |
| Simple rate at 68% | ₩12,800,000 |

Books win by ₩5.8 million. **Neither method is reliably cheaper — it depends
on the real cost structure**, which is why records are worth keeping even for a
business that might qualify for an estimate.

### Missing evidence is expensive under the standard method

Two businesses, ₩80m revenue each, assumed 12% rate, similar real costs — one
kept evidence, one did not:

| | A | B |
|---|---:|---:|
| Documented major expenses | ₩45m | ₩20m |
| Other expenses at 12% | ₩9.6m | ₩9.6m |
| Direct calculated income | **₩25.4m** | **₩50.4m** |

₩25 million apart on the direct calculation, for businesses that may have
spent much the same. The comparison calculation may cap the final figure, so
this illustrates the direct method rather than a final assessment — but the
practical point stands. Reconstructing a year of undocumented supplier and
payroll payments in May is the problem that regular records prevent.

## Evidence for major expenses

| Major expense | Useful records |
|---|---|
| Merchandise and materials | Invoices, tax invoices, card records, qualifying receipts |
| Business premises rent | Lease, invoice or other accepted payment evidence |
| Machinery or equipment rent | Agreement and qualifying expense evidence |
| Employee wages | Payroll records, withholding reports, payment statements |
| Other qualifying major expenses | The documents prescribed for that category |

The rules decide what suffices, and a prescribed statement may apply where
qualifying expenses lack ordinary standard documents.

⚠️ **A bank transfer supports a payment without establishing the facts about
it.** ₩5 million to an individual could be wages, an outsourced fee, a loan
repayment or a personal transfer. The amount does not say which.

### Ordinary operating costs are not major expenses

Advertising, subscriptions, professional fees, communications, insurance —
legitimate expenses under a bookkeeping calculation, and **not** separately
deductible as major expenses under the standard method. The prescribed
percentage covers them.

So a business with heavy software and advertising costs may find those real
costs are not separately recognised in the direct calculation, while proper
books could recognise the actual allowable expenses. **The choice is not only
about convenience** — it affects whether the calculation reflects the real
cost structure. The wider business-income calculation is [income tax for
foreign sole
proprietors](/business/korean-income-tax-for-foreign-sole-proprietors/).

## Edge cases that change the threshold

**Several activities.** Consulting plus an online store, or several registered
locations, may need an adjusted threshold calculation rather than treating
each independently. Where activities fall in different statutory groups, the
NTS provides a formula based on the principal business's revenue and the
others' adjusted for the relative threshold values — not a simple sum against
the highest threshold. Joint businesses may need separate treatment. 🔴 **Do
not split one business into several registrations assuming each gets its own
full threshold.**

**Changed activity.** Start the year in personal services, open a retail
operation later, and the original rate does not automatically carry to the new
revenue. The code affects the percentage and the eligibility thresholds, and if
the registration no longer describes the activity it may need updating —
though changing a code purely for a better rate, without a genuine change in
activity, is not that. **Classification follows the business; the rate follows
the classification.**

**Several clients.** Three clients paying ₩12m, ₩8m and ₩10m is ₩30 million of
relevant revenue, not three separate threshold tests. The method is not
decided by how many payers there were. Where genuinely different businesses are
involved, the mixed-business rules apply.

## Two things it is not

**간이과세 is not 단순경비율.** The simplified VAT threshold — ₩104 million,
subject to exceptions — is a VAT classification. The simple expense rate is an
income-tax estimation method. Different thresholds, different industry rules.
A business can qualify for one and not the other, which is why one revenue
figure cannot describe a tax position. VAT is [its own
guide](/business/korean-vat-for-small-business-owners/).

**3.3% withholding is not an eligibility finding.** It does not establish that
registration obligations are met, and registering a sole proprietorship does
not determine whether simple-rate filing is available. Some freelancers treat
the withholding as evidence that nothing further is required. The analysis runs
the other way: actual activity → industry code → bookkeeping and expense-rate
rules → estimated income.

## The schedule expires

The 2025 income-year rates were notified effective 30 March 2026, with detail
published 29 April 2026. **They are 2025 rates** — not to be copied into a 2026
calculation filed in 2027. Rates change, classifications get revised,
eligibility rules move. Which is how an undated blog table produces a wrong
return.

Record the income year, the industry code, the expense-rate category, the
percentage, where it came from and the date you checked it. Then check again
when a new schedule lands.

To look it up: the NTS annual materials carry downloadable tables by industry
code, and Hometax has services for checking rates and classifications. Useful
search terms are `기준경비율`, `단순경비율`, `업종코드`, `기준·단순경비율
조회`. Confirm the income year matches the return, then the activity and code.
**Do not copy the rate of a business whose English name merely sounds close.**

### What Hometax offers, and does not

Filing season may show income information, filing classifications and
calculation assistance, and eligible taxpayers may get the prefilled 모두채움
service. Useful — and not a substitute for checking the facts underneath:
income sources, gross amounts, industry codes, the expense-rate
classification. Several activities, overseas income or a changed structure all
need more examination.

🔴 **Clicking the most convenient filing option does not establish legal
eligibility for that method.**

## Estimated filing is not penalty-free filing

The existence of an estimated method does not suspend bookkeeping
obligations. For simplified-bookkeeping taxpayers a bookkeeping penalty can
apply at **20% of the relevant calculated tax**, adjusted where only part of
income is unbooked, with specified small-taxpayer exceptions — for 2025
income the NTS identifies qualifying newly commenced businesses, prior-year
revenue below **₩48 million**, and certain recipients of only year-end-settled
business income.

Those are exceptions from that penalty. Not exemptions from filing or from
evidence requirements. For double-entry taxpayers the consequences of
estimating without the required books are more serious, including non-filing
and bookkeeping penalties with revenue-based comparisons.

### A lower estimate does not justify skipping the books

Finding that an estimate produces lower income than the records would is not a
reason to leave the records undone. You may be required to keep books; the
method may not be available; penalties may apply; and deductions, credits and
loss treatments can be lost. **Comparing the two income figures is not the
comparison that matters.**

This matters more as a business grows. A method available in a small first
year may not be available later, and each year is tested on its own.

### Losses are the clearest case

A business with ₩30 million revenue and ₩38 million of actual allowable
expenses has an ₩8 million loss before statutory adjustments. At an assumed 60%
simple rate:

```
₩30,000,000 − ₩18,000,000 = ₩12,000,000 of positive estimated income
```

The estimate says income where the business made a loss — and the NTS
bookkeeping guidance specifically warns that failing to keep required books
brings disadvantages for loss recognition. The actual ₩8 million may not be
claimable without satisfying the requirements.

**Records matter most in the year a business loses money**, because the goal is
not the smallest immediate bill. It is the correct result under the law.

## What the wrong method costs

On ₩50 million, an assumed 70% simple rate against a direct standard
calculation with ₩3 million of major expenses and an assumed 6% rate:

| | Business income |
|---|---:|
| Simple rate at 70% | ₩15,000,000 |
| Direct standard-rate | ₩44,000,000 |

₩29 million apart — and **the taxpayer does not get to pick ₩15 million.** For
a standard-rate taxpayer the comparison rule gave ₩42 million, which is not the
same as becoming a simple-rate taxpayer. A return filed on the wrong method can
materially understate income, drawing corrections, additional tax and
penalties.

**The largest apparent saving is not a saving if it comes from a method you
were never eligible to use.**

And it reaches past this filing. The income reported through an estimated
method becomes part of the tax record an institution later reads on an income
certificate — which is not an argument for inflating income or inventing
expenses, only a reason to use the correct calculation. The documents
themselves are [Korean tax certificates: which one, and
where](/business/getting-korean-tax-certificates-on-hometax/).

## The order

```
industry code
  → eligibility
  → bookkeeping obligation
  → expense calculation
  → comprehensive income-tax return
```

Confirm the income classification — a contract labelled "freelancer" is not
conclusive. Establish the income year. Identify the industry code. Examine
prior-year *and* current-year revenue. Rule out the exclusions. Determine
whether simplified or double-entry books are required. Calculate on the
applicable method, collecting major-expense evidence where the standard rate
applies. Reconcile the national and local tax already withheld. File, including
the local income-tax step. Keep the rate, the code, the source and the
confirmation.

The sequence matters because **the percentage should never be chosen before
eligibility is established.**

### Five ways a return goes wrong

Treating every freelancer as simple-rate eligible. Using the wrong income
year's table. Treating every operating cost as a major expense. Ignoring the
comparison formula. Assuming an estimate suspends the bookkeeping penalty.

All avoidable — all requiring the legal classification to be settled before any
percentage is entered.

The simple and standard rates are methods for estimating business income in
prescribed circumstances. They are not tax-free thresholds, not refund
percentages, and not options open to every freelancer. **The aim is not to make
the expense number as large as possible. It is to calculate income by the
method the law actually permits.**
