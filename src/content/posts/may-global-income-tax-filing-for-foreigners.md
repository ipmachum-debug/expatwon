Your employer deducted income tax from every month's salary. Your freelance
clients withheld 3.3%. Your bank took tax out of the interest. So why would
there be another return to file in May?

Because withholding, year-end settlement and the annual comprehensive return
are three different things. For some people the tax taken during the year
finishes the job. For others it was only a deposit. And once there is more
than one kind of income, no single employer's payroll and no single client's
withholding statement can produce the final figure.

Korea calls the annual reconciliation **종합소득세 확정신고**, the final
comprehensive income tax return. The ordinary filing period is 1–31 May of the
following year, subject to deadline adjustment.

But not everyone files. A foreign employee with one job and a completed
year-end settlement may have nothing to file at all. The question worth
answering is not how much was withheld. It is **whether your income has already
been finally settled under the rules that apply to you.**

## Three questions before you open Hometax

| Question | Why it decides the answer |
|---|---|
| What kind of income did you earn? | Employment, business, rental, interest and other income follow different filing rules |
| Has it already been settled? | Withholding does not always complete the annual obligation |
| Are you a Korean tax resident? | Residence sets the scope of income Korea may tax |

## What comprehensive income tax actually covers

The National Tax Service names six categories inside comprehensive income:
interest (이자소득), dividends (배당소득), business income including rental
business income (사업소득), employment income (근로소득), pension income
(연금소득) and other income (기타소득).

Having income in one of those categories does not by itself prove a May return
is required. Some income is separately taxed or finally taxed at source. Some
employees finish through year-end settlement. Certain pension and other-income
arrangements have their own exceptions. The live question is whether the income
has to be **included in the final comprehensive calculation**.

A second distinction matters as much. Capital gains on property or shares,
retirement income and certain other income types run on separate tax systems
with their own filings. They do not simply get added to this return.

Comprehensive income tax is an annual reconciliation of the income inside that
system — not a second tax imposed on everything you earned.

## Who actually has to file

| Your situation | Ordinary position |
|---|---|
| One Korean employer, salary only, year-end settlement completed | Generally not required |
| Two employers, income never fully combined in the settlement | Generally required |
| Employment income plus business income | Generally required |
| Freelancer with qualifying 3.3% business income | Generally comprehensive-filing, absent a specific exception |
| Sole proprietor with business income | Generally required |
| Rental income subject to comprehensive taxation | Filing or separate-tax treatment has to be established |
| Only income already finally taxed at source | May not be required |
| Foreign resident with taxable overseas income | Depends on residence and income scope |

These are classifications, not determinations. The NTS identifies specific
categories of business income — certain insurance and door-to-door salespeople
among them — where a year-end settlement can satisfy the obligation once the
statutory conditions are met. Equally, an employee paid by more than one
employer may avoid a May return where all the employment income was properly
combined and settled through the prescribed process.

Before filing, get the document that shows how the income was reported: the
근로소득 원천징수영수증 for employment income, the 사업소득 원천징수영수증 for
personal-service business income. They are not interchangeable, and the
classification drives the calculation method, the available deductions and
whether you file at all.

## If you are an employee, you may already be finished

A Korean employer withholds tax monthly and then runs year-end settlement
(연말정산), reconciling the year's employment income against deductions,
credits and tax already taken. One employer, a properly completed settlement
and no other qualifying income generally means no separate return. That is one
of the exceptions the NTS lists outright.

Four situations deserve a second look.

**You changed employers.** If the new employer folded the earlier job's income
into the settlement, the employment-income obligation may already be done. If
it did not, a return reconciles it.

**You worked two jobs at once.** Income from several employers has to be
combined somewhere. If the settlement did not do it, May does.

**You also freelance.** The employer's settlement disposes of the salary. It
does not touch separate personal-service business income, which may still have
to go into the comprehensive return.

**You are paid from abroad.** Foreign-paid compensation may never have passed
through a Korean withholding agent at all.

The principle underneath all four: **year-end settlement settles what was
included in it, and nothing else.**

## A freelancer's 3.3% does not close the year

A Korean client paying qualifying personal-service business income withholds
3.3% — 3% national income tax and 0.3% individual local income tax — and
transfers the rest. On ₩30,000,000 of gross revenue that is ₩990,000 withheld
and ₩29,010,000 received.

The revenue is still ₩30 million. The ₩990,000 is prepaid tax, and the final
figure depends on allowable expenses, other taxable income, deductions and
credits. The reconciliation itself — checking the withholding was actually
reported, and claiming what is owed — is its own guide. This one answers the
question before it: whether you have to file at all, and how the final tax is
worked out.

One warning. Not every independent worker is inside a 3.3% arrangement. Income
type, the payer's obligation and tax residence all change the withholding
position. **No withholding does not mean no income tax.**

## Which year you are filing for

Korea uses the calendar year for individuals. Income earned between 1 January
and 31 December is reported the following year.

| Income year | Ordinary filing period |
|---|---|
| 2024 | May 2025 |
| 2025 | May 2026, adjusted to 1 June 2026 |
| 2026 | May 2027, subject to adjustment |

The 2025 income-year deadline moved to 1 June 2026 because 31 May fell on a
non-business day. That is the reason to check the date each year instead of
copying it from an older guide.

**Filing and paying are separate.** The filing period is also the ordinary
payment period, but the NTS can grant payment extensions in particular
circumstances — as it did for certain affected businesses in the 2025 filing
season. An extension to pay does not extend the deadline to file, and trouble
paying is not a reason to skip filing.

**Some people have an earlier deadline.** The NTS identifies special deadlines
for death and for certain departures abroad; under the relevant departure rule
the deadline is the day before you leave, not the following May. If you are
leaving Korea, read [leaving Korea: tax, pension and what you are
owed](/business/leaving-korea-tax-pension-and-refunds/) before assuming May.

## How the tax is calculated

It starts with income, not with a rate.

```
gross business revenue − necessary expenses = business income
  + other comprehensive income
  − income deductions
  = comprehensive taxable base
  × progressive rates
  − credits and reductions
  = national income tax
  − qualifying prepaid tax
  = payable or refundable
```

Individual local income tax is then calculated and filed under its own rules.
This is a frame, not the statutory calculation — special taxes, additional
charges and particular treatments change results. What it does show is that
**gross revenue, business income, the taxable base and the final tax are four
different numbers.**

### The rate table

| Taxable base | National rate | Progressive deduction |
|---|---:|---:|
| Up to ₩14m | 6% | — |
| ₩14m–50m | 15% | ₩1,260,000 |
| ₩50m–88m | 24% | ₩5,760,000 |
| ₩88m–150m | 35% | ₩15,440,000 |
| ₩150m–300m | 38% | ₩19,940,000 |
| ₩300m–500m | 40% | ₩25,940,000 |
| ₩500m–1bn | 42% | ₩35,940,000 |
| Over ₩1bn | 45% | ₩65,940,000 |

This is the schedule the NTS labels **2023–2025 income years**. It is not
presented here as a separately confirmed schedule for 2026 income.

The rates are progressive. A base of ₩30 million is not taxed at 15%
throughout: ₩14,000,000 × 6% = ₩840,000, then ₩16,000,000 × 15% = ₩2,400,000,
for ₩3,240,000 before credits. The NTS shortcut gives the same answer —
₩30,000,000 × 15% − ₩1,260,000 = ₩3,240,000. The ₩1,260,000 is the bracket's
progressive deduction, not an expense anyone can separately claim.

Individual local income tax sits on top. In a simplified case it corresponds to
10% of the national calculation — ₩324,000 here, ₩3,564,000 combined — but
credits, reductions and local provisions can move it, so take the final figure
from the two filings rather than assuming every local result is exactly a tenth
of the national one.

## A worked example: ₩60 million of revenue

A Korean tax resident running an individual business, with ₩60m gross revenue
and ₩25m of established allowable expenses, has ₩35m of business income.
Assume a further ₩5m of income deductions, no other income and no credits.

```
₩60,000,000 − ₩25,000,000 = ₩35,000,000   business income
₩35,000,000 −  ₩5,000,000 = ₩30,000,000   taxable base
₩30,000,000 × 15% − ₩1,260,000 = ₩3,240,000
```

| | National | Local |
|---|---:|---:|
| Final liability | ₩3,240,000 | ₩324,000 |
| Already prepaid | ₩1,800,000 | ₩180,000 |
| **Additional payment** | **₩1,440,000** | **₩144,000** |

Combined, **₩1,584,000** still to pay.

Every input there is an assumption — the expenses, the deductions, the absence
of other income and credits. What the example demonstrates is the shape: the
tax is not gross revenue times the top rate on a table, and it is not derived
from the net deposits either.

## If the business made a loss

Revenue of ₩40m against ₩44m of eligible expenses is a ₩4m loss before other
rules apply. That establishes neither a right to a refund nor the absence of a
filing obligation.

Losses are subject to rules on documentation, computation and possible offset
or carryforward, and the outcome depends on income type, bookkeeping duties,
other income and statutory restrictions. A loss in a private spreadsheet is not
a tax loss under the Income Tax Act. This is exactly why records matter in the
years a business makes nothing — and why it gets hard when business and
personal payments share one bank account.

## Actual expenses, or an estimated ratio

Korean business-income taxation runs on bookkeeping-based calculations and, for
eligible taxpayers, estimated ones using prescribed rates: the simple expense
rate (단순경비율) and the standard expense rate (기준경비율).

They are not two interchangeable discounts. Eligibility turns on industry,
previous revenue, bookkeeping obligations and other statutory conditions, and
the NTS attaches penalties and restrictions to inadequate bookkeeping or
improper estimated filing. Someone required to keep double-entry books cannot
simply pick the simplest calculation.

So the question is not which percentage produces the lowest tax. It is **which
calculation is applicable and supported** for that taxpayer.

## Foreign residents: which income belongs in Korea

A foreign passport does not decide Korean tax residence, and neither does a
residence card. The Income Tax Act looks at domicile or a place of residence
for 183 days or more, read alongside the circumstances. A resident is taxed on
domestic and foreign income within the statutory scope; a non-resident on
Korean-source income.

There is an exception that changes the answer for most arrivals: where a
foreign resident's cumulative residence over the preceding ten years is five
years or less, foreign-source income is taxed only so far as it is paid in
Korea or remitted to Korea. The full test, and what happens when that threshold
passes, is in [income tax for foreigners: resident or
not](/business/income-tax-for-foreigners-resident-or-not/).

Two traps follow. Rent from overseas property cannot be included or excluded by
looking at a Korean bank balance. And income paid into an overseas account is
not automatically foreign-source — where the service was performed and the
sourcing provisions matter more than where the bank is. **Do not use the
location of a transfer as a substitute for source analysis.**

## What to prepare

**Income records.** Employment and business withholding receipts, records of
other taxable income, sales and transaction records. Check what each payer
reported.

**Expenses and books.** Whatever supports the expense method you are using —
invoices, receipts, eligible card payments, transfers, rent. A bank transaction
alone does not prove an expense is deductible.

**Deductions and credits.** The records for the ones you actually qualify for.
Do not assume a deduction available to a resident employee applies identically
to a non-resident or a business-income taxpayer.

**Prepaid taxes.** The national and local tax already withheld or paid. Without
these the remaining payment or refund cannot be calculated.

Overseas income adds foreign tax paid, remittances, exchange rates and treaty
positions. The aim is not to collect every document available — it is to
support the income, expenses, credits and prepaid tax actually in the return.

## Filing it

1. **Sign in** at [Hometax](https://www.hometax.go.kr/).
2. **Find the return** under `세금신고` → `종합소득세 신고`.
3. **Pick the right form.** Some taxpayers get a prefilled return through
   모두채움; others enter detail. Do not choose the simple one because it looks
   easier.
4. **Verify reported income.** Review what employers and payers filed, add what
   must be combined, and look for duplicates and wrong classifications.
5. **Complete expenses and deductions** using the applicable method.
6. **Review the calculated tax** — base, liability, prepaid amounts.
7. **Submit.** Save the confirmation and a copy. A part-completed form is not
   an accepted return.
8. **Do the local filing.** Hometax links through to Wetax for individual local
   income tax; review and submit it there.

On that last step the 2026 filing-season guidance was explicit: do not omit the
separate individual local income tax filing. The national return does not
complete it.

## Why there are two filings

| | National | Local |
|---|---|---|
| Korean term | 종합소득세 | 개인지방소득세 |
| Authority | National Tax Service | Local government |
| Platform | Hometax | Wetax, or the linked filing |
| Confirmation | National filing receipt | Local filing confirmation |

The systems are linked to save duplicated work. They have not become one tax.
Refunds and additional payments can be processed separately, so a national
refund is not evidence the local side is settled — and the same applies when
money is owed. Check the confirmation for both.

## What missing the deadline costs

The NTS warns that failing to file when required can forfeit certain tax
benefits and attract penalties. On the guidance reviewed for the 2025 income
year, the ordinary failure-to-file penalty is **20% of the unpaid tax
associated with the missing return**, with separate rules for taxpayers under
double-entry bookkeeping obligations and heavier penalties for fraudulent
non-filing. Late payment accrues on top, at the **0.022% daily** rate the NTS
states for the period from 16 February 2022.

On ₩1,000,000 of unpaid national tax, unpaid for 30 days:

| | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Unpaid national tax | ₩1,000,000 |
| Failure-to-file penalty, 20% | ₩200,000 |
| Late payment, 0.022% × 30 days | ₩6,600 |
| **Total** | **₩1,206,600** |

₩206,600 of that is penalty. The example leaves out local income tax and its
penalties, special penalty calculations and any statutory reduction, and it
assumes the ordinary provisions apply. What it is not is a rule that every late
filer owes 20% of gross revenue or 20% of all withholding — the penalty base
and the available reductions have to be established. **A missed deadline is not
the same tax paid later.**

## If you already missed it

The obligation does not disappear, and the route depends on what happened.
Someone who never filed a required return may need a late return (기한후신고).
Someone who filed and understated tax needs an amended filing. Someone who
filed and overpaid is looking at a correction or refund claim. Three different
legal situations, with different deadlines and penalties.

Do not assume a fresh return for a past year corrects the original filing, or
that an earlier return can be replaced without regard to its status. Hometax
carries separate processes for late and corrected filings, and a refund claim
needs both a statutory right and a live claim period. Filing late voluntarily
is usually better than leaving it, but the consequences are specific.

## Refunds and penalties are not mutually exclusive

Having had tax withheld, and possibly being owed a refund, does not answer
whether a return was due by the original deadline. It is wrong to say everyone
who missed a May filing owes 20% of gross income; it is equally wrong to say no
consequence can arise once 3.3% has been withheld. Establish the final tax
position and the filing status first. Special NTS refund programmes exist for
qualifying taxpayers with unclaimed prepaid tax, but they have defined
eligibility and are not a universal route for unfiled returns.

## Three foreign taxpayers, ₩40 million each

**One employer.** All ₩40m is employment income, withheld monthly and settled
at year end, with nothing else to combine. Under the ordinary rule, no separate
May return.

**One contractor.** All ₩40m is qualifying personal-service business income
with tax withheld by clients. Expenses and the final comprehensive tax have to
be worked out; a return is generally relevant, and the result may be a payment
or a refund.

**Both.** ₩30m salary, settled by the employer, plus ₩10m of separate business
income that the settlement never touched. The return has to account for the
relevant categories and the tax already paid.

**The same ₩40 million, three filing positions** — because classification and
settlement status decide the obligation, not the size of the number.

## Does the return affect a visa or a loan?

Tax filing and immigration are separate procedures, but the records the tax
system produces turn up later. An extension application may ask for
income documents; a lender reviewing a loan will too, and the requirements
differ by status, programme and bank.

A tax return is not an income amount certificate. 소득금액증명 reflects
reported and processed income; 납세증명서 concerns national tax payment status
rather than annual profit; a withholding receipt does something else again. Do
not assume any document with a tax figure on it satisfies a request for proof
of income — the gap matters most for freelancers and sole proprietors, whose
deposits and reported taxable income are different numbers.

Keep the return, the filing confirmation and the withholding receipts. Together
they are the record of your Korean income history.

## The order to work in

Establish the **income year** first — a May filing is about last year, not this
year's revenue. Then **each income category**, separately. Then whether the
income is **already finally settled**, through year-end settlement or a
separate-tax exception. Then **residence and income scope**, which is not
answered by where a transfer landed. Then **expenses and deductions**, on the
applicable method. Then **prepaid taxes**, checked against payer statements.
Then the **national return**, confirmed as accepted. Then the **local filing**.
Then **keep the records**.

The order is the point. Starting from a tax rate before fixing the
classification produces the wrong answer, and so does starting from a refund
estimate before confirming the withholding.

May filing is not a second tax on everyone who earned money. It is the final
calculation for income that has to be reported under this system — and the
question to answer before May is which income must be declared, what has
already been settled, and what is still legally due. Once those three are
fixed, the return is arithmetic rather than a guess.
