"I'll pay you when the next tenant moves in" is not a legal extension of the
repayment date. It is the landlord's funding problem, described to you as though
it were your risk.

Recovering a deposit is a sequence of dates, and every one of them has to be
provable afterwards. This half is about building those dates before the money
becomes a fight.

## 1. First, establish that the lease actually ended

A fixed-term lease does not reliably end because it reached its end date.

Under article 6 of the Housing Lease Protection Act, a tenant who has not given
notice of refusal to renew by
[two months before expiry](/tracked/lease-nonrenewal-notice-deadline/) can find
the lease tacitly renewed on the same terms.

A lease expiring on 31 March 2027 therefore needs the refusal to have **reached**
the landlord by 31 January 2027. Not been sent by then — arrived.

The landlord's own window is different: from six months to two months before
expiry. Which means there is a stretch of time in which the landlord can no
longer refuse renewal and the tenant still can.

## 2. If it already renewed itself, the rule changes

Missing the two-month deadline does not commit you to another full term.

Article 6-2 lets a tenant under a tacitly renewed lease give notice of
termination **at any time**. The catch is when it bites: termination takes effect
once
[three months have passed from the landlord's receipt](/tracked/tacit-renewal-termination-effective-period/).

So notice arriving on 10 June points at the lease ending after 10 September.

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">"I told them today, so pay me today" is not the structure</p>
  <p>This is the part that ruins moving plans. A tenant who has found a new
  apartment and gives notice expecting the deposit next month is three months out
  from the lease even ending — and the deposit is not due until it does. Work
  backwards from the new place's balance payment date, not forwards from today.</p>
</div>

## 3. A declaration only works when it arrives

There is no prescribed form for notice. Phone, text, KakaoTalk, email and
content-certified mail can all carry a declaration of intention.

The difficulty is not form. It is proving, later, **what** was said, **when**,
**to whom**, and that it actually reached them.

A phone call with no recording leaves the landlord free to say the tenant told
them they were staying. On a large deposit, that is a bad trade for the
convenience of not writing anything down.

## 4. Content-certified mail is not delivery certification

This distinction gets missed constantly.

Content-certified mail from the post office proves that a particular document was
sent on a particular date, and what it said. It does not by itself establish that
the other side received it.

For a declaration where arrival is the operative event, use the combination:

<div class="steps">
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">1</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Content-certified mail</strong> — fixes what you said and when you said it.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">2</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Registered post</strong> — puts it in a tracked chain.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">3</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Delivery certification</strong> — evidences that it arrived.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">4</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Keep the reply</strong> — "understood", "I'll have it ready by then" is evidence of receipt.</div></div>
</div>

Step 4 is cheap and often decisive. A KakaoTalk answer acknowledging the notice
does work that certified mail cannot.

## 5. A landlord who dodges the post has not stopped the clock forever

Landlords move, change numbers, and stop answering.

Article 113 of the Civil Act allows a declaration of intention to be served by
publication through the court where the person making it cannot ascertain the
other party's whereabouts **without fault of their own**.

That threshold matters. It means having genuinely exhausted the ordinary routes —
the address on the register, the resident registration address, known contact
details — not having called twice and got no answer.

But it does exist, and it is the answer to a landlord who believes not collecting
mail is a strategy.

## 6. The lease ends, and the tenancy relationship does not

Article 4(2) of the Act treats the lease relationship as continuing until the
tenant has been repaid the deposit, even after the term has ended.

The provision exists to stop a tenant who has not been paid from falling out of
the Act's protection on the expiry date.

Read it as a warning about what you do next. Passing the expiry date is not a
signal to empty the apartment and transfer your address. It is a signal to
decide, deliberately, whether you can afford to stay put.

If you cannot — a job elsewhere, a new lease already committed — that is what the
next section is for.

## 7. The leasehold registration order, and its one condition

Article 3-3 lets a tenant apply to the district court, branch court or city or
county court with jurisdiction over the leased housing where **the lease has
ended and the deposit has not been returned**.

What it does: it lets the tenant give up actual occupation and move the resident
registration — or, for a foreign national, the stay-address report — while
keeping the opposing power and priority repayment already acquired.

Three things about it that get asked in the same order every time.

**The landlord's consent is not required.** "Please don't register it", "the next
tenant is nearly signed", "just wait a little" are requests, not conditions. The
court decides on the facts: the lease ended, the deposit was not returned, the
property, and the rights already acquired.

**A landlord refusing post can no longer stall it indefinitely.** This used to be
a real obstacle — a landlord who declined to accept the decision could delay the
registration. Since the 2023 amendment the court can commission the housing
leasehold registration before the decision has been served on the landlord.

**But protection follows the completed registration.**

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">A court receipt protects nothing</p>
  <p>The Act preserves opposing power and priority repayment where the leasehold
  registration <strong>has been completed</strong> —
  <a href="/tracked/leasehold-registration-order-effective-point/">not where it has been applied for</a>.
  The single most damaging mistake in a deposit dispute is filing the application,
  taking the receipt as protection, and transferring the address that week. The
  2023 amendment made the registration faster; it did not move the moment the
  protection attaches.</p>
</div>

The order that holds:

  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">1</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Apply</strong> to the court with jurisdiction over the property.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">2</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Confirm the decision</strong> has been made.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">3</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Wait for the commission</strong> to the registry.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">4</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Pull a fresh certified copy of the register</strong> and read it.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">5</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Confirm the leasehold entry is actually recorded</strong> — then move, and not before.</div></div>
</div>

## 8. Registration is not payment

The leasehold registration order is a preservation measure for the claim. It does
not make anyone pay.

No court takes money from the landlord's account and sends it to you because the
entry exists. If the landlord still does not pay, the claim has to be turned into
something enforceable — a payment order or a deposit return action — and then
enforced.

Which is a different set of dates, a different set of interest rates, and the
other half of this.
