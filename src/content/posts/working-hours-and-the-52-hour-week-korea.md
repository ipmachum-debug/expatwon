The most expensive misunderstanding about Korean working hours is that the
weekly cap and the overtime premium are the same regulation, so that paying
one settles the other.

They are not. The Labor Standards Act limits how long you may be worked and
separately requires what you must be paid, and the two are enforced through
different mechanisms — one of which is criminal.

<p class="hand">This guide is the clock. The payslip is the afternoon's.</p>

## Before anything: headcount

The statutory working hours limits and the premium pay provisions apply
principally at workplaces with
[five or more ordinary employees](/tracked/annual-leave-minimum-workplace-size/).

Below that, do not assume the same structure. What does not depend on size is
a separate list — minimum wage, the written employment contract, the
obligation to pay wages — so a small workplace is not outside labour law, it
is inside a different part of it.

## 40 hours, 8 hours, and what counts as an hour

Article 50 caps working hours, excluding breaks, at
[40 a week and 8 a day](/tracked/statutory-weekly-hours/).

The definitional question comes first, though: **working time is not only time
spent performing tasks.** Time spent waiting for work under the employer's
direction and supervision is working time.

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">A break is a break only if you can actually use it</p>
  <p>Pre-shift preparation, mandatory training and on-call waiting cannot be
  converted into break time by labelling them that way on a roster. The test is
  whether the employee was genuinely free to use the period, or was under the
  employer's direction. Companies whose reported hours sit suspiciously close to
  a limit are often carrying the difference here.</p>
</div>

## 52 is 40 plus 12

Article 53 permits extended work, by agreement, up to
[12 hours a week](/tracked/max-weekly-overtime-hours/).

```
statutory        40 h
extended     +   12 h
                ─────
weekly max       52 h
```

Agreement is a live requirement, not a formality discharged at signing. A
contract clause saying the company may order overtime when necessary does not
by itself authorise unlimited extended work, and it certainly does not
displace the cap.

## Each week stands on its own

Under the ordinary regime, **60 hours one week and 44 the next is not lawful
because it averages 52.** The first week is a breach when it happens.

Averaging across a period is possible — but only through the statutory
flexible or selective working hour schemes, and those are arrangements with
conditions attached:

<div class="steps">
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">1</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Workplace rules or a written agreement with the employee representative</strong>, depending on the scheme.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">2</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>A defined settlement period</strong>, over which the average is taken.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">3</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Daily and weekly ceilings</strong> that still apply inside it.</div></div>
</div>

Changing a shift pattern and calling it flexible working adopts nothing. If
your employer is averaging your hours, there is a document that says so, and
you are entitled to know which scheme it is.

## Special extended work is an authorisation, not a decision

Work beyond the cap on grounds of disaster, accident or other statutory
special circumstances runs through a procedure: the statutory grounds, the
employee's consent, and the Minister's authorisation or after-the-fact
approval.

<div class="callout callout-note">
  <p class="callout-title">Business pressure is not a ground</p>
  <p>A tight delivery date, a surge in orders, or being short-staffed are
  management problems. They do not, on their own, convert into the statutory
  exception — and an employer that treats them as one has not obtained
  anything, it has simply exceeded the cap.</p>
</div>

## The sanction is criminal

Breach of the working hours limits carries imprisonment of up to two years or
a fine of up to
[₩20,000,000](/tracked/working-hours-violation-max-fine/), separately from any
liability for unpaid premiums.

Which changes what the evidence is. Attendance records, building access logs,
PC sessions, work messaging, email timestamps and the instructions actually
given are all capable of establishing long hours — and they are largely the
employer's own systems.

## The order to check in

```
ordinary headcount at the workplace
  → contractual working hours
  → hours actually worked
  → over 8 in a day?
  → over 40 in a week?
  → extended hours within 12?
  → is a flexible or selective scheme lawfully in place?
  → then, separately, the premiums
```

Everything after the last arrow — the 50% rates, how extended and night work
stack, the two tiers of holiday work, and why a comprehensive wage clause does
not remove any of it — is the subject of its own guide, because it is a
different regulation with different failure modes.

The sentence to distrust in either direction is "we are within 52 hours, so
there is nothing to pay" and its mirror, "we pay for the overtime, so the
hours are fine." Both halves have to be true at once.
