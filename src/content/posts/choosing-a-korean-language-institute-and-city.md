Two decisions come before the school's name, and neither of them is really
about teaching.

**Which institution you choose determines what status you can hold**, and
therefore how long you may stay. **Which city you choose determines what a
year costs**, and therefore whether you can finish. Those are the
decisions. The brochure is downstream of both.

<p class="hand">Not "which school is better". Which one supports the stay you need.</p>

## 1 · The institution question is a status question

If the plan is to study Korean over a long period, register as a foreign
resident, and possibly progress to a university or graduate programme, the
route normally examined first is a **regular course at a
university-affiliated Korean language institute**.

The government's Study in Korea listings show Korean language training
courses run as D-4 or D-4-1 at university after university — Mokwon,
Paichai, Dong-eui, Kyung Hee and Kwangwoon among many others.

<div class="callout callout-note">
  <p class="callout-title">The rule is about the course, not the category</p>
  <p>It is tempting to say "only universities can sponsor a D-4-1", and that
  is too narrow. The requirement is that the <strong>institution and the
  course</strong> meet the standards set for hosting foreign students on that
  status. University-affiliated institutes are the representative route, not
  a legal monopoly. So the question to ask is always about the specific
  course: <em>does this one support D-4-1?</em> — in writing, from the
  institute.</p>
</div>

What a private academy generally is **not** is a D-4-1 sponsor. Which does
not mean you cannot study at one.

## 2 · A private academy is a different arrangement, not a lesser one

Attending an academy and holding a status are two separate things. The
usual pattern is studying at one **within a status you already hold**.

That makes academies a good fit for a specific set of people:

- Someone already on **E-7, F-2, F-4, F-5 or F-6** who wants better Korean
  around a job and a life that already exists.
- Someone visiting Korea for weeks or a few months for conversation
  practice or a cultural programme.

And it makes them the wrong instrument for someone whose right to be in
Korea depends on studying.

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">Settle your own eligibility before paying</p>
  <p>If you are coming specifically to attend a private academy, the question
  is not the academy's paperwork — it is yours. Whether your nationality has
  visa-free entry, whether K-ETA applies to you, and which short-stay status
  fits your purpose and its permitted length. Confirm that first. Paying
  tuition and then discovering the permitted stay is shorter than the course
  is an expensive sequence.</p>
</div>

## 3 · What a university institute course actually looks like

Regular courses run on terms. A shape that recurs across many institutes
is **ten weeks of about 200 hours, with four intakes a year** aligned to
spring, summer, autumn and winter.

It is common enough to plan around and it is **not a national standard** —
curricula differ by school, so read the course page for the institute you
are actually considering.

Levels run from beginner to advanced, with attendance and grades managed
together. Some courses set **80% attendance** as a condition for
progressing a level or completing the course, and school materials
describing a ten-week 200-hour course alongside an 80% attendance and
minimum grade requirement are easy to find.

That 80% is the institute's own threshold. It is not a statutory cut-off,
and treating it as the pass mark for your visa misreads how extensions are
decided — the full picture is in
[keeping D-4 status](/study/alien-registration-for-language-students/).

**The consequence worth internalising:** at a university institute your
enrolment, attendance, tuition record and academic history are not just
school administration. For a long-stay student they become the material
your later extensions are assessed on.

## 4 · Side by side, by purpose

| | University-affiliated institute | Private academy |
|---|---|---|
| Main purpose | Long-term study, progression to a degree | Conversation, short courses, specific goals |
| D-4-1 | Available on qualifying courses | Generally not a sponsor route |
| Structure | Regular terms | Wide choice of schedule |
| Typical load | Ten-week, roughly 200-hour courses are common | Varies greatly by course |
| Attendance and grades | Managed strictly | Varies by academy |
| Alien registration | Required on a long D-4 stay | Depends on the status you hold |
| Progression to D-2 | Well suited to preparing for it | Prepared separately |
| Day-to-day freedom | Lower | Higher |

The useful question is therefore not *which is better*. It is **why are
you studying Korean**.

If the answer involves a degree or a long stay, the administrative value
of a university course is the point of it. If you already hold a status,
or you are here for a few months, the high contact hours and strict
attendance of a regular course are a cost rather than a benefit.

## 5 · The city question is a cash-flow question

Seoul gives you the widest choice of universities, the cultural
infrastructure, the largest foreign community, the best access to
employment information, and the most services that work in English.

It also gives you the highest deposits and rents. Around the campuses —
Sinchon, Hongdae, Seongbuk, Dongdaemun, the Gangnam side — one-room and
officetel prices sit well above the equivalent near major regional
universities.

Regional cities are a different arithmetic. **Busan, Daegu, Daejeon and
Gwangju** all have large universities with Korean language institutes, and
generally lower dormitory and housing costs. The Study in Korea search
lets you filter by region, by whether a university has a Korean language
institute, and by whether a dormitory is provided — so you can compare
schools city by city rather than one at a time.

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">"Regional is cheaper" is a tendency, not a rule</p>
  <p>Parts of Busan — Haeundae among them — and newly built officetels
  anywhere can cost more than outer Seoul. And a dormitory place is not
  guaranteed to every language student at every school. Check the specific
  campus and the specific building, not the city's reputation.</p>
</div>

## 6 · The language environment cuts both ways

Seoul is easy to live in with English. University districts, tourist
areas, large hospitals and commercial facilities are well signposted, and
the foreign community is large.

For immersion, that is a disadvantage. In a regional city more situations
resist English — the bank, the restaurant, the estate agent, the bus — so
everyday Korean gets used because using it is the shortest path.

But the city does not do the work. **A student who socialises only with
other foreigners will speak little Korean in Busan or in Seoul.** Choose
the city to fit your budget and how you want to live, not because a map
promises fluency.

## 7 · Region, and what comes after

| | Seoul and the capital region | Busan, Daegu, Daejeon, Gwangju |
|---|---|---|
| Choice of universities | Very wide | Concentrated on major institutions |
| Housing cost | High | Often materially lower |
| Dormitory | Competitive | Often a relative advantage |
| Foreign community | Large | Smaller |
| Living in English | Easier | Varies by area and facility |
| Chances to use Korean | Can shrink, depending how you live | Often greater |
| Employment and networking | Heavily concentrated here | Tied to regional industry |
| Cost of a long stay | Heavy | Easier to manage |

**You do not have to take your degree at the university whose institute
taught you Korean.** Studying at a regional institute, taking TOPIK, and
then entering a Seoul university on D-2 is an ordinary path — and a
deliberate one for students who want cheap housing during the language
year. The reverse is equally sensible: if you already know you want to
study in Busan or Daejeon, starting there means adapting to one city
instead of two.

## 8 · Compare total cost of stay, not tuition

Tuition is the number schools publish, which is why it is the number
people compare. It is rarely the number that decides anything.

The real figure is:

```
tuition
  + dormitory, or deposit and monthly rent
  + maintenance charges
  + health insurance and other residence costs
  + transport + food + phone + books
```

A Seoul school ₩200,000 cheaper per term than a regional one, with rent
₩300,000 higher per month, is not cheaper. Over a year it is far more
expensive, and the difference lives entirely in housing.

So for a long D-4 course, compare **twelve months of total cost of stay**.
The deposit and rent structure is in
[jeonse and wolse explained](/cost-of-living/jeonse-vs-wolse-korean-rental-system/)
and maintenance charges in
[utilities and maintenance fees](/cost-of-living/utilities-and-maintenance-fees-korea/);
the assembled figure is in
**the 3, 6 and 12-month budget**.

## 9 · In practice there are three combinations

<div class="steps">
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">A</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Degree or employment in Korea is the goal.</strong> University institute (Seoul or regional) → D-4-1 → TOPIK → D-2 → D-10 or E-7 after graduation. The most common shape, and the one the administrative record is built for.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">B</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>The budget is tight.</strong> A regional hub university's institute plus its dormitory, then move to the university you actually want once the degree begins. This is combination A with the housing cost removed from the most expensive year.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">C</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Weeks or a few months, for conversation or culture.</strong> Your own lawful short-stay status → a private academy anywhere you like → a short course. And for someone already on E-7 or an F-series status, evening and weekend academy classes are simply more realistic than a full-time regular course.</div></div>
</div>

## 10 · Four things to confirm before you choose a school

Reputation is the last of these, not the first.

<div class="steps">
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">1</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Does this specific course support D-4-1?</strong> In writing, from the institute — not inferred from the fact that it teaches foreigners.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">2</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>What are the contact hours, and the attendance and grade requirements?</strong> Per term, from the course page.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">3</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Is a dormitory available, and what is twelve months of housing actually going to cost?</strong> Including whether a place is guaranteed beyond the first term.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">4</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Is there a next status?</strong> D-2 for a degree, D-10 or E-7 for work. Decide now, because it sets how many terms you are budgeting for.</div></div>
</div>

What happens after you land — registration, attendance, extensions and
permission to work part-time — is in
[keeping D-4 status](/study/alien-registration-for-language-students/).
The city-by-city cost comparison is in
**Seoul or a regional city**.

Choosing an institute is not choosing an education product. It is choosing
a period of stay. And choosing a city is not a matter of taste — it is
designing six or twelve months of cash flow.
