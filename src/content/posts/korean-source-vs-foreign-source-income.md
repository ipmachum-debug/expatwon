You live in Seoul and work remotely for an American company. The salary lands
in a US bank account. The company has no Korean office and nothing reaches
your Korean bank.

Foreign-source income?

Not necessarily.

Now the other direction. You live in Korea and own an apartment in Vietnam.
The tenant pays rent into a Vietnamese account, and later you move some of it
to Korea.

Korean-source now?

No. Moving money to Korea does not change where the rent arose.

**Where money is paid is not where income is sourced.** Korean tax law
separates domestic-source from foreign-source income, and that line decides
what falls inside Korea's taxing jurisdiction. For certain foreign residents it
also decides whether the five-years-in-ten rule can limit the foreign-source
income Korea reaches.

There is an order to it. Residence first. Then the income category. Then the
source. Only then do payment location, remittances and treaties mean anything.
A foreign bank account does not shortcut any of that, and neither does an
overseas employer, a foreign client or a payment in dollars.

## Residence comes first

A Korean tax resident is generally taxed on domestic and foreign income within
the scope the Income Tax Act prescribes. A non-resident is generally taxed
only on Korean-source income under art. 119.

The test is not nationality or visa type: domestic law looks at domicile in
Korea or a place of residence for 183 days or more, read with the factual
circumstances — occupation, family, housing, where economic and personal life
sits. Which is why someone who arrived recently cannot assume non-residence
because 183 days have not elapsed, and why a residence card settles nothing.

That question is [income tax for foreigners: resident or
not](/business/income-tax-for-foreigners-resident-or-not/). This guide starts
after it, with the position established, and asks a different question: which
of your income is Korean-source.

## There is no single sourcing rule

Korean tax law does not apply one universal test. Employment income and
dividends do not follow the same rule; neither do rent, interest, royalties
and business profits.

Article 119 identifies the Korean-source categories for non-residents —
interest, dividends, income from Korean real estate, business income,
specified personal-service income, employment income, royalties, certain gains
— and the conditions differ by category. So the classification has to come
first.

Four payments: salary for duties performed in Korea; interest from an overseas
bank on a deposit held abroad; a dividend from a Korean company to a
shareholder living overseas; rent on a property outside Korea. All four can
land in the same foreign account, and the source analysis still comes out
differently for each.

**Source follows the legal character of the income, not the name of the bank
receiving it.**

## Employment: where did you do the work?

For ordinary employment income, the place the duties are performed is central.
Article 119 identifies remuneration for employment performed in Korea, with
certain other prescribed employment income, as Korean-source.

So: a US company employs a software engineer who lives in Seoul and performs
every relevant duty from a home office in Korea, paid in dollars into a US
account under an English-language contract. The employer is American. The
money never touches Korea.

None of that makes the salary foreign-source. The work happened in Korea.

There are real further questions — the foreign employer's payroll and
withholding position, the employee's residence, the applicable treaty — and
they need their own analysis. But the payment location alone does not convert
Korean-performed employment into foreign-source income.

**Reverse it.** A non-resident performs all duties at an overseas office of a
Korean company, and Korean headquarters pays. A Korean payer does not
automatically turn compensation for work done entirely abroad into
Korean-source employment income. The NTS guidance distinguishes employment
performed abroad from employment performed in Korea, subject to special
provisions and treaty rules.

Record both: the employer's location, and where the employee actually worked.
The payer matters. The payer is not the whole test.

## The remote worker's expensive assumption

Someone works from Korea for an overseas client, is paid into a foreign
account, and calls it overseas earnings. The label sounds fair — foreign
client, foreign money, maybe foreign governing law.

It is not how source works. For employment income the location of the duties
is central. For independent services and business income the classification
and the source provisions need examining against the activities actually
performed, and the answer can turn on whether the person is an employee, an
independent personal-service provider or a business operator.

A developer working from Korea for foreign clients should not assume
foreign-source because the client is abroad. The Korean activity has to be
looked at. And the reverse holds: genuinely foreign activity paid by a Korean
company is not settled by the Korean address either.

Work split across countries is harder again. A salary or fee covering both
Korean and overseas work may need allocating on the actual activity, and then
the contract, the work records and the travel history become the evidence.

**Remote is a work arrangement, not a source classification.**

## Property: the building does not move

A foreign individual owns an apartment in Seoul, rents it out, and the tenant
pays into an account outside Korea. The apartment is still in Seoul, and
Korea's domestic-source rules reach income arising from real estate situated
in Korea. The overseas account changes nothing.

Reverse it: a Korean tax resident owns an apartment in Vietnam, and the tenant
pays into a Vietnamese account. The income arises from property outside Korea,
so it is a foreign-source candidate on the classification. If the owner later
remits money to Korea, the rent does not change geographic source — but the
remittance may decide whether that income falls within Korea's taxable scope
for a qualifying foreign resident.

**Source is where the income arose. Remittance is where money moved
afterwards.** The second only matters once the first is settled.

## Interest: look at who pays it

Article 119 identifies Korean-source interest by reference to the payer and
its relationship with Korea. Interest paid by a Korean resident or domestic
corporation can fall inside those rules; interest paid by a foreign bank on a
qualifying deposit held outside Korea may be foreign-source. The actual
contract and payer structure still need checking.

| Account | Interest payer | Likely sourcing |
|---|---|---|
| Foreign-currency deposit at a Korean bank | Korean financial institution | Generally Korean-source |
| Deposit at an overseas bank | Foreign financial institution | Generally foreign-source, subject to the arrangement |

Currency is not decisive either way. A dollar deposit at a Korean bank does
not yield foreign-source interest because it is denominated in dollars, and
converting overseas interest into won does not change where it arose. If the
interest is already Korean-source, moving it to an overseas account does not
take it out of Korean tax.

## Dividends: look at the company, not the app

The brokerage's location is not the source of the dividend. A dividend from a
Korean domestic corporation is generally Korean-source; one from a foreign
corporation is a foreign-source candidate.

- **Foreign shares, overseas brokerage** — foreign-source candidate.
- **Korean shares, overseas brokerage** — the overseas brokerage does not make
  it foreign-source. The payer is a Korean corporation.
- **Foreign shares, Korean brokerage** — receiving it through Korea does not
  change the underlying foreign character, but the payment and settlement
  route matters when applying the paid-in-Korea condition.

That last one is the subtle case. A foreign-source dividend can stay
foreign-source and still be taxable in Korea because it was paid in Korea.
**Source and taxability are two questions.**

## Business income: where is the activity?

Not where the customer is. A Korean-registered business may sell abroad; a
foreign-registered business may operate substantially in Korea; a self-employed
person may work from several countries in one year.

Someone running an online consulting business while physically working in
Korea, with every client overseas, has not thereby established that the income
is entirely foreign-source. The activity carried out in Korea has to be
considered. For non-residents, art. 119 identifies specified Korean-source
business income and, separately, particular personal-service income — and
treaties commonly restrict Korea's right to tax business profits through
permanent-establishment provisions, with independent personal services sitting
in a dedicated article in some treaties and inside business profits in others.

So several questions, not one: what kind of service, performed where, received
by a resident or non-resident, inside which statutory category, and modified by
which treaty. A payment from an overseas customer answers none of them.

**Foreign customer revenue and foreign-source income are not the same term.**

## The five-in-ten-year rule, and what it does not do

Article 3 subjects residents to tax on the income the Act prescribes, with an
exception for qualifying foreign residents: where cumulative domicile or
residence in Korea during the ten years preceding the end of the tax year is
five years or less, foreign-source income is taxed only where it is **paid in
Korea or remitted to Korea**. The rule itself, and what happens when the
threshold passes, is in the [residence
guide](/business/income-tax-for-foreigners-resident-or-not/).

What matters here is what it does not reach. It is not a rule that foreigners
are exempt from foreign income tax for five calendar years. It is not a visa
benefit. It is not a rule that overseas transfers are tax-free. And it limits
the scope of **foreign-source** income only — Korean-source income is not
exempt because someone has been in Korea under five years.

Which is why source has to be settled before the exception is applied at all.

### Five years is cumulative, not consecutive

Someone who lived in Korea, left, and came back is not assessed on the current
uninterrupted stay. The test looks at cumulative qualifying domicile or
residence across the ten-year window ending with the tax year, so an earlier
period can count — and an old one can eventually drop out of the window.
Writing down the most recent arrival year is not the calculation.

A usable residence record holds every relevant period of Korean domicile or
residence, entry and departure dates, housing and employment arrangements,
where the permanent home was, the family and economic circumstances, and the
calculation for each tax year.

The threshold is inclusive — five years **or less**. Once the cumulative
period exceeds it, the exception is not available for that year on that basis.
Follow the legal rules rather than counting every calendar year in which you
visited, and note that a brief absence does not automatically end Korean
domicile.

## When foreign income never comes to Korea

A qualifying foreign resident receives dividends from an overseas corporation
into an overseas account, does not receive them in Korea and does not remit
them. Under the rule, qualifying foreign-source income outside the
paid-in-Korea and remitted-to-Korea conditions can fall outside Korea's
taxable scope for that person — materially different from a resident who no
longer qualifies.

But that depends on the income genuinely being foreign-source. The same person
receiving pay for employment performed in Korea cannot treat it as
foreign-source because it was deposited overseas. **Source first. Remittance
second.**

It also matters which year the money is from. A foreign account can hold
current income, old savings, sale proceeds and loan funds at once, and without
identifying what a balance represents, a transfer cannot establish how much
taxable foreign-source income was remitted.

## A worked case: ₩12 million of foreign rent

Assume a Korean tax resident who qualifies for the exception, owning overseas
property that produces ₩12 million of foreign-source income for the year.
Assume the classification is correct, no treaty changes it, and the
remittances trace to that income.

| | Foreign income | Paid in or remitted to Korea |
|---|---:|---:|
| A | ₩12,000,000 | ₩0 |
| B | ₩12,000,000 | ₩4,000,000 |
| C | ₩12,000,000 | ₩12,000,000 |

In **A** neither condition is met on these assumptions, so the rule may
exclude the income. In **B**, ₩4 million creates a taxable-scope question for
the remitted amount, with the statutory rules and the facts deciding what is
actually included. In **C** the exception cannot exclude income merely because
the property is overseas.

Two warnings. This shows how the rule operates; it is not a formula for
splitting every transfer between income and capital, and mixed accounts or
prior-year balances need their own attribution analysis.

🔴 And ₩4 million being within scope does not mean ₩4 million of tax. The
liability depends on the income calculation, deductions, other income and
credits. **The remitted amount and the tax payable are different figures.**

## Sending your own savings is not automatically income

Savings accumulated before moving to Korea, later transferred in, are not a
new income event because a transfer happened. A movement of existing capital
is not necessarily income at all. But where the funds connect to
foreign-source income, the remittance condition has to be examined.

| Funds in an overseas account | The question |
|---|---|
| Savings from before the Korean residence period | Their origin, and the effect of remitting them |
| This year's foreign dividends | Whether qualifying income was paid or remitted to Korea |
| Foreign employment income | Where the duties were performed |
| Proceeds from selling an overseas asset | Capital recovery separated from any taxable gain |
| Loan proceeds | Whether it is genuine borrowing rather than income |

No rule makes every incoming transfer taxable income. Equally, labelling a
transfer "savings" does not establish its origin where the funds are recent
foreign income. Keep the original amounts, the dates earned, the balances and
the transfers — and for a mixed account get advice on attribution and timing
rather than assuming the taxpayer picks which part of the balance moved.

## "Paid in Korea" is not only about transfers

The statutory wording covers foreign-source income **paid in Korea**, not just
money moved after it reaches an overseas account.

A qualifying foreign resident holds an overseas investment and arranges for the
payment to arrive directly in Korea instead of abroad. The income can still be
foreign-source — and the payment location can still bring it inside Korea's
taxable scope under art. 3.

**Foreign-source does not mean exempt.** The source stays foreign; the
condition deciding whether Korea taxes it has been met. So records should
identify the original source *and* the payment route, and for investments held
through intermediaries the settlement and crediting arrangements deserve a
look. Do not infer it from the brokerage's country of registration.

## What changes once you pass the threshold

Availability depends on a rolling cumulative period, so it ends without
anything in your finances changing. Past the threshold, the general resident
scope matters more, and foreign-source income can be taxable in Korea even
where it stays overseas and is never remitted.

Someone with foreign investments, overseas rent and employment income earned
abroad may find that the payment and remittance conditions limited the scope
in one year and do not in the next. That does not mean foreign tax paid is
ignored, or that every overseas asset generates taxable income — the income
still has to be identified and computed, relief for foreign tax may be
available, and the treaty may affect it.

**The threshold can change the scope of your return while your bank accounts
and investments stay exactly the same.**

## A treaty can move the domestic-law answer

Korea has bilateral treaties with many jurisdictions, published through the
NTS. A treaty may allocate or restrict taxing rights over employment income,
business profits, independent personal services, dividends, interest,
royalties, income from immovable property, capital gains, pensions and
directors' fees — and not every treaty words these the same way. Some have a
dedicated independent-personal-services article; others do not.

Treaty residence needs attention too. A person can be resident under two
countries' domestic laws, and the treaty's tie-breakers — permanent home,
centre of vital interests, habitual abode — resolve it. The analysis is not
choosing whichever country taxes less: establish the Korean-law position
first, then identify the treaty and see how it affects the income.

**A treaty changes taxing rights. It does not turn one kind of payment into
another.**

## Two different 183-day rules

The number 183 appears in Korea's domestic residence test *and* in treaty
provisions on employment income and services. They are not the same test.

The domestic question is whether you are a Korean tax resident. A treaty's
short-term employment exemption can involve separate conditions about days of
presence, who the employer is and whether a permanent establishment bears the
remuneration. Staying under 183 days does not qualify you for every treaty
exemption, and meeting the domestic threshold does not mean treaty provisions
stop mattering.

For work split across two countries this can decide the outcome — so keep the
dates and locations of work, not just the country that paid.

## Overseas shares: three questions, not one

Shares held through an overseas brokerage rise in value, are sold, and the
money comes to Korea. Ask: did a taxable realisation occur; is the gain
Korean- or foreign-source; and does Korea have the right to tax it given
residence, statutory limits and treaty.

Sale proceeds are not the gain. Bought at ₩20 million and sold at ₩27 million,
the proceeds are ₩27 million and the simple difference is ₩7 million before
adjustments — describing the full ₩27 million as profit is wrong, and the
taxable gain is not reliably exactly ₩7 million either, once acquisition
costs, transaction expenses, currency conversion and the statutory rules are
applied.

Capital gains may also run on a filing regime separate from comprehensive
income tax, so overseas share transactions should not be dropped into a May
return as ordinary business income. The foreign-source classification and the
five-in-ten-year question still apply where relevant — after the capital-gains
treatment is established. A brokerage balance is not a transaction-level
calculation.

## Foreign tax paid does not decide the source

A foreign company withholds on a dividend; an overseas tenant's rent is taxed
locally; an overseas payroll provider withholds from salary. None of that
determines whether Korea regards the income as domestic- or foreign-source.
Different countries apply different domestic rules and may claim taxing rights
over the same income on different bases.

Keep three questions apart:

```
where was the income sourced     →  its legal character and the source rules
does Korea tax it                →  residence, scope, exceptions, treaty
how is double taxation relieved  →  credits, deductions, treaty relief
```

The third cannot be answered until the first two are. The relief calculation
is its own guide.

## Overseas accounts are a separate regime

Income tax filing and overseas financial-account reporting are different
obligations. You can hold an overseas account without much taxable income from
it, and you can earn foreign-source income without a reportable account in
your own name.

The account-reporting system has its own conditions on who reports, balances,
periods and exemptions, and the five-in-ten-year income exception does not
settle the reporting outcome. Assess them independently: a reporting
obligation can exist for an account whose income is not in the comprehensive
calculation, and taxable foreign-source income does not mean every account is
reportable. Keep the information now, analyse the reporting separately.

## Four cases, four reasons

Each assumes the classification is correct and no treaty provision changes it.

| Situation | Likely source | Why |
|---|---|---|
| US employer pays into a US account for work done in Seoul | Korean-source | Duties performed in Korea |
| Vietnamese tenant pays into a Vietnamese account on Vietnamese property | Foreign-source | Income from property abroad |
| Korean corporation pays a dividend into an overseas brokerage | Korean-source | Korean corporate payer |
| Foreign corporation pays a dividend through a Korean brokerage | Foreign-source candidate | Foreign payer; payment route reviewed separately |

What the table really shows is what source is *not*. Not where the payer is
headquartered. Not where the account is. Not the currency. And not what the
taxpayer calls it.

## What to keep

Bank statements show payments moving. They rarely establish where employment
was performed, where a business operated, or which asset produced the income.
Organise by income type instead.

| Income | Records |
|---|---|
| Employment | Contract, payroll statements, work-location records, travel dates |
| Independent services | Agreements, invoices, where the work was performed |
| Rental | Ownership documents, lease, rent statements, local expenses and tax |
| Interest | Statements, deposit agreements, payer information |
| Dividends | Shareholding records, dividend statements, the company |
| Capital gains | Acquisition and disposal records, cost evidence, fees |
| Remittances | Source-account statements, transfer confirmations, what the funds were |

For the five-in-ten-year rule, also keep what supports the cumulative
residence period. Anyone expecting to rely on the payment or remittance
condition should be able to say which foreign-source income was received or
transferred, and when — which matters most where one account holds old savings
and several kinds of current income.

The point of the file is not to make every transaction taxable. It is to
establish which amounts belong in the calculation.

## One worksheet, before any rate

A foreign tax resident in Korea receives: ₩48 million of salary for employment
performed in Seoul, paid by an overseas company; ₩12 million of net rent from a
Vietnamese property; ₩3 million of dividends from a foreign corporation; and ₩2
million of interest from a Korean bank. Assume the five-in-ten-year exception
applies.

The first task is not the tax rate.

| Income | Amount | Source |
|---|---:|---|
| Salary, Seoul employment | ₩48,000,000 | Korean-source candidate |
| Net Vietnamese rent | ₩12,000,000 | Foreign-source candidate |
| Foreign corporation dividends | ₩3,000,000 | Foreign-source candidate |
| Korean bank interest | ₩2,000,000 | Korean-source candidate |

Now the payment arrangements. The Seoul salary goes to a US account. Most of
the Vietnamese rent stays in Vietnam. The foreign dividend goes to an overseas
brokerage. The Korean interest is credited in Korea.

The salary and the Korean interest do not become foreign-source because money
sits outside Korea. The foreign rent and the foreign dividend need the
paid-in-Korea or remitted-to-Korea analysis. What ends up in Korean taxation
cannot be fixed without the transfer records and the rules — and the items in
this table will not all be combined in one comprehensive calculation, because
some financial income is separately taxed. Treatment is established by
category.

The worksheet does one job: it separates source from payment arrangements
before anyone calculates tax.

## Five errors, one cause

**A foreign account means foreign-source income.** The destination is not where
the income arose.

**A foreign employer means foreign-source salary.** The employer's location is
not the test for employment income.

**Being a tax resident means every overseas payment is taxable.** A qualifying
foreign resident may have the five-in-ten-year exception — tested on the real
residence history and the payment conditions.

**Transferring savings means the whole transfer is income.** It may be nothing;
it may be a remittance of identifiable foreign-source income. Establish what
the funds are.

**Foreign tax paid means no Korean tax.** Withholding abroad does not
extinguish Korean obligations; treaty and relief have to be examined.

All five do the same thing — take one fact as a substitute for the analysis. A
foreign account, a foreign employer, a foreign tax receipt: each is evidence.
None is an answer.

## The order

```
residence
  → income category
  → source
  → paid in or remitted to Korea
  → treaty
  → filing method
  → relief for foreign tax
  → separate reporting obligations
```

Residence decides whose rules apply, and where there is dual residence the
treaty position. Category separates employment, business, interest,
dividends, rent and gains. Source applies the statutory provisions to the
actual income — not the bank's country by default. The exception is tested
only then, the treaty after that, and the filing method after that, because
not every item belongs in the same return.

Run it in that order and the rate arrives at the end, applied to the right
income. Run it backwards and you apply a correct rate to the wrong set — which
is how a taxpayer reports income Korea never taxed, omits income it does, or
leans on an exception that was not available.

**The dividing line is not the border the money crosses. It is where the
income legally arises.**
