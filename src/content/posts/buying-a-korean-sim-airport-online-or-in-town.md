Three ways to get connected: collect at the airport, order before you fly,
or walk into a store once you are here. When everything works they end in
the same place.

They are not the same purchase, and the difference only appears at the
moment something fails. **Compare them on recovery, not on price.**

<p class="hand">The cheapest option is not the one that failed at 23:40 in Incheon.</p>

Which product to buy is a separate question, answered in
[eSIM, USIM or prepaid SIM in Korea](/cost-of-living/esim-usim-or-prepaid-sim-in-korea/).
This guide is only about **where you get it and how you recover**.

## The airport is buying support, not convenience

KT runs roaming centres at Incheon Terminals 1 and 2, Gimpo and Gimhae,
with **round-the-clock cover at Incheon T1 arrivals** — tracked at
[airport counter hours](/tracked/airport-sim-counter-hours/), since these
move with terminal operations.

What you are actually buying there is somebody who can look at the problem:
a SIM the handset will not read, voice and SMS that have not come alive, an
eSIM profile that failed halfway, a passport detail that does not match. All
of it is solvable **before you leave the building**.

<div class="callout callout-note">
  <p class="callout-title">Even an online purchase may need the counter</p>
  <p>Some tourist products activate voice and SMS only after entry has been
  confirmed. KT states that its tourist (e)SIM provides those services to
  customers who have completed the entry check, and that airport roaming
  centres can process it. So the counter is not merely a retail desk you can
  skip by buying ahead — for some products it is <strong>part of
  activation</strong>.</p>
</div>

Do not treat wait times as a fixed number. A quiet mid-morning arrival and a
bank of simultaneous landings are different experiences, and the honest
statement is that activation and testing can normally be finished on the
spot while busy periods add queueing.

## Do not walk away until four things are true

Being handed a SIM is not the same as being connected.

<div class="steps">
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">1</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Data works</strong> — and works with airport Wi-Fi switched off. This is the test people skip, and it is the one that matters: a page loading over airport Wi-Fi tells you nothing about whether the line is live.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">2</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>The number is visible</strong> — you can see your own 010 in the handset.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">3</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>A call connects</strong> — if the product includes voice.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">4</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>A test SMS arrives</strong> — if the product includes messaging. This is where an incomplete entry check shows up.</div></div>
</div>

On a data-only product, steps three and four do not apply — but step one
still does, and it still has to be done off Wi-Fi.

## Ordering ahead is fastest when it works

Two shapes: an eSIM QR or profile emailed before departure, or a physical
SIM shipped to your accommodation.

The eSIM needs no collection at all. Prepare before you fly, activate on
landing, and it is the quickest of the three by some margin.

Its weakness is that success depends on your handset and on a record you
cannot see. The recurring failures:

- carrier lock
- a handset that does not support eSIM
- profile installation failing partway
- APN settings
- roaming settings
- passport details that do not match the order
- entry check not completed

KT's guidance for its tourist eSIM states plainly that use can be
restricted where the information given at purchase does not match the actual
passport details.

So the question to ask yourself about ordering ahead is not whether it is
cheaper. It is **whether you can debug it alone, in a terminal, with no
connection.**

## Install groundwork before departure, not at the gate

If you go the eSIM route, settle these at home:

```
exact handset model
  → does it support eSIM
  → is it carrier locked
  → what are the QR's validity conditions
  → when may it be installed
  → how is it activated after landing
```

A handset bought on contract abroad may be locked, and a Korean profile
will not install on it.

And treat the QR as **single-use until told otherwise**. Some profiles can
be downloaded once per device, so scanning repeatedly or deleting a
half-installed profile to "start again" can leave you with a code that no
longer works.

## The city store has the best range and the worst first hour

A store in Seoul, or in a university district, gives a long-stay arrival
what the airport cannot: the full spread of prepaid and postpaid plans,
staff who see foreign customers constantly, and a recommendation matched to
your actual stay. SK Telecom publishes lists of T world stores for foreign
customers and stores with foreign-language support.

The cost is the gap. You have to reach the store — checking the airport
railway, contacting your accommodation, calling a taxi — with no phone
service to do any of it.

So if this is the plan, arrange the bridge deliberately: airport Wi-Fi,
roaming on your existing foreign line, or a short data eSIM for the first
day.

## The three routes, compared honestly

| | Speed | Fixing it on the spot | Choice of plan | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Airport counter** | High | Highest | Mostly tourist products | Queues, price, limited range |
| **Online eSIM** | Highest | Lowest | Wide | Locks, settings, records |
| **Online physical SIM** | Depends on delivery | Low | Wide | Delivery, loss, activation |
| **City store** | After you travel in | High | Widest | No service until you arrive |

Note what is not in that table: a claim that one is always cheaper. Online
being cheaper than the airport is a common tendency, not a rule —
promotions, carriers and periods reverse it often enough that the price
comparison is worth doing fresh each time.

## Type your name exactly as the passport has it

This is the highest-value detail in the whole guide, and it has published
backing rather than being folklore.

KT's booking lookup asks for **Given Name and Family Name entered in
capitals, exactly as registered on the passport**, and its eSIM guidance
states that use can be restricted where purchase details do not match actual
passport details.

So when you order:

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">Five ways foreign names break a booking</p>
  <p>Dropping a middle name · swapping given and family name order · adding a
  hyphen that is not on the passport · using a nickname or an anglicised form
  · abbreviating a spelling. Use the romanised name exactly as printed on the
  passport photo page.</p>
</div>

**This is not about typing the MRZ.** Carriers do not generally ask you to
enter that string, and "one character off the MRZ means automatic
rejection" overstates it. The requirement is simpler and stricter in
practice: the booking details and the passport have to agree.

### If they already disagree

Do not immediately buy a replacement. Show the booking reference and the
passport, and ask whether the subscriber details on **that booking** can be
amended.

Spacing and name-order differences can sometimes be corrected on the spot.
Other products need a new contract. And an online eSIM already issued is the
hardest of the three, because cancellation and refund conditions on tourist
eSIMs can be restrictive — which is the real argument for re-reading what
you typed **before** you pay.

## Failures, and which is which

The single most expensive mistake is treating every failure as a faulty SIM.

| Failure | What you see | Check first | Realistic fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Carrier lock** | Handset rejects the SIM or eSIM outright | The handset's lock status | Unlock via the original overseas carrier |
| **eSIM install error** | Profile will not add | The QR and install history | Ask the seller to reissue |
| **APN** | Signal but no data | APN, and which line carries data | Re-enter the official APN |
| **Name mismatch** | Sign-up or entry check fails | Booking name against passport | Amend at the counter or with support |
| **Entry check pending** | Data works, voice and SMS do not | Entry confirmation status | Airport counter |
| **Faulty SIM** | Unstable recognition | Try another handset | Ask about on-site replacement |

### Carrier lock cannot be fixed here

No Korean carrier can unlock a handset locked by an overseas one. A staff
member telling you "it is the phone" is being accurate and still leaving you
with a problem only your home carrier can solve.

Buying another SIM does not help. The bridge is a different unlocked handset,
or roaming on your existing foreign line while you sort it out.

### APN: check which line carries data first

If the SIM is recognised but data will not run, start with **which line is
set as the mobile data line**. Dual-SIM users very often still have the old
foreign SIM holding that role.

Then data roaming, then the APN — which is usually automatic, and sometimes
needs entering by hand on overseas Android models and some MVNOs.

**Use the seller's official APN values.** Typing in another carrier's
settings found through a search engine is how a working line stops working.

### eSIM profile: stop scanning and ask

If installation failed partway, check the seller's reinstall policy before
scanning again:

```
screenshot the failure
  → find the order number
  → check whether a profile was already installed or deleted
  → contact the seller
  → ask whether a new QR can be issued
```

### Data works but calls do not

This is the entry-check case, not an APN problem. Some tourist products
enable voice and SMS only after entry is confirmed, and the airport roaming
centre is where that gets done — which is exactly why a counter still has
value to someone who bought online.

## What fits which trip

| You are | Route that fits | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Here 1–2 weeks, comfortable with eSIM | Online eSIM | Simplest, and you can debug it |
| First visit, need to be reachable on landing | Airport counter | Support is the product |
| Business traveller with meetings from day one | Airport counter | Failure has a cost measured in meetings |
| D-4 or D-2 arriving for a long stay | Airport prepaid, then switch after the card | Bridge first, settle later |
| Been before, have roaming to reach the city | City store | Widest choice, and the gap is covered |

The long-stay pattern is deliberately two-stage. What happens at the second
stage — whether the number survives, and the prepaid balance that does not
— is in
[a Korean number before your registration card](/cost-of-living/korean-phone-number-before-your-arc/).
Arrival products are temporary anyway: tourist SIMs run to a maximum of
**90 days from activation**.

## Always keep one fallback

The genuinely dangerous position on arrival is having prepared exactly one
method and finding it does not work.

The cost of getting connected is not the price of the SIM. It is that price
plus, if it fails, the taxi you could not call, the accommodation you could
not reach, the booking you could not confirm and the message to your school
or employer you could not send.

Measured that way, the right choice is rarely the cheapest one. It is the
one you can still fix the same day.
