Read TOPIK as a language exam and you will aim at level 6. Read it as
**administrative currency** and you will aim at whatever level unlocks the
thing you need next — which is usually much lower, and much sooner.

The score is not a badge. It is a document that gets reused: university
admission, graduation, part-time work permission, scholarship review, and
parts of some status assessments.

## Your institute level is not a TOPIK level

Institutes run their own graded courses, often numbered 1 to 6, which is
why a student says "I finished level 6" and means something entirely local.

They are different instruments:

| | Institute level | TOPIK |
|---|---|---|
| Set by | Your school | NIIED, nationally |
| Assessed on | That term's syllabus, plus attendance and coursework | General Korean ability, no textbook |
| Structure | Varies by school | TOPIK I → levels 1–2 · TOPIK II → levels 3–6 |
| Reusable elsewhere | Within that institution | Anywhere a transcript is accepted |

TOPIK I awards levels 1 and 2 from listening and reading. TOPIK II awards
levels 3 to 6 from listening, writing and reading.

So completing institute level 6 does **not** mean you hold TOPIK level 6.
Nor does a high TOPIK score guarantee placement into a matching institute
level — the placement test is the school's own.

## Why TOPIK travels further

TOPIK is a national test with an official transcript, which is precisely
what makes it convenient: a university or agency can read it without
knowing anything about your school.

<div class="callout callout-note">
  <p class="callout-title">Most portable, not the only one accepted</p>
  <p>It would be wrong to say universities accept nothing else. Depending on
  the institution, a KIIP completion certificate, a KIIP pre-assessment
  score, or a King Sejong Institute certificate can also serve as Korean
  proficiency evidence, and requirements differ by department. The accurate
  statement is that TOPIK is <strong>the most widely reusable</strong> proof,
  not the only admissible one.</p>
</div>

One thing to diary: **a score is valid for two years from the result
announcement** — not from the test date.

## The levels, and what each is actually good for

| Level | Where it does work | The caveat |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | Basic evidence of Korean ability | Little independent administrative effect |
| **2** | The Korean standard for D-4 part-time work | Six months, school confirmation and immigration permission are separate conditions |
| **3** | Generally indicated as entry for Korean-track degrees | Universities and departments raise it |
| **4** | Generally indicated for graduation; strengthens some applications | Not a uniform national requirement |
| **5** | Advanced study, professional work, scholarship review | No automatic award |
| **6** | Highest official evidence of Korean ability | Grants no visa or scholarship by itself |

## Level 2 is the one with the clearest effect

For a D-4-1 trainee this is the sharpest administrative line in the table.

Current guidance sets part-time work eligibility for D-4-1 and D-4-7
holders at **six months after entry or the change of status**, with TOPIK
level 2 — or an equivalent from KIIP or a King Sejong Institute — as the
Korean standard.

The difference it makes is in hours:

```
Standard not met  →  about 10 hours a week,
                     weekdays, weekends and vacation alike

Standard met      →  about 20 weekday hours,
                     with a higher ceiling at weekends and in vacation
```

So level 2 is not merely a beginner's certificate. It is the difference
between working ten hours and twenty.

**And it is not permission on its own.** The six months, the school's
confirmation, the employment contract and immigration's prior approval are
all separate requirements — set out in
[keeping D-4 status](/study/alien-registration-for-language-students/).

## Levels 3 and 4 are the realistic target for a degree

For a D-4 student intending to progress to a Korean university, the gap
between 3 and 4 matters more than the gap between 4 and 6.

The government indicates **level 3 or above to enter** a Korean-track
undergraduate programme and **level 4 or above to graduate**.

Which suggests a better plan than "finish level 6 at the institute":
secure **level 3 first**, then aim for **4 or above before applying**.

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">Two overstatements to avoid</p>
  <p>Level 3 is not "the level that gets you into any university" — it is the
  level at which the conversation can start, and many departments require 4
  from entry. And level 4 is not "safe for the good universities": admission
  weighs academic record, the department, nationality, intake size and
  interviews, and the Korean requirement itself differs by school. Arts,
  English-track, exchange and GKS routes run on different standards
  entirely.</p>
</div>

The band is tracked at
[university Korean-track TOPIK level](/tracked/university-general-topik-admission-level/).

## Levels 5 and 6 widen options; they do not grant things

Both are strong evidence, useful for Korean-heavy degrees and graduate
programmes, for professional roles, and in scholarship review.

There is no national rule attached to either. "Level 5 means a full
scholarship" and "level 6 means admission to a top university" are not
things anyone published — university and foundation scholarships range
across roughly 30% to 100% of tuition on each institution's own criteria.

**GKS** uses TOPIK substantially, but its requirements are set per intake
year and per track, and some selected candidates face follow-up steps tied
to submitting level 5 or 6. Read that year's official guidelines rather
than any general claim.

## E-7 and F-2: be careful here

TOPIK can help with employment and status changes. It is not a common
requirement across them.

**E-7** is assessed on the occupation, education, experience, salary, the
employment contract and the employer's qualifications, and the sub-types
differ. TOPIK can strengthen a case; "level 4 equals E-7 points" is not a
formula that applies across E-7.

**F-2** point-based residence does use Korean ability as a scoring element
in the relevant type, so TOPIK's value there is more direct. But the point
tables and recognised standards change with guidance revisions, so **do
not memorise a points-per-level figure** — check the Ministry of Justice
and HiKorea position at the time you apply.

## PBT and IBT are both the real thing

| | PBT | IBT |
|---|---|---|
| Format | Paper | Computer-based |
| TOPIK I | Listening, reading | Listening, reading |
| TOPIK II | Listening, writing, reading | Listening, writing, reading |
| Recognition | Official TOPIK | Official TOPIK |
| Rounds a year | 6 | 6 |
| Suits | Familiar paper conditions | Comfortable typing |
| Speaking | Separate test | Separate test |

For 2026 the plan indicates **PBT in January, April, May, July, October
and November**, **IBT in February, March, June, September, October and
November**, and the separate **Speaking test three times, in March, June
and October**.

IBT is not a lightweight version and carries no lesser administrative
weight. Confirm which formats and transcripts your target university
accepts in its own prospectus — that is the only place the answer is
binding. Round counts are tracked at
[TOPIK test rounds](/tracked/topik-annual-test-rounds/).

## Speaking is a separate exam

TOPIK II is listening, writing and reading. **There is no speaking
component in it.**

TOPIK Speaking is its own test with its own dates. Sitting IBT does not
get you assessed on speaking. Unless a university or employer specifically
asks for a Speaking score, prepare it as a separate decision rather than
assuming it comes bundled.

## Plan from the result date, not the test date

This is the mistake that costs a semester.

Sitting the test does not give you the level. What administrations accept
is the **officially announced score**, and that lands weeks later.

So work backwards:

## Sit it more than once

A year-long trainee who takes TOPIK once, at the end, carries every risk
in a single attempt: a missed registration, an absence, a score below
target, a result that arrives late.

Staging it is more stable:

```
early    →  secure level 2
middle   →  attempt level 3
later    →  secure level 4 or above
```

There is no rule requiring three sittings. The point is to keep **at least
one retake available** before your D-4 expiry and before the application
deadline that actually matters.

## Decide the date before the level

For language learning, the goal is 5 or 6. For administration the order is
reversed, because each level is needed at a different moment.

holding a valid one when the process that needs it begins** — which is a
scheduling problem, not a language problem.
