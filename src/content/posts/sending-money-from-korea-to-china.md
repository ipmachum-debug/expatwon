Everything on this corridor follows from one question, and it is not which
service is cheapest. It is **how the money is finally paid out in China**.

Alongside an ordinary bank credit, China is served by UnionPay-linked
accounts and by wallets — Alipay and WeChat — acting as real payout rails.
The same CNY transfer reaches a different place depending on which one you
pick, and recipient verification, local limits and settlement time all
change with it. That is unusual: on most corridors "it goes to their bank
account" is a safe assumption. Here it is not.

This guide is only about what differs on the China route. The mechanics that
apply to every destination — why the exchange rate costs more than the fees,
how a SWIFT wire loses money at each bank it crosses, how to compare on the
amount that arrives — are in
[sending money from Korea](/banking/how-to-send-money-from-korea-abroad/),
and the Korean rules on what you may send are in
[managing money in Korea](/cost-of-living/managing-money-in-korea-fx-remittance-cards/).

## Which service, and which rail

Read the payout method the app shows you, not the company name. The same
provider changes rails by country, by the recipient's nationality, and by
which local institution it is partnered with at the time — so a channel that
worked last year is not evidence about this transfer.

What the services publish for China currently runs along three rails:

- **Wallets** — Alipay and WeChat. The final destination is a
  real-name-verified wallet, or the settlement account attached to it.
- **UnionPay** — the card number identifies the Chinese bank account behind
  it. The money lands in that account.
- **A local bank account** — the ordinary route, and the one that is *not*
  universally available on this corridor.

Availability moves. At the time of writing one service lists Alipay as
temporarily suspended while continuing to pay out over UnionPay, which is
the normal state of affairs here rather than an outage worth waiting out.
Check the rail on the day you send.

## Verification happens on the recipient's side

On most corridors the sender is the one being checked. Here the binding
condition is that the **recipient's legal name matches the account being
paid**.

For a Chinese national that generally means the simplified-character name as
registered on the national ID and with the bank, a Chinese mobile number,
and the account details. For a foreign or Korean recipient it generally
means the romanised name as registered with the bank, and a UnionPay-linked
card or account.

Two consequences worth stating plainly:

**Sending to yourself is not restricted, but it is not exempt either.** Your
own account in China still has to clear the Chinese payout partner's or
bank's KYC on its own terms.

**Paying a third-party account usually fails.** Where the registered
recipient and the actual account holder differ, most small-amount channels
either refuse it outright or fail the payout. This is not a rule you can
work around by explaining the relationship in the memo field.

Under SAFE's framework the governing principle is that the transaction must
be genuine, so further evidence can be requested according to the amount and
the type of transfer: employment and income records for salary, the family
relationship and the basis for the payment for family support, distribution
records for investment income.

## China's USD 50,000 is not a receiving limit

This is the number that goes wrong most often, and it goes wrong in a
specific way.

The USD 50,000 applied to an individual in China is an **annual
facilitation quota for settling foreign currency into renminbi and for
buying foreign currency**. It is a quota on conversion, not a ceiling on
what may arrive from abroad. SAFE's own position is that a genuine, lawful
current-account transaction can be processed above the figure against
supporting documents, and that treating it as a person's maximum annual
foreign exchange activity is a misreading. Capital-account transactions are
governed separately again.

Then there is a third limit that nobody expects, because it is not law at
all: the **operating limits of the remittance service and its Chinese payout
partner**. A daily cap in renminbi, an annual cap, a maximum number of
receipts per month — these are set by the payout network, they differ by
provider, and they are the limits most likely to stop an ordinary transfer.
They are published on each provider's own pages and they change.

So three separate layers, and they are routinely collapsed into one:

| Layer | What it governs | Whose rule |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Korea | What you may send, and on what evidence | Korean FX regulations |
| China | Converting and buying foreign currency | SAFE |
| The service | How much its network will pay out | The provider and its partner |

Your transfer has to satisfy all three, and the one that blocks it is
usually the third.

## Names, account class, and the days either side of a holiday

**Do not romanise a Chinese name yourself.** Where the rail asks for a
Chinese name, use the simplified characters exactly as registered on the ID
and with the bank. Where it asks for a romanised beneficiary name, use the
form registered with the bank or shown in the passport. Even when pinyin is
required, matching the registered value matters more than getting the
surname order or the spacing to look right — a single character or an extra
space is enough to have the payout rejected.

**Account class is the question UnionPay hides.** China classifies personal
accounts, and a Class II account carries limits on money arriving from
unlinked accounts that a Class I account does not. Those limits can collide
with the payout partner's own conditions. For a large or a repeated
transfer, "my recipient has a UnionPay card" is not the fact that matters —
the class of the bank account behind the card, and whether it can receive
from abroad, is.

**Holidays are settlement, not opening hours.** A wallet transfer can show
as instant and still wait for a Chinese business day once a bank settlement
or a compliance review is in the path. Around Spring Festival and the
National Day week, leave one or two business days of slack; services that
state their timing in Chinese business days mean it.

## Comparing on what arrives

The figures below are **a worked structure, not a live quote**. They assume
a rate of ₩195.00 to the yuan, a ₩5,000 sending fee, and nothing deducted in
China.

| Sent | Rate | Fee | Total leaving Korea | Received |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ₩1,000,000 | ₩195.00 / CNY | ₩5,000 | ₩1,005,000 | CNY 5,128.21 |
| ₩5,000,000 | ₩195.00 / CNY | ₩5,000 | ₩5,005,000 | CNY 25,641.03 |

Work it the same way for a real comparison: start from the **total won
leaving your Korean account**, end at the **confirmed yuan the recipient
gets**, and divide. A headline fee tells you almost nothing next to the rate
that was applied.

And on this corridor cost is not the only axis. A cheaper quote on a rail
your recipient's account cannot actually receive is not cheaper. What
decides the real price of sending money from Korea to China is the final
amount *and* the certainty that it will be paid at all.
