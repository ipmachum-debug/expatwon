Korean severance is described almost everywhere as one month's salary for
each year worked. That sentence gets the order of magnitude right and
almost everything else wrong.

What decides the figure is your continuous service, your contractual weekly
hours, the average wage in your final three months — and, before any of
that, which retirement benefit scheme your workplace actually runs. On a
defined contribution plan, the last three months barely enter into it.

## Two gates, and neither is about attendance

An employer's obligation to provide a retirement benefit scheme does not
extend to an employee whose **continuous service is under one year**, or
whose **contractual working hours average under 15 a week over four weeks**.

Both have to be cleared —
[the minimum continuous service](/tracked/severance-minimum-continuous-service/)
and the same 15-hour line that governs
[weekly holiday pay](/employment/minimum-wage-and-weekly-holiday-pay-korea/).

The first gate is measured on the employment relationship, not on days
attended:

<div class="steps">
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">1</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Probation counts.</strong> The relationship existed. An employer excluding a three-month probation from the service calculation is applying a rule that does not exist.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">2</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Periods where the relationship survives without work count in principle</strong> — parental leave among them.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">3</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>One year is not 365 days of attendance.</strong> It is a year of the relationship existing.</div></div>
</div>

The second gate runs on **contractual** hours averaged over four weeks, so a
single quiet week does not push a 20-hour employee below it.

## The formula, and the part people get backwards

```
daily average wage × 30 days × (days of continuous service ÷ 365)
```

Three years of service at a daily average wage of ₩150,000:

```
₩150,000 × 30 × 3  =  ₩13,500,000
```

That is the whole statutory calculation. Everything difficult is inside
"daily average wage".

## The divisor is calendar days

Average wage is the **total wages paid in the three months before you leave,
divided by the total calendar days in that period** — not by the days you
actually worked.

```
three-month wage total   ₩15,000,000
calendar days in period          92
                          ──────────
daily average wage         ≈ ₩163,043
```

Divide the same ₩15,000,000 by the roughly 62 working days in a quarter and
you get about ₩242,000 — nearly 50% too high. This is the single most common
error in do-it-yourself severance arithmetic, and it runs in the direction
that makes people think they were underpaid when they were not.

## Bonuses and unused leave enter on their own terms

Not everything paid in the final three months belongs in the total, and not
everything in the total was paid in the final three months.

| Item | How it enters |
|---|---|
| Base pay, fixed allowances | In full, as paid in the three-month period |
| Overtime, night, holiday premiums, weekly holiday pay | As wages, on the same basis |
| Regular bonus | **Three twelfths** of what was paid across the prior twelve months |
| Unused annual leave allowance already claimable before leaving | Can enter on the same three-twelfths basis |
| Unused annual leave allowance arising **because** you left | Generally not in the average wage |

<div class="callout callout-note">
  <p class="callout-title">The leave-allowance split is where DB and DC diverge</p>
  <p>An unused-leave allowance that arises by reason of retirement is
  generally outside the average wage for ordinary severance — but it can fall
  inside the annual total wages that drive a DC contribution. Same payment,
  two schemes, two answers. If you are on DC, do not import the severance rule
  and assume it was correctly excluded.</p>
</div>

So the audit runs item by item, on when the entitlement arose and whether it
is a wage — base pay, then fixed allowances, then premium pay, then regular
bonus, then unused leave.

## The ordinary wage floor

If your final three months contained leave, reduced hours or an unpaid
period, the average wage can come out artificially low.

The Labor Standards Act answers this directly: where the computed average
wage is **lower than the ordinary wage**, ordinary wage is used as the
average wage. A Ministry of Government Legislation interpretation in May
2026 reconfirmed that this applies to the severance calculation.

```
compute the three-month average wage
  → compare with ordinary wage
  → if average < ordinary, use ordinary
```

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">This is why the online calculators disagree with your payslip</p>
  <p>A calculator that asks only for your last three months of pay cannot
  apply the floor, because it never asked for your ordinary wage. Anyone who
  left after a period of leave and got a number that felt too low has probably
  met exactly this gap.</p>
</div>

The [severance calculator](/tools/severance-calculator/) on this site takes
the ordinary daily wage as an input for that reason, and tells you when the
floor is doing the work.

## DB and DC decide different things

| | DB — defined benefit | DC — defined contribution |
|---|---|---|
| What is fixed in advance | The employee's **benefit** | The employer's **contribution** |
| Who manages the fund | The employer | The employee |
| Employer's basic duty | Secure the statutory benefit level | Pay at least **1/12 of annual wages** each year |
| Who carries investment risk | The employer | The employee |
| Effect of pay rises | Large — the final wage level drives it | Reflected year by year, as paid |
| Effect of returns | Limited effect on the employee's benefit | Direct effect on the final balance |

The employer's DC floor is
[one twelfth of annual total wages](/tracked/dc-employer-minimum-contribution/).

## "DB if your pay rises, DC if you invest well" is a comparison, not a choice

The generalisation is fair enough on its own terms. Someone expecting
sustained promotion and pay growth is helped by a structure that keys off
the final wage level; someone with flat pay and a long horizon of good
returns may do better carrying the investment result themselves.

But it describes an investment trade-off, not an option on your desk.

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">You usually do not pick</p>
  <p>The scheme is set by your workplace's retirement benefit rules, and
  establishing or changing one runs through an employee representative
  procedure — not an individual election. Before comparing DB and DC for
  yourself, find out what your employer has actually set up. That is a
  question for HR with a documentary answer.</p>
</div>

## On DC, audit contributions — not a final average wage

Applying the ordinary severance formula to a DC plan produces a number that
means nothing. The plan is built forward from yearly contributions, not
backward from a closing salary.

What to check instead:

<div class="steps">
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">1</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Total wages for each year</strong> of your service.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">2</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>The contribution that should have followed</strong> — at least a twelfth of each.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">3</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Whether it was actually paid</strong>, and on time. This is the failure that accumulates quietly.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">4</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Investment gains and losses</strong>, which are yours either way.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">5</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Any unpaid contributions</strong> still outstanding at the point you leave.</div></div>
</div>

A shortfall from three years ago is invisible in the closing balance. It
shows up only in the contribution record.

## Fourteen days, and where the money goes

Retirement benefits are payable within
[14 days](/tracked/severance-payment-deadline/) of the date the entitlement
arises. The period can be extended **by agreement between the parties** where
there are special circumstances — which is an agreement, not an announcement
from payroll.

Since 2022, ordinary severance is in principle transferred to an **IRP**
account the employee nominates, rather than paid to a regular bank account.

There are statutory exceptions, and one of them matters here: a foreign
national who worked in Korea on a status permitting employment and who
**actually departs** after leaving the job can fall outside the IRP transfer
requirement.

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">Being foreign is not itself the exception</p>
  <p>The exception turns on actual departure and the statutory conditions, not
  on nationality. An E-7 holder changing employers inside Korea is not covered
  by it. Confirm which route applies before assuming the money can land in an
  ordinary account — and note that fourteen days can outlast your flight, which
  is a reason to settle the destination account before you go rather than
  after. The wider exit sequence is in <a href="/business/leaving-korea-tax-pension-and-refunds/">leaving Korea: tax, pension and refunds</a>.</p>
</div>

## Departure guarantee insurance is a separate instrument

Not every foreign worker has one. It is an **Employment Permit System**
arrangement — E-9 and H-2 — and not a retirement pension product of the DB or
DC kind.

The employer accrues it at
[8.3% of monthly ordinary wage](/tracked/departure-guarantee-insurance-rate/),
and a payout event can arise where the worker has a year or more of service
and departs, or on certain changes of status.

Because it accrues on **ordinary wage** while statutory severance is computed
on **average wage**, the two figures do not match:

```
statutory severance        ₩10,000,000
departure insurance payout  ₩8,700,000
                            ──────────
employer still owes          ₩1,300,000
```

That difference is the employer's to pay. "We took out the insurance, so the
severance is settled" is not a defence, and this is the most common way an
EPS worker is underpaid on the way out.

The claim mechanics — when to file, the airport route, and what to do if you
have already flown — are in
**departure guarantee insurance for EPS workers**.

## Being foreign does not change the arithmetic

Whether you hold E-7, F-5 or a Korean passport, the calculation is the same.
What a foreign worker gets is not a lower standard but an additional payment
route to reconcile:

```
continuous service
  → four-week average of 15 hours
  → three-month average wage
  → compare with ordinary wage, take the higher
  → statutory entitlement
  → which scheme: ordinary severance, DB or DC
  → EPS departure guarantee insurance, if applicable
  → settle the shortfall
```

## What to have in front of you before you leave

- contract start date, and the **legal** leaving date
- contractual weekly working hours
- the last three months of payslips
- bonuses paid across the last twelve months
- unused annual leave allowance, and when the entitlement arose
- which retirement scheme, in writing
- for DC, the contribution record year by year
- for EPS workers, the departure insurance accrual

Three failure modes, one per scheme. On ordinary severance and DB it is a
mis-stated average wage. On DC it is missing employer contributions. On E-9
and H-2 it is the unpaid gap between the insurance payout and the statutory
figure.

Severance in Korea is not "a year worked, a month paid". It is: clear both
gates, compute the average wage properly and test it against ordinary wage,
then follow the scheme your workplace actually runs to the number it
produces — and, if there is an insurance policy in the picture, check what it
left behind.
