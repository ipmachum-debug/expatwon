Owning a car in Korea is not the purchase price plus insurance. Two other
obligations attach and never detach: taxes at acquisition, and taxes plus
a statutory inspection for as long as the vehicle stays in your name.

None of this varies by nationality. Register a car in your own name on an
alien registration card and you are inside the same framework as any
Korean owner — the same rates, the same deadlines, the same penalties.

Which means the number that matters before buying is not the sticker
price and not the monthly instalment. It is the cash required to get the
car registered, plus the annual cost of keeping it.

## Acquisition tax: the first number people forget

The tax due at acquisition is set by the Local Tax Act and depends on the
vehicle class, not on who is buying.

| Vehicle class | Acquisition tax rate |
|---|---|
| Passenger car, non-commercial | **7%** |
| Light vehicle (경차) | 4% |
| Other non-commercial (vans, trucks) | 5% |
| Commercial-use vehicle | 4% |

On a taxable value of ₩40,000,000, the passenger-car rate produces
₩2,800,000. At ₩50,000,000 it is ₩3,500,000. That is cash, due at
registration, and it is not financed by the vehicle loan.

The light-vehicle line deserves its own note, because 4% overstates what
most light-car buyers actually pay. Under the Restriction of Special
Local Taxation Act, acquisition tax on a light vehicle is **waived up to
₩750,000**, with only the excess payable — which means a light car with a
taxable value at or below roughly ₩18.75M pays nothing at all. The relief
currently runs to the end of 2027, and a reduction of the ₩750,000 cap
has been under discussion, so confirm the cap in force on your
registration date rather than assuming this year's figure.

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">"4% of the price" is not the answer for a light car</p>
  <p>Nor is "7% of the price" the whole answer for anything else. Rates are
  the starting point; the exemptions, the taxable base and the local bond
  obligation all move the final cash figure. Get the number from the
  registration office for your specific vehicle before you commit.</p>
</div>

## Used cars: the contract price is a floor, not a lever

A recurring idea in private used-car sales is that writing a lower price
on the contract lowers the tax. It does not.

For a transfer registration, the taxable base is the acquisition value —
but where no price is declared, or the declared price is **below the
published standard market value** (시가표준액) for that model and age, the
standard value applies instead. The standard value is built from the
original list price adjusted by a residual-value ratio for elapsed years,
and it functions as a floor.

The practical consequence falls hardest on the case people most want to
game: an expensive imported used car transferred well below market. The
contract says one number, the registration office assesses another, and
the gap appears as cash you did not budget on the day you register.

If you are financing the purchase, note that acquisition tax sits
*outside* the loan. Down payment, acquisition tax, registration costs and
the first insurance premium can all fall due in the same week — worth
modelling alongside
[how instalment financing is actually screened](/cars/car-installment-financing-halbu-korea/).

## Registration bonds are a cost, not a tax

Registering a vehicle can also trigger a mandatory purchase of local
development bonds (지역개발채권) or, in Seoul, Busan and Daegu, urban
railway bonds (도시철도채권). This is not tax revenue — it is a compulsory
loan to the local government, and it is governed by each local
authority's own ordinance. The purchase ratio varies by vehicle class,
engine size, registration region and vehicle price.

Two things about it are worth knowing before you register.

**First, sub-1,600cc passenger cars are generally exempt.** Since March
2023, local governments have waived the mandatory bond purchase on new
and transfer registrations of small non-commercial passenger cars
(1,000cc up to 1,600cc). In Seoul that removed a bond obligation of about
9% of the vehicle price. The exemption is set by ordinance and carries an
expiry date that has been extended before, so treat it as a question for
the registration office on the day — not a permanent feature.

**Second, if you do have to buy, the real cost is the discount loss.**
Most owners do not hold the bond to maturity (five years for local
development bonds, around seven for urban railway bonds). They buy it at
registration and sell it back immediately through the bank at the
prevailing discount rate. What leaves your pocket is not the face value —
it is the difference between face value and the immediate resale price.

## The annual tax runs on engine size, not price

For an ordinary non-commercial passenger car, the annual automobile tax
ignores what you paid. It is engine displacement multiplied by a
per-cc rate.

| Displacement | Rate per cc |
|---|---|
| 1,000cc and under | ₩80 |
| Over 1,000cc to 1,600cc | ₩140 |
| Over 1,600cc | ₩200 |

Then a **local education surtax of 30% of the automobile tax** is added.
This is the step most calculators-by-hand miss, and it is why the number
on the bill is always higher than displacement × rate.

The structure cuts both ways. An expensive small-displacement car can
carry a modest annual tax, while a large-engine used car whose market
value has collapsed still attracts a substantial one. If you are choosing
between a cheap 3,000cc car and a costlier 1,600cc one, the annual tax
gap is close to half a million won a year before anything else.

## Older cars pay less — by formula

Non-commercial passenger cars get an age-based reduction. From the third
year the tax falls **5% per year, capped at 50%**, which is reached at
year twelve. The Local Tax Act sets it out as a formula rather than a
table, with the vehicle age bounded between 2 and 12.

## Prepayment: worth doing, not worth the headline

Automobile tax is normally billed in two instalments. You can instead pay
the whole year up front and take a deduction — the prepayment scheme
(연납).

The often-repeated line that "paying in January gives you 5% off" is not
accurate. The 5% is a statutory ceiling, and the deduction is computed
only on the portion of the year following the payment deadline. The
published effective rates for 2026:

| Application window | Effective deduction |
|---|---|
| 14 Jan – 4 Feb | about **4.6%** of the annual tax |
| 16–31 Mar | about 3.8% |
| 16–30 Jun | about 2.5% |
| 16–30 Sep | about 1.3% of the second-half amount |

On a ₩520,000 annual bill, January prepayment saves roughly ₩24,000 — a
real saving, and about the size it actually is.

<div class="callout callout-note">
  <p class="callout-title">Prepay only if you are keeping the car all year</p>
  <p>If you sell, export or deregister the vehicle mid-year after
  prepaying, the tax for the months you did not own it is settled back —
  but it is one more thread to unwind at exactly the moment you are
  leaving. If your departure is already scheduled, read
  <a href="/cars/selling-or-exporting-your-car-when-leaving-korea/">the exit
  procedure</a> before you prepay.</p>
</div>

## Inspection is the obligation people actually miss

Paying the tax does not discharge the ownership duty. Under the Motor
Vehicle Management Act every registered vehicle must pass a periodic
inspection — or, in designated air-quality control regions, a
comprehensive inspection that adds emissions testing — within its
validity window.

For an ordinary non-commercial passenger car:

| | Validity period |
|---|---|
| New vehicle, first inspection | **5 years** |
| Every inspection thereafter | 2 years |

Two things about that first line are worth flagging. It was **4 years
until the end of 2024** and was extended to 5 by an amendment effective
in 2025, so English-language guides written earlier are wrong. And it
applies to non-commercial passenger cars only — commercial passenger
cars, vans, trucks and special vehicles run on their own cycles of six
months, one year or two years depending on class, commercial use and
vehicle age.

The submission window is wider than it used to be: you may present the
vehicle from **90 days before** the expiry date to **31 days after** — a
122-day window, expanded from 63 days. For anyone who travels for work,
that is the single most useful number in this article: an inspection due
while you are abroad can usually be done three months early.

<p class="hand">If you travel for work, do the inspection three months early. Nobody tells you that you can.</p>

When buying used, read the current inspection expiry off the
registration certificate — not just the first registration date. It is
part of the same document chain covered in
[buying a used car as a foreigner](/cars/buying-a-used-car-in-korea-as-a-foreigner/).

The law does allow extension or deferral, but only on defined grounds:
theft, repair following an accident, seized plates, an operating
suspension. These are applied for in advance. "I was in my home country"
is not among them, and the clock does not pause while you are away.

## The failure mode specific to foreign owners is administrative

The rates are the same for everyone. What is not the same is the
likelihood that official mail reaches you.

When you change your residence, the Immigration Act requires a change-of-
residence report **within 15 days** of moving in, at the local office or
the immigration office with jurisdiction. Do not assume that updating
your immigration record automatically propagates everywhere else. Four
records need to agree:

The point that costs money: **not receiving a notice does not remove the
obligation.** An unpaid automobile tax bill and a missed inspection both
accrue against the vehicle regardless of whether the paper found you, and
both surface later — at renewal, at transfer, or on the day you try to
deregister the car before leaving.

Keeping the tax and utility payments on a working Korean account matters
here too; if yours is still restricted, see
[limited accounts and how they are released](/banking/korean-limited-accounts-hando-gyejwa-explained/).

## Own the whole cost, not the monthly payment

The most common budgeting error is judging affordability by the
instalment. The real structure has three stages, and only the middle one
is monthly.

**Acquisition** — down payment, acquisition tax, bond discount loss,
registration fees, first insurance premium.

**Holding** — loan principal and interest, automobile tax plus education
surtax, insurance renewals, inspection fees, maintenance, parking, fuel
or charging.

**Disposal** — settling the remaining loan, releasing the lien,
sale or export costs, and the final tax and insurance refunds.

For a long-stay foreign resident on E-7, F-2, F-4, F-5 or F-6, one
comparison sits above all of these: **the financing term against the
remaining stay.** A loan that outlasts your visa turns into a forced sale
under time pressure, where the gap between the outstanding balance and
the used-car bid is paid in cash on your way out of the country. Where
the holding period is genuinely short, compare against
[a long-term rental](/cars/long-term-car-rental-janggi-rent-korea/) or
[a lease](/cars/car-lease-in-korea-for-foreigners-real-cost/) on total
cost rather than assuming ownership is cheaper.

Korea does not run a separate motor-tax regime for foreigners. Registering
a car in your name simply moves you inside the domestic one — acquisition
tax and registration costs on the way in, automobile tax and inspection
throughout, transfer or deregistration and a final settlement on the way
out. The number to check before buying is not the price and not the
instalment. It is the total of all three stages.
