The first decision in starting a business in Korea is not the trade name
or the industry code. It is the legal form: sole proprietorship or
corporation.

<p class="hand">The day you stop treating the company account as yours is the day the structure starts working.</p>


That choice determines how far liability reaches into your personal
assets, how profit is taxed, how you are allowed to take money out, how
banks assess you for credit, how an investor could ever buy in — and, for
a foreign national, whether your residence status permits the arrangement
at all.

Start with the claim that circulates most: that foreigners must
incorporate. They do not. The National Tax Service registers foreign
nationals as sole proprietors on an alien registration card or a passport
copy, the same as anyone else.

But a business registration certificate and permission to run that
business are two different pieces of paper, issued by two different
authorities. The tax office registers you for taxation. Immigration
governs what your residence status lets you do. Neither validates the
other, and the tax office will not stop you from registering something
your visa does not allow.

So foreign entrepreneurship in Korea is always two questions at once: the
structure of the business, and the status of the person running it.

## Liability: who is the legal person

A sole proprietorship is not a separate legal person from you. Where
business debts exceed business assets, the shortfall can reach your
personal property. If the business owes a supplier ₩200,000,000 and holds
₩50,000,000 in assets, the remaining ₩150,000,000 is a claim that can
follow you personally.

A company is a separate legal person. Company assets and the
representative director's personal assets are in principle distinct, and
shareholders are liable to the extent of what they contributed.

That principle has more holes than most first-time founders expect.
Limited liability is pierced routinely by ordinary commercial practice:

<div class="check-card">
  <div class="check-row miss"><span class="mark"></span><span><strong>Personal guarantee.</strong> Korean lenders and landlords frequently require the representative director to guarantee the company's obligations — which converts the debt back into a personal one.</span></div>
  <div class="check-row miss"><span class="mark"></span><span><strong>Taxes and wages.</strong> Unpaid tax and unpaid employee wages carry their own liability rules that reach the responsible officer.</span></div>
  <div class="check-row miss"><span class="mark"></span><span><strong>Embezzlement and breach of trust.</strong> Misuse of company money is a personal criminal exposure, not a corporate one.</span></div>
  <div class="check-result">Limited liability separates ordinary contractual debt from shareholders. It does not mean the company can fail without consequence to you.</div>
</div>

## Sole proprietor: business income is your income

The great advantage of the sole proprietorship is that the money question
does not arise. Revenue minus deductible expenses is business income, and
business income is part of *your* global income. Drawing money from the
business account for living costs is not a second taxable event, because
the business is not a separate person holding it.

The cost of that simplicity is the rate curve. Global income tax runs
from 6% to 45% on a progressive schedule:

| Tax base | Rate |
|---|---|
| Up to ₩14,000,000 | 6% |
| ₩14M – ₩50M | 15% |
| ₩50M – ₩88M | 24% |
| ₩88M – ₩150M | 35% |
| ₩150M – ₩300M | 38% |
| ₩300M – ₩500M | 40% |
| ₩500M – ₩1,000M | 42% |
| Over ₩1,000M | 45% |

A local income tax of 10% of the computed tax sits on top of every line.
As the business grows, its whole profit stacks onto your other income and
climbs this curve.

## Corporation: the company is taxed first, and then you are

A company pays corporate tax on its own income, separately from you.

| Tax base | Rate (FY2026) |
|---|---|
| Up to ₩200,000,000 | **10%** |
| ₩200M – ₩20bn | 20% |
| ₩20bn – ₩300bn | 22% |
| Over ₩300bn | 25% |

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">These rates went up for 2026</p>
  <p>Every bracket rose by one percentage point — 9→10, 19→20, 21→22,
  24→25 — for fiscal years beginning on or after 1 January 2026. English
  guides written before the amendment still show 9%. A local income tax of
  10% of the corporate tax also applies, so the effective bottom-bracket
  burden is about 11%, not 10%.</p>
</div>

Compare the headline rates alone and the corporation looks decisively
cheaper. It is — at the first layer.

## The half of the calculation nobody puts in the chart

A company's after-tax profit belongs to the company. To use it
personally, it has to leave through a route the law recognises — and each
route is taxed again.

<div class="check-card">
  <div class="check-row ok"><span class="mark"></span><span><strong>Salary or director's remuneration.</strong> Deductible for the company; taxed to you as employment income on the progressive schedule.</span></div>
  <div class="check-row ok"><span class="mark"></span><span><strong>Dividend.</strong> Paid from distributable profit under the Commercial Act. Withheld at 14% plus local surtax, and pulled into global taxation once your financial income passes the threshold.</span></div>
  <div class="check-row ok"><span class="mark"></span><span><strong>Retirement pay and reimbursed business expenses.</strong> Recognised routes with their own rules and documentation.</span></div>
  <div class="check-row miss"><span class="mark"></span><span><strong>Just transferring it to yourself.</strong> Not a route. It becomes a director loan on the books.</span></div>
</div>

That last line is where new corporate owners lose money. An informal
withdrawal is recorded as a loan from the company to you. The company is
then deemed to earn interest on it — **4.6%** where the statutory
overdraft rate applies — and if you never actually pay that interest, the
amount is treated as a bonus to you and taxed as your income. Depending
on what the money was used for, it can also become a criminal question.

So the naive arithmetic — "10% corporate plus 14% dividend equals 24%,
still better than 38%" — is wrong in both directions. Dividend income is
not necessarily taxed at the withholding rate once it enters global
taxation, and how much you pay depends on the mix of salary and dividend
you choose, not on a fixed formula.

<div class="callout callout-note">
  <p class="callout-title">The question is not how much you earn. It is how much you take out.</p>
  <p>If nearly all the profit funds your household, the corporation's rate
  advantage is consumed at the second layer. If a substantial share stays
  in the company to hire staff, buy inventory, fund a lease deposit or
  build equipment, the lower entity rate compounds on money you would
  otherwise have paid out in tax first.</p>
</div>

Even the salary route has form requirements. Director remuneration is
deductible when it follows the articles of incorporation and the
shareholder or board resolutions that authorise it; set arbitrarily high
or paid without those steps, deductibility becomes contestable. A
dividend likewise requires distributable profit and the Commercial Act
procedure — sending cash from the company account to a shareholder is not
a dividend just because you call it one.

Whatever you choose, apply one rule from day one: **the company's bank
account and your bank account are different accounts, and money moves
between them only with a paper trail.**

## The visa layer

For a foreign national, the tax comparison is downstream of residence
status. Three routes matter.

| Route | Investment | Structure |
|---|---|---|
| **D-8-1** | ₩100M+ | Korean company; 10%+ of voting shares; foreign-invested company registration |
| **D-8-3** | ₩100M+ | Sole proprietorship jointly with a Korean national, both listed as co-representatives; the Korean partner's own business capital must also meet a threshold |
| **D-9-4** | ₩300M+ | Sole proprietorship run independently by the foreign national |

Two corrections to the version of this that circulates in English.

**D-8 is not corporations-only.** D-8-3 covers investment into a sole
proprietorship — but jointly with a Korean national, with both registered
as co-representatives. A foreign national putting ₩100M into their own
sole proprietorship does not qualify.

**Independent sole proprietorship has its own route, at a higher bar.**
D-9-4 is the path for a foreign national running an individual business
alone, and the investment threshold is ₩300M rather than ₩100M. The
₩200M gap between D-8-3 and D-9-4 is the price of not having a Korean
co-representative.

Neither is issued because money arrived. Immigration examines the
remittance path of the investment funds, the existence of a real place of
business, what the capital was actually spent on, the business plan, and
subsequently revenue, employment and tax records. The capital-continuity
requirement is covered in detail in
[the D-8 investor visa guide](/business/d-8-investor-visa-korea-requirements/).

### If you already hold an F-series status

F-2, F-4, F-5 and F-6 are not granted on the basis of investment. If you
already hold one, you can generally choose either structure within the
activities your status permits — and building a ₩100M investment
structure purely to obtain a D-8 you do not need is an expensive
non-solution.

"F means unrestricted" is still too strong. F-4 (overseas Korean) is
subject to restrictions on categories such as simple manual labour, so a
registration certificate does not by itself settle whether your actual
day-to-day activity is permitted. F-2 varies by sub-type. And for
F-5 or F-6, licensed industries — food service, accommodation, medical,
education, construction, labour supply — impose their own approval
requirements on everyone regardless of nationality.

## Registration is tax administration, not permission

When you register, the tax office wants the application, the lease for
the place of business, any industry licence, and for a foreign national
an alien registration card or passport copy. Registration is due
**within 20 days of commencing business** under the Value-Added Tax Act,
and you may register before you start. Missing the deadline carries a
penalty calculated on supply value.

## What banks actually see

A sole proprietor's credit and the business's credit are largely the same
thing. Early on, with little trading history, lending and card decisions
turn on your domestic credit score, declared income, existing debt and
remaining period of stay — the same variables described in
[whether foreigners can borrow in Korea](/loans/can-foreigners-get-a-loan-in-korea/).

A company is a separate entity, but a newly formed one has no credit of
its own. Banks initially assess the representative and major
shareholders, the paid-in capital, revenue, transaction history, tax
records and collateral. "Incorporate and the company borrows on its own
name" is not true in year one.

It becomes true later. Once financial statements, revenue and a tax
record accumulate, a company builds standing on its own balance sheet —
and that is also what makes outside investment, share transfers, employee
stock options and an eventual sale possible. Those are structural
capabilities a sole proprietorship does not have at any profit level.

## Converting later is possible, and not free

You can start as a sole proprietor and incorporate later. The crude
method — close the sole proprietorship, form a company, start again — is
fine while the business is mostly you and a laptop.

It stops being fine once real assets have accumulated in your personal
name: property, machinery, vehicles, inventory, goodwill, contracts.
Then a comprehensive transfer of the business's assets and liabilities to
the company is the structure to examine, with the sequence running
feasibility review → valuation → incorporation → transfer agreement →
moving assets, contracts and licences → registration → closing the sole
proprietorship → final tax settlement.

Where real estate is involved, or where you intend to claim deferral or
reduction reliefs, this is not a form-filling exercise. Value-added tax,
capital gains tax, acquisition tax and the conditions attached to each
relief all have to be reviewed together, at the point of conversion.

## The decision, in order

Low profit, most of it spent on living, no need for outside investment or
a share structure: the sole proprietorship is simpler and the simplicity
has real value.

Sustained profit with a meaningful share retained and reinvested, growing
headcount, outside investment in view, or an eventual sale: the
corporation earns its overhead.

For a foreign national there is one more criterion, and it comes first.
Your residence status may not permit the structure the tax maths prefers
— and if you already hold a stable F-series status, the tax maths is the
only question you need to answer.

So the sequence is not "sole proprietor or company." It is: confirm the
status you have and the status you will need → estimate the profit and
how much of it you must actually withdraw → weigh liability, financing
and investment plans → and only then choose the form.
