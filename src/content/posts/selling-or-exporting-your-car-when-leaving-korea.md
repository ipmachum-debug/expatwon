For a long-staying foreigner, the car is usually the last major asset in
Korea — and the one people most often mishandle on the way out. The
reason is a distinction that is easy to miss: **physically handing over
the vehicle and legally disposing of it are two different acts.**

Giving a dealer the keys does not end your position. Your legal
relationship to the car ends when the **vehicle register** shows either
a transfer of ownership to someone else, or deregistration.

That gap matters more for foreigners than for locals. Departure sets off
a chain — ARC surrendered, residence status ended, Korean phone line
cancelled, banking access narrowed — that makes any unfinished paperwork
dramatically harder to fix from abroad. So car disposal is not a
departure-day errand.

The workable rhythm: start checking the register, liens, seizures,
unpaid fines and loan balances about **30 days before departure**, and
lock in your disposal route — domestic sale or export — one to two weeks
out.

## Handing over the car is not transferring ownership

A domestic used-car sale runs on **transfer registration** under the
Motor Vehicle Management Act. You can hand over the vehicle and take the
money, but if the register still names you, the administrative
relationship has not closed.

While that is true, a car driven by someone else can still route vehicle
tax, compulsory-insurance obligations, fines, official notices and
accident disputes back to you.

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">⚠️ Never leave the transfer "for later"</p>
  <p>In a private sale, handing over the car and keys on the promise that
  the buyer "will register it soon" is the single riskiest move in this
  whole process. Minimise the window between handover and transfer
  registration. This is not administrative tidiness — a car still in your
  name is how vehicles end up circulating illegally under a departed
  owner's identity.</p>
</div>

Even when you sell to a dealer, verify the completed transfer yourself
through the vehicle register. Do not treat the dealer's assurance as the
record.

## Domestic sale and export are different machines

| | Domestic used-car sale | Export |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Speed | Fast | Depends on shipping schedule |
| Admin difficulty | Low | Higher |
| Cash timing | Quick | Varies by contract |
| Core steps | Sale contract → transfer registration | Export contract → export deregistration → shipping → export-performance report |
| Plates | Generally go with the car | Surrendered at deregistration |
| Price driver | Domestic used-car market | Overseas demand, FX, model, shipping cost |
| Best when | Departure is near, or domestic prices are strong | A model commands a genuinely higher overseas price |

The domestic route's real advantage is **certainty**. Selling to a
registered used-car dealer prices the car at domestic wholesale levels,
but the administration is simpler and settlement is faster. With a fixed
flight date, closing cleanly can be worth more than squeezing the last
won out of the price.

A private sale may beat the dealer's offer, but you personally manage
buyer sourcing, inspection, payment and transfer registration. Days
before departure, that trade rarely favours you.

Export needs its own arithmetic. Korean used cars in demand abroad can
fetch more than domestic buyers pay — but inland transport, shipping,
the exporter's margin and FX all come out of that number. So compare
**net proceeds after every cost**, not the export price against the
dealer's price.

## Export is not "send the car to a port"

Taking a car out of the country requires **deregistration for export
purposes**. Once that completes, the vehicle is no longer a normally
registered domestic vehicle: the plates are surrendered and you receive
a deregistration certificate, which then feeds customs clearance and
shipping.

And deregistration is not the end of your obligations:

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">⚠️ The export-performance report</p>
  <p>Whoever applies for export deregistration must subsequently
  <strong>report to the authorities whether the export actually
  happened</strong>, within the period set by the decree. Failing to
  report carries a fine of <strong>up to ₩1,000,000</strong> under the
  Motor Vehicle Management Act. If the car is not exported, follow-up
  steps — re-registration or scrapping — are required.</p>
  <p>Do not rely on the "90 days" figure that circulates online: the
  period is set by decree and has been amended. Confirm the current
  deadline with the vehicle registration office when you file.</p>
</div>

Which produces one concrete instruction for the contract. If you sell to
a specialist exporter, do not stop at "purchased for export." Have the
contract state **who files the export deregistration and who carries the
export-performance reporting duty** — because that duty attaches to the
filer, and you will be on a plane.

## Check the register before you price the car

The first task at D-30 is pulling the **vehicle register** — not for
ownership, which you know, but for the rights that can block disposal:
mortgages (liens) and seizures.

Financing a car does not always mean a lien was registered — but where a
finance company did register one, **paying off the balance does not
automatically remove the registration**. Debt repayment and lien
cancellation are separate events; confirm both.

Seizures are the other blocker: unpaid vehicle tax, local tax arrears,
parking or traffic fines can all sit on the register and stall a sale.

Work the sequence in this order:

> loan balance → pay off the finance company → confirm lien cancelled →
> check the register for seizures → settle unpaid taxes and fines →
> confirm seizures released → then sell or deregister

This is where the financing structure you accepted at purchase comes
back — the reason
[how car installment financing is secured](/cars/car-installment-financing-halbu-korea/)
matters at both ends of ownership. You must close the finance
relationship before the asset is freely disposable.

## Documents depend on how you sell

The base document for a sale-based transfer is the **vehicle transfer
certificate (자동차양도증명서)**.

Where the seller does not appear at the registration office in person to
confirm the transfer, a **seal certificate issued for vehicle sale
(자동차 매도용 인감증명서)** or a signature-verification certificate is
typically required. If you use the signature-verification route, it must
be issued *for vehicle sale* — not as generic ID — with the buyer's
details entered correctly.

For a foreign owner, assemble in advance:

- ARC (or residence report card) and passport
- Vehicle registration certificate
- Vehicle transfer certificate
- Seal certificate for vehicle sale, or signature-verification certificate
- Power of attorney, if someone acts for you
- Vehicle register plus proof that liens and seizures are released

Not every transaction needs all of it. Where seller and buyer attend the
registration office together, or where a registered used-car dealer
handles the deal, the document set differs. Confirm the final list with
the competent vehicle registration office **once the deal structure is
fixed** — not before.

## The 30-day countdown

    Pull the vehicle register; check loan or auto-loan balance, liens,

  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">2</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>D-20 to D-14 — clear the encumbrances.</strong>
    Pay off the balance, cancel the lien, settle vehicle tax and fines,
    confirm seizures released, and choose your route. There is a lag
    between a lender's payoff certificate and the register showing the
    lien gone — which is exactly why this is not a departure-week
    task.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">3</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>D-7 to D-3 — fix the counterparty.</strong>
    Buyer or exporter confirmed, price final, transfer certificate
    prepared, documents in hand, registration or deregistration date
    booked. From here, plan your remaining driving around that
    date.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">4</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>D-2 to D-1 — close it.</strong> Hand
    over the car, confirm payment, and <strong>confirm the transfer
    registration or export deregistration is complete</strong> on the
    register or certificate. File for vehicle-tax and insurance
    settlement.</div></div>
</div>

Do not leave transfer registration for departure day. Close the legal
position the day before and cover your last day with a taxi, the airport
railway or a rental.

## Vehicle tax is money you can get back

If you prepaid the annual vehicle tax, the portion covering the period
after ownership transfer or deregistration can be settled. Pay in
January, sell in August, and the remainder of the year is refundable in
principle — calculated pro rata from the transfer or deregistration
date. Check the refund and the payout account with the local government
or the local-tax system.

Which creates a sequencing trap specific to foreigners:

<div class="callout callout-tip">
  <p class="callout-title">💡 Close the bank account last</p>
  <p>Sell the car, then close the account — never the reverse. Vehicle-tax
  and insurance refunds land <em>after</em> the paperwork clears, and
  chasing them once your ARC is surrendered and your Korean number is
  disconnected is painful. Keep the account open until refunds arrive,
  then move the money out — see
  <a href="/banking/how-to-send-money-from-korea-abroad/">what actually
  arrives when you remit</a>.</p>
</div>

## Cancel insurance after the register moves, not before

Cancelling the policy the moment you hand over the car is the wrong
order. While the vehicle is still registered and requires compulsory
insurance, an early cancellation opens a coverage gap.

For a domestic sale: confirm the transfer registration completed, then
ask the insurer to cancel on the basis of the transfer. For an export:
cancel on proof of export deregistration. Unused premium may be refunded
under your insurer's terms — and note that **insurance refunds and
vehicle-tax refunds are entirely separate processes at separate
institutions.** Selling the car does not trigger one consolidated
settlement. The experience-rating consequences are covered in
[the car insurance guide](/insurance/korean-car-insurance-for-expats-explained/).

## The car is the first domino, not the last

Leaving Korea is a sequence, and the car sits at the front of it:

> dispose of the vehicle → settle vehicle tax → settle insurance →
> close out remaining finance contracts → complete immigration and
> residence-status formalities → collect Korean deposits and refunds →
> remit funds abroad → close Korean bank accounts

Ownership began with
[financing and registration decisions](/cars/buying-a-used-car-in-korea-as-a-foreigner/)
and ran through
[the licence you exchanged](/cars/getting-a-korean-drivers-license-license-exchange/);
it ends here.

And when you compare offers, do not compare only price. If a domestic
sale nets slightly less but guarantees the transfer and the payment
complete before you fly, that difference is the price of removing
administrative risk — usually worth paying. If your model has genuine
overseas demand and you have time, take both quotes and compare net
proceeds properly.

Either way the closing test is the same. Not *did I hand over the car* —
but: **is my name properly off the vehicle register, are the tax and
insurance settlements finished, and do I still have a working Korean
bank account to receive what is owed to me?**
