Your child has been accepted by an 어린이집. Then the centre tells you what to
pay, which is confusing, because Korea is described everywhere as providing
free childcare from zero to five.

Both things are true at once. A foreign child can be perfectly entitled to
attend while the ordinary national subsidy does not attach to that child — and
then, depending on your address, a completely separate municipal programme may
pay anyway.

Three questions, and the second cannot be answered from the first.

## The national rule starts with the child

The 2026 childcare guidance sets the beneficiary rule for childcare fees and
the home-care allowance around children aged zero to five with Korean
nationality and a properly issued resident-registration number, with specified
exceptions including recognised refugees and Afghan special contributors.

That produces the two cases foreign parents most often invert.

Two foreign parents may have a foreign-national child, and legal residence,
employment and tax payments do not convert that child into a national
beneficiary. A child holding Korean nationality is assessed as such even where
one or both parents are foreign.

The test is not whether the parents are Korean. It is the status of the child
the benefit is for.

## Admission and subsidy are different decisions

An 어린이집 is a provider; the childcare-fee programme is a government benefit.
A centre may accept a foreign-national child and deliver childcare while the
family remains responsible for some or all of the charge.

So *do you take foreigners* is only half the question. The other half is
*which subsidy, if any, gets applied to my bill* — and the answer may come
from the national programme, from a city or provincial programme, or from
nowhere.

There is a further ambiguity worth forcing into the open. Korean childcare
financing includes support paid at the institution level, and the 2026
guidance refers to foreign children in that institutional context. Support
reaching the centre is not the same as the parent entitlement reaching you.
Ask the centre to separate four things: the parent childcare subsidy, any
institutional support paid to the centre, any municipal foreign-child subsidy,
and what you still owe.

## Your address can decide the bill

A foreign-national child outside the national rule may still be supported by
the city. Seoul and Incheon both do it, and they do it differently enough that
treating one as the national picture produces a wrong budget.

**Seoul** runs a 2026 programme for foreign children aged zero to five
attending daycare in Seoul, paying a share of the applicable fee ceiling:
{{fig:seoul-foreign-child-childcare-share}} for ages three to five, and 50%
for ages zero to two and for qualifying disabled-child childcare. Foreign
registration is required and undocumented residents are not covered. Payment
runs through a 국민행복카드.

Because Seoul pays a *share of a ceiling*, the money moves with the type of
centre — which is why its published figures for private and home daycare at
ages three and above sit well over the national ₩280,000 and must not be read
as the same programme paying more.

**Incheon** is narrower. Its published scheme covers foreign children aged
three to five at a centre in the city, at
{{fig:incheon-foreign-child-childcare-subsidy}} a month, applied through the
childcare centre. The child must have registered stay and more than 90 days of
residence in the jurisdiction; undocumented or unregistered children are
excluded.

That is why "foreign children get ₩280,000 for daycare in Korea" is wrong in
two directions at once. It is Incheon's figure for one age band, it coincides
with the national ages 3–5 rate by accident, and neither is a universal
entitlement.

## The same family, two cities, two answers

Take two foreign-national siblings, lawfully registered, aged two and four.

In Seoul, the current foreign-child programme reaches both age groups, though
at different shares and amounts. In Incheon, the published programme reaches
the four-year-old and not the two-year-old, whose age falls outside it. The
older child's subsidy cannot be borrowed for the younger one.

Same parents, same nationality, same household, different age — different
result. And if that family moves from Seoul to Incheon, nothing about the
children changes while both entitlements do.

The 90-day condition deserves its own line for anyone who has just arrived or
just moved: do not budget as though the money starts with the first invoice,
and ask the local office how it counts the period.

## Ask the centre for four numbers

Not the headline subsidy. These:

1. The monthly childcare charge that applies.
2. The national parent subsidy applied to this child, if any.
3. The local foreign-child subsidy applied to this child, if any.
4. What is billed to you after both.

Then ask what sits outside the basic amount — meals, optional programmes,
materials — because the useful figure for a household is the one that leaves
your account, not the one in a rate table.

## Before you commit to a centre

Establish the child's nationality, and do not derive it from yours. Establish
whether the child has a Korean resident-registration number or a
foreign-registration record, which are not interchangeable across benefits.
Test the national rule, then its exceptions. Then ask whether the city or
province where the child actually lives and attends runs a foreign-child
programme, whether it has a residence-period condition, whether age changes
either eligibility or amount, whether the type of centre changes the amount,
how the subsidy is actually paid — and finally what the parent payment comes
to.

That order is slower than asking a neighbour what they receive. It is also the
only sequence that survives a move, a birthday or a change of centre.
