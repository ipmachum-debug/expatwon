**The visa matters. It does not decide the loan.**

Two applicants walk into the same Korean bank. One holds an F-5 permanent-resident card but has no taxable income in Korea. The other holds an E-7 employment status, has received the same salary every month for four years, and has a clean domestic credit record. Which one can borrow more?

There is no sound answer from the visa codes alone. The F-5 applicant has stronger residence continuity; the E-7 applicant may have a much stronger repayment file. A bank has to evaluate both, and the actual product may impose its own residence-status rules before either file reaches credit underwriting.

This is where online tables go wrong. An E-7 is not automatically a small loan, an F-6 is not automatically a family mortgage, and an F-5 is not an entitlement to the terms offered to Korean citizens. The practical question is what each status changes *inside* a lender's risk review.

**At a glance**

| Status | Main advantage in a lender's file | Question the bank still needs answered |
| --- | --- | --- |
| E-7 | Employer-linked Korean salary that can be verified | What happens when the contract and stay period end? |
| F-2 | More durable residence basis than many work statuses | Which F-2 subtype, how long remains, and what supports repayment? |
| F-4 | Long-term residence prospects for eligible overseas Koreans | Is there current, documented Korean income and a domestic credit file? |
| F-5 | Permanent-resident status and strong continuity | Are affordability, collateral, credit and product rules satisfied? |
| F-6 | Marriage-migrant residence and potential household stability | Whose income and ownership position legally count for this product? |

This is an underwriting map, not an approval ranking. For your own status first, use ExpatWon's [loan-by-visa tool](/tools/loan-by-visa/). Then read the distinctions below before submitting documents or signing a property contract.

## The four decisions hidden inside one loan application

When someone says a bank “accepts F-2,” it may mean four different things. First, the product's written eligibility conditions permit that particular borrower and residence status. Second, the institution can establish identity, address and lawful residence. Third, its credit team accepts income and repayment capacity. Fourth, the proposed term, amount and guarantee or collateral fit the product.

Fail the first test and a perfect credit score may accomplish nothing. Pass the first two and you can still be rejected because the income figure in your employer letter does not match the figure the bank can verify. Pass the income test and an existing car loan can reduce the amount available for a mortgage. A guarantee-backed jeonse loan adds yet another test: the guarantee provider has to accept the transaction.

Do not confuse **legal ability to hold a visa** with **contractual eligibility for a specific financial product**. Immigration determines your permission to stay. Banks and guarantors determine their lending conditions within the applicable financial rules. Neither decision substitutes for the other.

## E-7: the clean salary file, with a date attached

An E-7 employee often presents the most intelligible income story: a named employer, an employment contract, monthly salary deposits, tax withholding and National Tax Service records. For unsecured credit, that combination can be more useful than a more durable visa with no Korean earnings.

The friction is continuity. E-7 permission is linked to qualifying employment and approved activities. A lender considering a longer loan may ask what happens if that employment ends, the sponsor changes or the period of stay approaches expiry. Some products restrict the repayment term, request more remaining residence time, or direct the applicant toward a different facility. Those are product decisions—not a statutory rule that every E-7 borrower must finish repaying when the residence card expires.

What improves an E-7 file? Consistent payroll over time, employment tenure, a contract extension where genuine, no unexplained gaps in tax documents, and a clear account of the intended repayment source. A newly hired professional with a high offer letter but little paid salary history may still be harder to assess than a moderate earner who has worked continuously for years.

Ask the branch to distinguish a rejection based on **status eligibility** from one based on **insufficient remaining residence** or **insufficient verified income**. The remedies differ. Changing banks will not correct contradictory tax documents; extending stay will not cure an affordability problem.

## F-2: the subtype is part of the story

“F-2” is a family of residence arrangements, not a single commercial lending profile. An F-2-7 points-based resident, someone on a different F-2 category, and a person close to an extension decision can present different risks. The suffix and actual period of authorized stay matter. A lender looking only at the broad label misses relevant information; a customer relying on a generic “F-2 accepted” advertisement may also.

For the applicant, the advantage is potential continuity beyond one employment contract. That may reduce concern that a single job change interrupts lawful residence. It does not prove that salary continues, and it does not eliminate DSR or other affordability controls.

Someone with F-2-7 who switches from employment to freelance consulting should be prepared to show how income has been declared and for how long. Regular bank deposits are useful, but they are not always a substitute for tax-assessed income. The borrower with two years of consistently reported independent earnings may be easier to underwrite than one who started invoicing last month, even if both have the same residence card.

## F-4: settlement is easier to explain; income may not be

F-4 status is commonly associated with overseas Koreans, and its residence framework may provide a clearer longer-term basis for staying than a short employment-sponsored status. That is an advantage at the continuity screen. It is not proof that the person lives, earns or banks in Korea.

Consider an F-4 holder who arrives with substantial savings and overseas investment income. Their assets may be real, but a Korean bank still needs to determine whether the relevant product recognizes those assets and whether overseas earnings can be authenticated in a form its underwriting process accepts. A separate F-4 applicant with ordinary Korean payroll and years of local transaction history may present a much simpler file.

The distinction becomes more important with real estate. A foreign-national overseas Korean and a Korean citizen living abroad can have different identity, registration and product conditions, even if everyday conversation calls both “overseas Koreans.” Always give the bank the precise nationality and residence document, not an informal label.

## F-5: the strongest stay signal is still only one signal

F-5 permanent residency removes much of the uncertainty associated with a work-sponsored stay. From a debt-collection and long-term relationship perspective, that is meaningful. But the status does not create a right to a mortgage, reverse a delinquency, remove an income requirement or waive the bank's regulatory responsibilities.

A permanent resident applying for an unsecured loan still needs an acceptable income and credit file. For housing finance, collateral appraisal, location-based restrictions, existing debt and the lender's recognized income determine affordability. Some policy housing products have distinct citizenship or household eligibility rules that permanent residence alone does not satisfy.

There is also a documentation point. Permanent residency is not the same as a card that never needs attention. The Ministry of Justice explains that F-5 permanent-resident cards require periodic reissuance. An expired physical card and expired residence permission are not the same legal issue, but either can interrupt a bank's identity-verification workflow until corrected.

If an F-5 borrower receives a disappointing limit, ask for the constraint in numbers. Is the DSR calculation binding? Is the property in a more restrictive lending zone? Is the bank recognizing only one year's income? An extra residence document cannot change arithmetic that is already at the regulatory ceiling.

## F-6: marriage does not transfer borrowing capacity

F-6 marriage-migrant status can suggest household stability. It does not automatically make a Korean spouse a co-borrower or allow the foreign spouse to borrow against the spouse's income. Whether household income can be combined depends on the product, the ownership structure and the bank's specific rules.

For a personal credit loan in one spouse's name, the applicant's own income ordinarily carries substantial weight. In a mortgage, the household's property holdings, joint ownership, marital relationship and applicable underwriting rules can be relevant. In jeonse finance, guarantee-provider criteria may determine whether an apparently eligible household can proceed.

Do not treat the bank's request for a marriage certificate as proof it will count the spouse's entire income. A document can establish family relationship while the actual debt assessment remains individual. Equally, a working foreign spouse should not assume that a Korean spouse must be the applicant; ask whether the chosen product accommodates the intended borrower and ownership structure.

The best F-6 application is organized around the transaction: applicant, co-applicant if permitted, property title or lease, household income evidence, existing liabilities and the guarantee or mortgage conditions. The visa is a supporting identity and residence fact, not the loan itself.

## What does not change by visa type

Once a borrower is eligible for a product, fundamental repayment and risk limits still matter. Debt-service-ratio rules constrain many household loans. Mortgage loan-to-value calculations depend on the property and regulatory context. Unsecured loans depend on recognized income, credit data and existing obligations. The details can change by date, location and product, so a published numerical cap should never be applied to every borrower without checking scope.

A foreign applicant also needs a consistent Korean identity record. Transliteration differences among passport, residence card, bank account and employer records can delay the file. A mismatch is not necessarily a bad credit signal, but the institution cannot safely rely on records it cannot link to the same person.

Credit reports from NICE and KCB can be thin for recent arrivals even where payments have always been on time. A thin file is not evidence of financial misconduct; it is less evidence on which to assess repayment behavior. Time, regular transactions and correctly reported obligations address that problem better than repeated loan applications.

## The product matters more than the headline

“Loan” can mean four very different things.

**Personal credit loans** usually rely heavily on recurring verified income, domestic credit data and current liabilities. The ability to recover money after a borrower departs may matter to the lender, particularly for longer unsecured terms.

**Mortgages** add property valuation, senior claims, loan-to-value constraints, registration, settlement and sometimes regulated-location rules. Strong collateral does not always compensate for low recognized income.

**Jeonse loans** involve the borrower, bank, landlord, lease and often a guarantee institution. The bank may support foreign applicants through one guarantee route but not another. An attractive advertised rate means little if the guarantee provider excludes the tenant.

**Vehicle loans or installment finance** can use the vehicle and a different financing company, but the loan still creates liabilities that may affect later housing finance. Approval at a higher rate is not the same thing as a cheap overall financing plan.

Before choosing a lender, identify the product class and ask for its current foreign-applicant criteria in writing or via a documented branch response. A website summary from two years ago cannot establish availability today.

## Run a disciplined pre-application review

Build a one-page application summary with the exact status code, residence expiry date, nationality, employer or business type, annual tax-documented income, monthly debt payments, credit history length and purpose of borrowing. Attach only genuine, relevant documents. The goal is to make the financial story consistent before any institution has to reconstruct it.

For an employee, reconcile the employment certificate, contract, tax withholding statement and deposits. If annual tax records lag a recent raise, mark the gap and ask which evidence the lender can recognize. For a sole proprietor, distinguish revenue from taxable profit. For a company director, separate corporate turnover from personally taxed compensation. These are not semantic differences: they decide which earnings can service a private debt.

If the bank refuses, request the category of refusal rather than a generic “foreigner not possible.” Was the app unable to process your card? Was the product limited to particular residence statuses? Was the guarantee declined? Was DSR already at the ceiling? If the institution will explain the reason, the next step becomes clearer—and often cheaper than applying blindly elsewhere.

## The five-question bank conversation

Ask these questions in this order:

1. Does this exact product accept my **specific status and nationality** today?
2. Is the **remaining authorized stay** a product condition, and how is it assessed relative to maturity?
3. Which **income figure** will you recognize, and from which tax year or documents?
4. Is my limit determined by a **bank credit review, regulatory cap or external guarantee**?
5. If I do not qualify, what **specific missing condition** would change the result?

Keep the answers with your application notes. Rules and product lineups change. A quote from a call center is useful for screening, but final terms come from the lender's formal process and contract.

## Bottom line

A visa can make a borrower easier—or harder—to understand. It cannot turn unverified cash flow into income, erase existing debt or guarantee repayment. E-7 may offer excellent salary evidence with an employment-continuity question; F-2 and F-4 may strengthen settlement prospects; F-5 offers the clearest residence continuity; F-6 can bring household considerations into view. None operates as an automatic approval tier.

The better question is not “Which status wins?” It is “Which part of my file is preventing a lender from explaining how this debt will be repaid?” Solve that part first.
