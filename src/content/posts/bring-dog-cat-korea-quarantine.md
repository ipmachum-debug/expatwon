Your airline says the dog can fly, the carrier fits, the vaccination card is
in your bag. You land at Incheon and none of those facts answers the question
the quarantine officer is actually asking, which is whether this animal
satisfies Korea's import-quarantine conditions.

Airline permission and government clearance are separate systems. For most
dogs and cats the Korean side reduces to four things: identify the animal by
microchip, establish whether a rabies antibody test is required, hold the
correct government-issued quarantine certificate, and clear animal quarantine
after arrival and before customs.

Get those right and the inspection is short. Get one wrong and the cost is
never the quarantine fee.

## The fee is the small part

The statutory quarantine application fee for dogs and cats is
{{fig:pet-import-quarantine-fee}} per application. That number gives entirely
the wrong impression of the exercise.

Before the flight an owner may pay for microchip implantation, a veterinary
examination, rabies vaccination, neutralizing antibody testing, health
certificates, government endorsement or export certification, airline
transport, an approved carrier and any country-specific testing. None of that
sits under one Korean tariff — it is private veterinary and laboratory pricing
in the country you are leaving.

## Start with the microchip

The procedure requires the officer to compare the animal's microchip number
with the number in the quarantine documentation. That is not bookkeeping: it
is the only thing connecting the animal in front of them to the test result on
the paper. A flawless antibody certificate belonging to chip A proves nothing
about an animal carrying chip B.

So scan the chip before you travel rather than assuming one implanted years
ago still reads, and check that the same number appears, digit for digit, on
every document. One wrong character turns a veterinary record into an identity
problem at a counter where nothing can be fixed.

## Age and origin decide the antibody test

For a dog or cat aged 90 days or older from a rabies-affected country or
region, the standard requires a rabies-neutralizing antibody result of at
least {{fig:rabies-antibody-import-threshold}}, performed by an
internationally recognised laboratory or the exporting country's government
authority, within 24 months of the blood-draw date.

That is a more useful sentence than "your dog needs a rabies shot", because a
vaccination history and the import antibody requirement are different
documents and only one of them is being checked here.

Two situations change it. An animal **under 90 days** is exempt from the
antibody requirement — which is not the same as being exempt from paperwork;
identification and the applicable certificate requirements remain. And an
animal from a region Korea currently recognises as **rabies-free** is not
subject to the same requirement as an adult from a rabies-affected area.

That list is live. The current standard ties the status to World Organisation
for Animal Health reporting and allows a region to come off it after a rabies
report, so a screenshot taken today is not evidence two years from now. Check
before each departure, not before each move.

## The certificate has to come from a government

The document that does the work is the quarantine certificate issued or
certified by the competent government authority of the exporting country,
carrying the information the Korean standard requires. For an adult animal
from a rabies-affected origin that means tying together the animal, the
microchip, the blood collection, the testing institution and the result.

A private vaccination booklet does not automatically stand in for it. Your
veterinarian may produce the medical content; a government authority may still
have to issue or endorse the export document, and that appointment is the step
people discover too late.

The one narrow alternative: an EU Pet Passport can substitute where the
exporting country is an EU member state, and for the United Kingdom the
current wording limits that treatment to Northern Ireland. "A pet passport is
enough" is not a rule — which passport, issued where, under whose system.

## At the airport

Present the documentation to the animal-quarantine officer before completing
customs. The inspection is a document review, an identification check and a
clinical look at the animal: the chip against the certificate, the rabies
information against the standard.

Where identification and the rabies conditions are satisfied, same-day release
is provided for — for animals under 90 days or from recognised rabies-free
areas, a readable compliant microchip is the key identification requirement
under the simplified condition. The difference between twenty minutes and a
detention is usually a number printed wrongly thousands of kilometres away.

Where there is a problem, the animal can go to a quarantine facility until it
is corrected, or be held while microchipping or testing is completed. Those
corrective procedures are at the owner's cost, along with transport,
itinerary changes and, at the far end, return transportation. Incheon is
direct about the base case: without the required quarantine certificate, a dog
or cat is subject to return.

## Australia and Malaysia carry an extra step

Origin can impose requirements with nothing to do with rabies. Current rules
include additional Hendra and Nipah virus controls for **cats from Australia**
and **dogs and cats from Malaysia**, and the airport flags both specifically.
Qualifying certification or testing satisfies the controls; without the
required documentation the current rule provides for **21 days** of
quarantine.

Australia is also on the rabies-free list, which is exactly why a generic
checklist fails here. Rabies-free status removed one requirement and left
another in place. Do the country-specific check after the general one, every
time.

## Three origins, three different answers

| The animal | What changes |
| --- | --- |
| Adult dog from Japan | Rabies-free origin: the adult antibody requirement does not apply the same way. Microchip and official certificate still do. |
| Adult dog from a rabies-affected country | Needs the chip, the certificate, and a qualifying titre of 0.5 IU/ml or more inside the validity window. |
| Cat from Australia | Rabies-free, and still inside the Hendra provisions for cats — missing documentation means 21 days. |

"Japan means no quarantine" is the wrong reading of the first row. Japan
changes one part of the requirements.

## Budget it in layers

Veterinary preparation — chip, vaccination, examination, blood draw,
laboratory test. Government export paperwork — certificate, endorsement,
appointment, courier. Transport — airline fee, cargo or excess baggage,
carrier, any relocation agent, ground transport. Korean quarantine — the
₩10,000 application. And the layer worth spending real effort to avoid:
corrective veterinary work, additional testing, facility costs, itinerary
changes or return.

## Before you leave for the airport

Have the chip scanned and confirm every document shows that number exactly.
Know the animal's age against the 90-day line. Check Korea's current treatment
of the origin rather than an old post. If a titre is required, confirm it is
at or above the threshold and inside the validity window. Hold the exporting
government's certificate, not only a clinic record. Check whether the origin
adds requirements — Australia and Malaysia are the current examples. Confirm
the airline has separately accepted the animal, because quarantine compliance
reserves no space on an aircraft. Know where the animal-quarantine desk is in
your arrival terminal; Incheon operates offices in both Terminal 1 and
Terminal 2. And declare the animal before leaving customs.
