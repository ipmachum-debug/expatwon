For a foreign worker in Korea, unemployment benefit is not one question. It is
two systems that have to be satisfied at the same time, and satisfying either
one alone gets you nothing.

The Employment Insurance Act asks whether you were insured, for long enough,
left for a recognised reason, and are genuinely looking for work. The
Immigration Act asks whether you may lawfully be in Korea and look for work at
all.

## The first gate is not 180 days. It is whether you were ever insured

Korean employees are covered as a matter of course. Foreign employees are
sorted into groups by status of stay, and the group decides how coverage
arises:

| Group | How the Act applies |
|---|---|
| F-5, and certain F-2 and F-6 statuses | In full, without an application |
| C-4, E-1 to E-7, E-9, E-10, F-1, F-4, H-2 | **On application** — coverage exists once one has been filed |

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">E-9 and H-2 are not compulsory-coverage statuses</p>
  <p>This is stated the wrong way round in a great deal of material, usually as
  "E-9 is mandatory, E-7 is excluded." Both halves are wrong. E-9 and H-2 sit in
  the application-based group alongside the E-1 to E-7 professional statuses and
  F-4 — and permanent residence, which is often described as optional, is in the
  group where the Act simply applies.</p>
</div>

So two colleagues on identical salaries can hold different insured status, and
neither payslip is wrong.

## The zero that decides everything

Coverage on an application-based status generally begins **the day after the
application is filed**. There is
[no backdating](/tracked/employment-insurance-voluntary-coverage-backdating/).

```
worked from      March 2024
application       December 2025
insured from      December 2025
recoverable        nothing
```

An employee two years into a job whose employer applied in month 21 does not
have two years of insured period. They have three months.

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">Which makes this a week-one question, not an exit question</p>
  <p>By the time you are leaving, the answer is fixed and cannot be improved.
  If you are on E-7, E-9, F-4 or any other application-based status,
  the thing to do today is confirm your insured acquisition date — not the fact
  that <a href="/tracked/employment-insurance-employee-rate/">0.9%</a> is coming
  off your pay, which does not by itself establish when coverage started.</p>
</div>

The document to look at is your **insured status history** on the Employment
Insurance portal. Not the payslip, not the contract, and not what HR remembers.

## 180 insured days, which is not six months

Job-seeking benefit generally requires at least
[180 insured days](/tracked/unemployment-benefit-insured-days/) in aggregate
across the 18 months before leaving.

Insured days count days that were the basis for remuneration, plus paid weekly
holidays. Unpaid days are excluded.

Two reductions therefore stack for a foreign employee on an application-based
status:

<div class="steps">
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">1</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>The insured period may be shorter than the job</strong>, because it starts at the application.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">2</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>The insured days may be fewer than the calendar days</strong>, because unpaid days do not count.</div></div>
</div>

"I worked here exactly six months" is not a finding of 180 insured days.

## The reason for leaving, and the document that records it

Involuntary departure is the ordinary route: redundancy, being asked to resign,
the business closing, a fixed term ending without renewal.

Resigning to take a better job is the clearest case on the other side. But
voluntary resignation is not an automatic bar — the Act recognises certain
reasons as justifiable even where the employee left, including unpaid wages, a
serious deterioration in working conditions, unreasonable discrimination, health
grounds and a commute that has become impossible.

<div class="callout callout-note">
  <p class="callout-title">"Personal reasons" is a sentence with consequences</p>
  <p>The separation report your employer files carries the stated reason, and it
  matters. A departure that was really a requested resignation, reported as a
  voluntary one, becomes something you have to unpick at the employment centre.
  Keep the contract, the termination or non-renewal notice, any emails or
  messages about being asked to leave, and your payslips — the centre decides on
  the actual facts, and the evidence is what makes them actual.</p>
</div>

The reverse case exists too: a fixed term that ended because the employee turned
down a lawful renewal offer is not a straightforward non-renewal. Facts, not
labels.

## It is a re-employment benefit, not a severance payment

Job-seeking benefit supports the period during which someone able and willing to
work cannot find it. So it requires job-seeking registration, unemployment
recognition at the employment centre, and genuine re-employment activity.

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">The activity standard changed in September 2026</p>
  <p>Revised unemployment recognition and re-employment support rules took effect
  on 1 September 2026, so the number and kind of activities that count are worth
  confirming with the employment centre at the time you apply rather than from an
  article — including this one.</p>
</div>

## The clock starts when you leave, not when you apply

The benefit is generally payable within
[12 months of the day after leaving](/tracked/unemployment-benefit-claim-period/).

Which is where the two systems collide. The natural instinct after losing a job
on a work visa is to deal with the status problem first and the benefit later.
That sequence spends the claim window on the immigration process, and the days
are not added back at the end.

```
leave the job
  → immigration and employment centre, in parallel
  → not immigration, then the employment centre four months later
```
