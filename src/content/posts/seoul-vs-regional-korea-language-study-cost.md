The comparison everyone runs is monthly rent. It is the wrong number.

Tuition at a university-affiliated Korean language institute does not vary
much by city — the fees are set by each university and they cluster.
**Housing varies a great deal, and the deposit varies most.** For a
student, the deposit is the decisive figure, because it is cash you have
to produce before your first class and it stays locked up until you leave.

## Why the deposit is the number

Korean rental deposits scale with local property values. They are not a
month's rent held as security — they are a lump sum, and in the capital
region a modest one-room deposit is a serious amount of money for a
student.

That has three consequences specific to studying:

- **It is due before you start.** Tuition, deposit and the visa's own
  financial evidence all land in the same few weeks.
- **It is not spent, but it is gone.** The money exists, and you cannot
  use it while you live there.
- **It scales with the city, not with you.** A student and a professional
  renting the same room pay the same deposit.

How deposits work, what protects them, and what happens when a landlord
will not return one, is in

and
[protecting a rental deposit in Korea](/cost-of-living/rental-deposit-protection-korea/).

## Dormitories change the arithmetic entirely

The comparison above assumes you rent privately. A dormitory place usually
does not carry a deposit at all.

That single fact can make an expensive city viable and a cheap city
irrelevant. A student in a Seoul dormitory may need less day-one cash than
a student renting a one-room in a regional city, even though every other
number favours the regional city.

So the real first question is not *which city* but **can I get a
dormitory place, and for how long**. Institute dormitories are limited,
often allocated by term, and frequently not guaranteed beyond the first
one. Ask the institute two things in writing:

<div class="steps">
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">1</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Is a place guaranteed for the first term, and on what basis for later terms?</strong> "Usually available" is not an answer you can budget against.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">2</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>What is included?</strong> Meals, utilities, internet, and whether the fee is per term or per month. A dormitory figure that excludes meals is not comparable to one that includes them.</div></div>
</div>

**dormitory, goshiwon or one-room**.

## Which places, specifically

Seoul's university districts are where the housing cost concentrates —
**Sinchon, Hongdae, Seongbuk, Dongdaemun and the Gangnam side.** One-room
and officetel prices around those campuses sit well above the equivalent
near major regional universities.

The regional alternatives with large universities and Korean language
institutes are **Busan, Daegu, Daejeon and Gwangju**. The Study in Korea
search filters by region, by whether a university has a Korean language
institute, and by whether a dormitory is provided — which is the fastest
way to build a shortlist city by city instead of school by school.

  <p class="callout-title">"Regional is cheaper" is a tendency, not a rule</p>
  <p>Parts of Busan — Haeundae among them — and newly built officetels
  anywhere can cost more than outer Seoul. And a dormitory place is not
  guaranteed to every language student at every school. The comparison that
  matters is between two specific campuses and two specific buildings, not
  between two cities' reputations.</p>
</div>

## What a regional city actually gives you

**More Korean.** This is the honest advantage and it is not small. In
central Seoul, a foreign student can run daily life in English, and the
path of least resistance frequently is English — including with Koreans
who want the practice. In a regional city that path is narrower, so the
language gets used because using it is easier than not.

**A smaller foreign community**, which is the same fact wearing different
clothes. Whether that reads as an advantage depends entirely on why you
came.

**Lower everything, particularly housing.** Transport is cheaper and
shorter. Rent and deposits are materially lower.

## What it costs you

**Part-time work.** Fewer positions, and fewer that will take limited
Korean. If your budget depends on working, this is a real constraint —
and the permission rules for it are the same everywhere, which means the
binding limit outside Seoul is the availability of jobs, not the rules.

**Proximity to hiring.** If language study is a step towards working in
Korea, most international-facing hiring is concentrated in the capital
region. That has a value that does not appear in a rent comparison.

**Not, however, your choice of degree.** You do not have to take a degree
at the university whose institute taught you Korean. Studying at a regional
institute, taking TOPIK, then entering a Seoul university on D-2 is an
ordinary path — and a deliberate one for students who want the cheaper
housing during the language year and the Seoul university afterwards.

**Administrative convenience.** Immigration offices, embassies, and
services that assume English exist everywhere, but they are denser in
Seoul.

## How to compare two cities properly

Do it in two numbers, not one.

| | What goes in it |
|---|---|
| **Day-one cash** | Deposit + first month + any agency fee + one term of tuition + the balance the visa requires for that region |
| **Monthly run rate** | Rent + utilities + food + transport + phone + insurance |

Most people compare the second, choose, and are then ambushed by the
first. The day-one number is what decides whether a city is possible at
all; the monthly number decides whether the stay is comfortable.
