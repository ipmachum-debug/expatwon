For a long-stay foreign resident in Korea, a driver's licence is less
about driving than about infrastructure. It sits underneath car
purchases, leases and long-term rentals, insurance enrolment,
identity checks at finance and rental companies, and any claim to a
driving record.

An international driving permit (IDP) does not do that job. The Road
Traffic Act allows someone who has entered the country lawfully and
holds a valid IDP — or a mutually recognised foreign licence — to drive
in Korea **for one year from the date of entry**, within the vehicle
categories on the document. That is an exception permitting driving
*without* a Korean licence. It is not a route to holding one.

So if you are here on E-7, F-2, F-4, F-5 or F-6 with a life and a car in
Korea, exchanging your foreign licence for a Korean one is the durable
arrangement.

## Two layers people constantly merge

Almost every confusion in this process comes from collapsing two
separate questions into one.

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">⚠️ An apostille does not make your country "recognised"</p>
  <p>An <strong>apostille or embassy certification</strong> verifies that
  your foreign licence is genuine. <strong>Recognition</strong> is a
  separate determination — whether that country or region exchanges
  licences with Korea without testing. You can hold a flawless apostille
  and still sit the written test because your issuing jurisdiction is not
  on the recognised list. Two different layers, decided independently.</p>
</div>

Note also that a foreign licence on its own does not authorise driving
here. KOROAD is explicit: the original foreign licence alone is not
valid for driving in Korea. You need either a separate legal basis such
as an IDP, or an exchange into a Korean licence.

## Recognised vs non-recognised: the difference is the written test

KOROAD publishes a notice listing the countries and regions whose
licences Korea recognises — currently well over 130 jurisdictions, on a
list that is amended periodically. From a licence holder's point of
view, the practical difference is narrow but decisive:

| | Recognised country/region | Non-recognised |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Document review | Required | Required |
| Physical (aptitude) test | Required | Required |
| **Written test** | **Generally exempt** | **Generally required** |
| Skills test | Exempt | Exempt |
| Road test | Exempt | Exempt |
| Licence issued | Class 2 Ordinary (default) | Class 2 Ordinary (default) |

The written test is 40 multiple-choice questions, and you can sit it in
**Korean, English, Chinese or Vietnamese** — which removes the language
objection most people raise before they check.

Two structural points about the exchange itself:

- **You are exchanged into Class 2 Ordinary (2종 보통) by default**,
  regardless of what your home licence permitted. A handful of countries
  with specific bilateral arrangements — Belgium, Poland, Spain, Italy,
  Ireland among them — exchange by equivalent class instead.
- **The United States is decided state by state, not nationally.** Some
  states are recognised and some are not, and — the detail that catches
  people — a few recognised states (Oregon and Idaho, for instance)
  still require the written test on exchange. Check your specific state,
  not "the US."

Japan and Vietnam are on the recognised list; China currently is not,
so Chinese licences generally follow the non-recognised route. Europe is
country-by-country — EU membership decides nothing here.

<div class="callout callout-note">
  <p class="callout-title">📌 Check the list on the day</p>
  <p>The recognised-jurisdiction notice changes, and the US state
  breakdown changes within it. Whatever any guide says — including this
  one — open KOROAD's current list before you go to the test centre.</p>
</div>

## Alien registration matters more than your visa code

People try to answer "can I exchange?" from their visa letter. The
statute answers it differently: the Road Traffic Act disqualifies
foreign nationals who have **not completed alien registration** under
the Immigration Act, with defined statutory exceptions (such as those
exempt from registration).

So the practical line runs between long-stay residents who hold an ARC
or a domestic residence report — E-7, F-2, F-4, F-5, F-6 and the like —
and short-stay visitors on B-1, B-2 or C-3, who are generally outside
the registration system and therefore outside the ordinary exchange
route. For a short visit, the correct instrument is a valid IDP, not an
exchange application.

But here is where a second, easily-missed rule bites:

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">⚠️ "Any long-term visa skips the written test" is false</p>
  <p>For <strong>non-recognised</strong> licences, KOROAD's written-test
  exemption runs off its own list of residence statuses — currently
  A-1/A-2/A-3, D-7/D-8/D-9, E-1/E-3/E-4/E-7, F-4 and certain family
  members. <strong>F-2, F-5 and F-6 are not uniformly on that list.</strong>
  A permanent resident with a non-recognised licence can therefore face a
  written test that a D-8 investor does not.</p>
</div>

That inversion surprises people, and it is worth internalising: for the
exchange, **the licence's country decides the test, and the residence
status decides the exemption** — two independent variables, not one
hierarchy.

## What the paperwork is actually proving

The document list looks routine. What the reviewer does with it is not.

| Document | What it is really checking |
| --- | --- |
| Original foreign licence | A full, valid, unexpired licence — not provisional |
| Passport | Your stay in the issuing country and entry/exit history |
| ARC or domestic residence report | Legal status and identity in Korea |
| Apostille or embassy certification | Authenticity — issued within one year of application |
| Entry/exit record certificate | Your record from birth year to present |
| Photos ×3 | Recent (6 months), 3.5 × 4.5 cm |
| Aptitude test | At the test centre or a designated medical institution |

The core of the review is residence: KOROAD cross-checks the passport
and entry/exit records to confirm you were **actually in the issuing
country when the licence was issued** — and, where applicable, that you
were there for **90 days or more**. Where your nationality and the
issuing country differ, expect requests for further proof of that stay:
enrolment or employment certificates, tax documents, permanent
residency, a long-stay visa. Fail to evidence the 90 days and the
exchange can be refused.

The apostille has its own failure modes. It should be issued within one
year of your application and be in Korean or English. Staple marks,
separated pages, a missing reverse side of the licence, or a name or
date-of-birth mismatch can all trigger additional evidence requests or
outright rejection.

So the exercise is not "show them a foreign licence." It is
**proving that a genuine, valid licence was lawfully obtained in a
country where you actually were — with your identity and travel history
lining up behind it.**

## Your original licence may not come back with you

When the exchange completes, the original foreign licence is often
**collected and held**. You can retrieve it by evidencing imminent
departure (an air ticket, for instance), by voluntarily cancelling your
Korean licence, or on a request from your diplomatic mission. Unclaimed
licences can be disposed of after ten years under the consent process.

If you rotate between your home country and Korea and need to drive in
both, ask about collection **before** you hand anything over — the
practice varies by issuing country.

Two logistics: applications are made **in person** at a driver's licence
test centre (proxy applications are generally not accepted), and KOROAD
recommends arriving **before 17:00** — or **before 16:00** if you need
the written test — because the review takes time.

## The licence is a qualification, not an approval

Holding a Korean licence does not pre-approve anything financial.

For [car installment financing](/cars/car-installment-financing-halbu-korea/),
the lender still weighs your registration number, remaining stay, visa,
tenure, income, Korean credit file and down payment. An E-7 or F-series
holder whose remaining stay is shorter than the financing term, or whose
[credit file is thin](/loans/korean-credit-score-for-foreigners-nice-kcb/),
can still see the limit cut. Leases and long-term rentals simplify the
contract and insurance steps, but the counterparty is a finance or
rental company applying its own stay-stability and deposit conditions —
which is why the structures are worth comparing on
[total cost rather than monthly payment](/cars/long-term-car-rental-janggi-rent-korea/).

Insurance carries the sharper trap:

<div class="callout callout-tip">
  <p class="callout-title">💡 Licence exchange ≠ driving history</p>
  <p>Exchanging into a Korean licence does <em>not</em> transfer your
  overseas driving record. Insurers may recognise foreign history, but on
  their own terms and documents — a foreign insurance certificate, a
  driving record from your previous insurer. Run that process in
  parallel, as covered in
  <a href="/insurance/korean-car-insurance-for-expats-explained/">the car
  insurance guide</a>, or your first Korean premium will be priced as if
  you had never driven.</p>
</div>

The same logic explains why banks, insurers and finance companies keep
re-checking your ARC, residence report, passport and remaining stay: a
driver's licence is part of your identity documentation, but it never
substitutes for residence status itself — the distinction that also
governs [limited bank accounts](/banking/korean-limited-accounts-hando-gyejwa-explained/).

Sequence it correctly and the whole thing is administrative:
**status and registration → jurisdiction check → authentication and
residence proof → aptitude and, if required, written test → Class 2
Ordinary licence → original-licence question → insurance history and
financing as separate tracks.**
