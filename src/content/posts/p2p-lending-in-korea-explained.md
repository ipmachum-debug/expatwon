Korean P2P lending — legally, *online investment-linked finance* (온라인투자연계금융,
"온투업") — is routinely misunderstood on both sides of the transaction.
It is not a high-interest savings substitute for investors, and it is not
just "faster bank credit" for borrowers. The governing statute defines the
structure precisely: an investor's money is linked to a loan made to a
specific borrower, and the right to receive that loan's principal and
interest belongs to the investor.

That one sentence explains most of what follows. A bank takes deposits and
lends off its own balance sheet — depositors are not exposed to any single
borrower. A P2P platform, by contrast, is fundamentally a matchmaker that
screens, services, and collects. The investor is not depositing money with
the platform; the investor is taking the credit risk of an individual
borrower. If that borrower fails to repay, the loss lands on the investor —
and the Financial Services Commission explicitly labels these products
high-risk with no principal protection.

This guide looks at the structure from both seats: a company (including a
foreign-owned Korean company) considering P2P as a borrower, and an
individual or corporate investor considering it as an asset. If you are new
to Korean finance generally, start with
[opening a Korean bank account](/banking/how-to-open-a-bank-account-in-korea-as-a-foreigner/)
and [how loans work for foreigners](/loans/can-foreigners-get-a-loan-in-korea/) —
this article assumes that groundwork.

**A note for foreign readers:** the law does not exclude foreigners, but
platform onboarding runs on Korean identity verification — in practice an
ARC, a Korean bank account, and a Korean phone number. Whether a given
platform accepts foreign individual investors, or a foreign-owned 법인 as a
borrower, varies; confirm with the platform before you plan around it.

## For borrowers: not a bank substitute — capital on different terms

If you run a company in Korea, the right lens for P2P borrowing is not the
interest rate. It is three other questions: how fast can the money arrive,
what collateral structure does it require, and how does it interact with
your existing bank covenants?

Banks underwrite conservatively and comprehensively — credit rating,
financial statements, debt-service coverage, existing borrowings, collateral
capacity, the CEO's personal credit — and the process can take weeks. P2P
platforms each run their own models, and many weight the recovery value of a
specific asset (real estate, receivables, inventory, contracted revenue)
more heavily than the overall balance sheet. That makes P2P plausible for a
company whose bank credit lines are fully drawn, or which needs bridge
money on a timeline banks cannot meet.

A concrete shape: a company has a confirmed ₩1B asset sale closing in three
months but needs ₩200M of raw-material purchasing today. A new bank
facility would take a month-plus to underwrite and the working-capital line
is already in use. A six-month collateralized P2P loan can bridge that gap.
The decision variable is **not** the quoted 10% annual rate — it is the
all-in annualized cost after platform fees, collateral registration costs,
and any early-repayment charges, weighed against the actual usage period and
the risk that the sale slips.

Two hard warnings:

- **P2P is not a way around your bank.** If your existing loan agreements
  contain negative covenants — restrictions on additional borrowing,
  prohibitions on further security interests, financial-ratio maintenance —
  a P2P loan can breach them just like any other debt. Registering a
  junior mortgage without re-reading your bank documents can trigger an
  event of default (기한이익 상실) on the debt you already have.
- **P2P works as a bridge, not as a lifeline.** It is most efficient when
  the term is short and the repayment source is identified in advance. The
  moment a company starts rolling high-rate P2P money to repay existing
  debt with no clear repayment source, it is no longer financing strategy —
  it is extending a liquidity problem at premium prices.

## For investors: credit exposure without bond liquidity

For individuals with spare KRW — including expats settled in Korea — and
for corporate treasuries, the honest classification of P2P investment is
**illiquid single-name credit**, not a deposit alternative.

A quoted 8–12% yield is not an expected return. Subtract expected losses,
delinquency drag, recovery costs, platform fees, and tax. On a 10% product,
a permanent loss of 3% of principal plus a six-month recovery delay produces
a realized return that looks nothing like the marketing page.

### Investor limits are set by law

The enforcement decree caps what a general individual investor can deploy:

| Cap | Amount |
| --- | --- |
| Per single borrower | ₩5,000,000 |
| Across real-estate-collateral loans | ₩10,000,000 |
| Total connected investments | ₩30,000,000 |

Corporations and professional investors are regulated separately — the
decree carves out corporate and institutional connected investment rather
than applying retail caps. But a corporate badge does not shrink the risk;
it usually enlarges the ticket. A corporate treasury investing in P2P needs
its own internal per-project limits precisely because the legal ones do not
apply.

### Tax treatment differs between individuals and corporations

Do not compare returns using one withholding rate. Interest an individual
earns through an FSC-registered platform can qualify for the 14%
withholding rate applied to standard interest income, while interest paid
to a domestic corporation on non-business loans (비영업대금의 이익) is
subject to a 25% withholding interpretation under the Corporate Tax Act —
and a corporation's real tax burden is settled in its annual corporate tax
return alongside everything else. Confirm the treatment with your
accountant before investing, not after.

## The most dangerous number is not the delinquency rate — it is how it is calculated

The most common evaluation mistake is comparing platforms on their published
delinquency ratios.

A platform showing 2% is not automatically safer than one showing 5%. Three
mechanical distortions make the headline number almost meaningless on its
own:

- **Growth dilution.** When new originations grow fast, the denominator
  inflates and the ratio falls, regardless of underwriting quality.
- **Extension accounting.** Loans whose maturities were extended may be
  reported as current.
- **Sale and removal.** Delinquent loans sold off to collection firms can
  exit the statistics entirely.

What to examine instead: cumulative defaults against cumulative
originations, the current outstanding balance of delinquent loans, the share
of 30-day and 90-day-plus delinquencies, the average time from default to
completed collateral disposal, and the final loss rate after recovery. For
real-estate project finance and junior-lien products, expect a year or more
between a default and actual cash recovery.

Also separate two facts that get conflated: "the loan is delinquent" and
"the principal is lost." A well-collateralized loan with little senior debt
can recover most of its value — slowly. Conversely, a platform with a
pristine delinquency ratio can hand investors a large loss from a single
project whose collateral was overvalued.

## "Secured" means nothing — seniority means everything

Never assess a product from the phrase "real-estate collateral" alone.

What matters is the realistic collateral value and your position in the
queue. Take a property appraised at ₩2.0B carrying a ₩1.4B senior bank
loan, with the P2P platform registering a ₩400M junior mortgage. Nominal
LTV: 90%. If the property sells at auction for 75% of appraisal — ₩1.5B —
the junior tranche's recovery is severely impaired before costs are even
deducted.

For mortgages, the registry (등기부) priority is the core fact, and
auction-distribution claimants that outrank you — certain taxes, wage
claims, tenant deposits — need checking too. For receivables or deposit
pledges, the words "pledge registered" matter less than whether the
underlying claim actually exists, whether the third-party debtor was
notified or consented, and whether set-off could erase it.

The law is on your side here, if you use it: platforms are required to
disclose collateral value, valuation method, and how the security is
registered, plus the collection and debt-sale procedures and costs that
apply on default. That disclosure section — not the banner yield — is the
part of the listing worth reading.

## Choose platforms on underwriting and recovery, not yield

Operating a P2P platform requires FSC registration, with minimum capital
tiered at ₩500M, ₩1B, or ₩3B depending on the linked-loan balance, plus
staffing, systems, and internal-control requirements. Registration is the
floor, not a safety endorsement — it tells you the platform is legal, not
that its products are sound.

Four practical screens:

1. **The platform's own financials.** Repeated operating losses and
   fast-eroding equity undermine the platform's ability to manage a loan
   book for years — which is what servicing and collection require.
2. **Underwriting staff.** A platform selling real-estate products with no
   real-estate-finance or appraisal expertise on the team is a pass at any
   yield.
3. **Growth rate of the loan book.** Unusually fast origination growth and
   loosening credit standards tend to travel together.
4. **Behavior after default.** How transparently does the platform disclose
   auction filings, security enforcement, debt sales, and workout
   negotiations? A platform that leads with yields and rewards while
   staying vague about recovery procedures has its priorities inverted —
   and the FSC has repeatedly warned about operators dangling excessive
   rewards or implied loss protection.

## Diversify risk factors, not product counts

Twenty investments are not twenty diversifications. If all twenty are junior
liens on Seoul officetels, you effectively hold one bet on one property
market — even across different platforms, they share the same appraisal
conventions and the same real-estate cycle.

Real diversification cuts across borrower, collateral type, region,
maturity, platform, and repayment source. A workable internal rulebook for,
say, ₩300M of investable cash: no more than ₩30M to any single borrower, no
more than 20–25% of the total on any single platform, and a deliberate mix
of recovery structures (real-estate-secured, personal credit, business
receivables) rather than a big count of similar products.

For corporate treasury money the bar is higher still. Funds earmarked for
payroll, VAT, corporate tax, or supplier payments do not belong in P2P at
all — a contractual maturity date is not a guarantee the borrower performs
on it. Only surplus cash with no planned use for at least six to twelve
months should even be considered. (If you are structuring a Korean company
in the first place, see
[registering a business in Korea](/business/registering-a-business-in-korea-as-a-foreigner/).)

## The working checklist

- **Borrowers:** check your existing lenders' additional-borrowing and
  negative-pledge covenants *first*.
- **Investors:** compute net recovery value — appraised value minus senior
  claims — not the headline collateral figure.
- Judge net yield after tax, fees, and expected loss, not the quoted rate.
- Weigh 90-day-plus delinquencies and final recovery rates over the
  published delinquency ratio.
- Set internal caps per borrower and per platform before the first won
  moves.

P2P finance is not good money because it is faster than a bank, and not a
good investment because it out-yields a deposit. It is a legitimate tool for
borrowers with a short, defined funding gap and an identified repayment
source, and for investors willing to read collateral structures and
recovery procedures themselves. Used on headline numbers alone, it becomes
the most expensive money either side ever touches.
