The contract matters twice for a foreign employee. It sets your pay, and it
sits underneath your status of stay — which is why a clause a Korean
colleague would shrug at is not one you can shrug at.

Three things in it are misread more than everything else combined:
**probation**, **the two-year rule**, and **the difference between signing a
contract and receiving one.**

<p class="hand">Read it as a status document that happens to state a salary.</p>

## Nationality is not one of the terms

Article 6 of the Labor Standards Act prohibits discriminatory treatment of
working conditions on grounds of nationality, religion or social status.

So the Act's protections do not arrive in a reduced form because the
employee is foreign, and a contract cannot be worse *because* of who signed
it. Everything below applies to an E-7 holder on the same footing as the
person at the next desk.

## Signing is not receiving

Article 17 requires the employer to specify the key terms and to **deliver
them to the employee in writing** — wages and how they are composed and
paid, contractual working hours, holidays, and annual paid leave.

An electronic document satisfies it. A signed contract sitting in the
company's filing cabinet does not.

The obligation is backed by a criminal fine of up to **₩5,000,000** —
[the written-delivery penalty](/tracked/employment-contract-written-delivery-penalty/).
That figure is the reason to ask rather than let it go.

<div class="callout callout-tip">
  <p class="callout-title">Ask in a medium that keeps a record</p>
  <p>Email or KakaoTalk, not across a desk. If the copy never arrives, the
  request itself is evidence — and if it does, you have both the contract and
  the date you got it.</p>
</div>

## A clause below the statute simply fails

Article 15 is the most useful thing in the Act for someone reading a
contract they have already signed: terms that fall below the standards the
Act sets are **void as to that part**, and the statutory standard applies in
their place.

Two consequences people miss:

<div class="steps">
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">1</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>The rest of the contract survives.</strong> One bad clause does not collapse your employment — the clause is replaced, not the contract.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">2</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Your agreement did not make it valid.</strong> "You signed it" is not an answer. A sub-statutory term is ineffective whether or not you consented to it.</div></div>
</div>

Which reframes the whole exercise. You are not reading the contract to catch
everything before signing — you are reading it to know which clauses are
real.

## Probation: the correction that is worth money

Almost every English-language explanation of Korean probation says it is
limited to three months. That sentence is a collapsed version of something
else.

| Often said | What the rule actually is |
|---|---|
| Probation is capped at three months | Probation itself has no such statutory cap — six months can be agreed |
| You get 90% of your salary | 90% of the **statutory minimum wage**, not of your contracted pay |
| It applies to any probation | Only where the contract runs a **year or more**, you are genuinely on probation, and the occupation is not designated simple labour |
| No dismissal protection during probation | Dismissal still needs a justifiable reason — see below |

What the three months limits is the special rule under Article 5 of the
Minimum Wage Act:
[the probation reduction period](/tracked/probation-minimum-wage-reduction-period/).

At the 2026 minimum wage of
[₩10,320 an hour](/tracked/minimum-wage/), the reduction floor is:

```
minimum wage             ₩10,320 / hour
× 90%                     ₩9,288 / hour
applies for at most       3 months of probation
```

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">90% of the minimum wage is not 90% of your salary</p>
  <p>An employer reducing an agreed ₩4,000,000 to ₩3,600,000 "because of
  probation" is not applying this rule — it has no bearing on a salary already
  far above the minimum. A reduction from your contracted pay needs its own
  basis in the contract, and a clause that simply asserts one is worth
  querying before the first payslip, not after the third.</p>
</div>

## Probation dismissal is easier, not free

There is a real difference during probation, and it is narrower than it
sounds: the advance-notice requirement in Article 26 does not apply to an
employee who has been continuously employed for less than three months.

Everything else stays:

- dismissal still requires a justifiable reason
- for probation, that reason is normally the evaluation — which means there
  has to have been one, against criteria you were told about
- Article 27 still requires the **grounds and the date in writing**

A dismissal delivered verbally or by message, with no evaluation behind it,
is not made lawful by the word "probation" appearing in your contract.

## Fixed term: two years, and the exception that catches E-7

The rule is that a fixed-term employee may not be used beyond two years, and
that continuing past it is treated as a contract without a fixed term —
[the fixed-term two-year rule](/tracked/fixed-term-two-year-rule/).

The exceptions are where the general article stops being true for this
audience. Longer engagement is allowed for, among others:

<div class="steps">
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">1</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Completion of a defined project</strong>, where the period needed is fixed.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">2</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Covering an absent employee</strong> until they return.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">3</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Education and training periods</strong> required for the work.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">4</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Older workers</strong>, and <strong>engagement using professional knowledge or skills</strong> — the one that reaches a large share of E-7 hiring.</div></div>
</div>

So "two years and I convert" is not a plan. If your renewal matters to your
status, the question to ask is which basis the company thinks it is hiring
you on.

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">Your contract period and your visa period are different things</p>
  <p>A contract running to December does not extend a status expiring in
  September, and a status valid for two years does not renew a contract. They
  are set by different bodies under different rules and they have to be
  tracked separately. Where a renewal is expected, get it in writing early
  enough to use for the status extension — not the week the contract ends.</p>
</div>

## The money clauses, in the order they cause trouble

**Gross or net.** A salary figure means nothing until the contract says
which. Deductions on a Korean payslip are substantial and split across
several bases —
[reading your Korean payslip](/employment/reading-your-korean-payslip/) is
the line-by-line version. A "net salary" arrangement also has tax
consequences of its own, so it should be stated explicitly rather than
assumed from the number sounding low.

**Fixed overtime built into the salary.** Where a contract folds a set
number of overtime hours into the monthly figure, the hours it covers should
be identifiable. If they are not written down, nobody can tell whether the
hours you actually worked exceeded them — and the difference is payable.

**Bonuses.** Fixed and paid on set dates, or discretionary. The distinction
decides whether it is part of your pay or a gift, and it should be on the
page, not in a conversation.

**Severance.** Statutory severance requires **one year or more of continuous
service** and an average of **15 hours or more a week over four weeks**. A
clause dividing severance into your monthly salary is not a way of paying it
early — the entitlement arises on the terms the statute sets.

## Clauses that do not survive contact with the Act

**Penalty and predetermined damages.** Article 20 prohibits an agreement
fixing a penalty or a predetermined amount of damages for breach. A round
number attached to leaving early is the thing the article exists to stop. An
agreement to reimburse identifiable costs the employer actually paid — a
named training course, with real invoices — is a different construction and
can hold, depending on how it is framed and how long it binds you.

**Non-compete.** Not automatically void and not automatically enforceable.
Validity is judged on the whole picture: whether there is a genuine interest
to protect, your position and access, the duration, the geographic and
sector scope, and whether anything was paid for the restriction. A clause
barring you from the entire industry nationwide, indefinitely, with no
compensation, is the weak end of that range.

**Language.** Where both a Korean and an English version exist, one of them
governs — and it is often the Korean one. If the contract does not say,
that is itself a question to ask, because the version you negotiated may not
be the version that decides a dispute.

## Termination, from the other side

| | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Advance notice | 30 days, or 30 days' ordinary wages in lieu (art. 26) |
| Form | Written, stating the grounds and the date (art. 27) |
| Under 3 months' continuous service | Notice not required — the written-notice and justifiable-reason requirements remain |

A dismissal that fails the written-notice requirement is not merely
impolite. It is the point at which the process itself is defective, which is
worth knowing before you agree that leaving was your idea.

## The order to read it in

```
who the parties are, and the contract period
  → is it fixed-term, and on what basis
  → gross or net, and what the figure includes
  → fixed overtime hours, if any, and whether they are stated
  → probation: length, and what exactly is reduced
  → bonus: fixed or discretionary
  → severance and annual leave
  → penalty, training-cost and non-compete clauses
  → which language version governs
  → and last: did they actually hand you a copy
```
