There are two different things in Korea called a business account, and only one
of them has a penalty attached.

One is a product: a chequing account the bank opens against your business
registration certificate. The other is a filing: the **business-use account**
reported to the tax office under the Income Tax Act.

Doing the first does not do the second. That is the whole of the confusion, and
it is expensive.

## 1. A sole proprietor and the business are the same person

Unlike a company, a sole proprietorship has no separate legal personality. The
income is ultimately the proprietor's business income, and the business debts
are in principle the proprietor's debts.

Which means using an account in your own name for the business is not, by
itself, doing something wrong. There is no rule that a sole proprietor's money
must sit in a specially branded account.

What matters is two different things: that the account is used for the business
in a way that can be distinguished, and that where the law imposes a reporting
obligation, the report is made.

## 2. Not every sole proprietor has this obligation

The business-use account obligation in **article 160-5** of the Income Tax Act
attaches to **double-entry bookkeeping obligors**.

Whether you are one turns on the previous year's revenue measured against
thresholds that differ by industry. Registering a business does not make you one
on day one.

The direction that catches people is the other one. A business that grows past
the threshold becomes an obligor without any visible event — no letter, no
change to the bank account, nothing that feels like a new duty. The obligation
arrives from a number in last year's accounts.

## 3. The deadline runs from the start of the period, not from an event

A double-entry bookkeeping obligor must report the business-use account to the
competent tax office within
[six months of the start of the taxable period](/tracked/business-account-registration-deadline/)
in which the obligation arises.

Where the obligation and the start of business coincide, the timing is judged
from the following taxable period under the rules for that case. And changing or
adding an account has its own timing, tied to the global income tax filing
deadline.

So this is not a one-time registration that stays correct forever. Switching
banks is a reportable event.

## 4. Reporting it and using it are two obligations, not one

A double-entry bookkeeping obligor must use the business-use account for
transactions settled through a financial institution, and for **paying and
receiving wages and rent**.

That second clause is where most of the failures live. Registering an account at
Hometax and then continuing to take sales into an old personal account, or
paying staff and the landlord from one, leaves the use obligation unmet even
though the report was filed.

The opposite is also poor practice, if not a penalty. One account carrying
groceries, personal shopping, family transfers, business sales and staff wages
together is a reporting obligation technically satisfied and an accounting
problem created.

## 5. Non-use and non-reporting are calculated differently

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">"Revenue × 0.2%" is the wrong formula for non-reporting</p>
  <p>It is one of the two bases, not the answer. Article 81-8 takes the
  <strong>higher</strong> of revenue attributable to the unreported period × 0.2%
  and the qualifying transaction amount × 0.2%. For a business whose payments and
  receipts through financial institutions exceed its reported revenue for that
  period, the second base is larger — and that is the one that applies.</p>
</div>

**Not using a reported account** where you should have: **0.2%** of that
transaction amount. [Tracked here](/tracked/business-account-nonuse-penalty-rate/).

**Never reporting an account at all**: the
[higher of the two bases](/tracked/business-account-nonregistration-penalty-formula/),
each at 0.2%.

Both can apply where there is no calculated income tax at all. A loss-making
year is not a shelter.

## 6. It can also cost a tax reduction, which is often the bigger number

A double-entry bookkeeping obligor who has not met the business-use account
obligation may find certain tax reductions under the Restriction of Special
Taxation Act restricted.

Two things about that, in order.

It is **not** "miss the report and every tax break disappears" — the restriction
applies to the provisions that carry the condition, not to reliefs generally.

But for a business actually claiming one — the small and medium enterprise
reductions, for example — the amount lost to a disallowed reduction can exceed
the penalty by a wide margin. Which is the reason not to treat the report as a
piece of administration to get to later.

## 7. Paying from a personal account does not void the VAT deduction

This connection gets drawn constantly and it is wrong.

Input VAT deduction turns on three things: whether the purchase relates to the
business, whether you hold qualifying evidence such as a tax invoice or card
sales slip, and whether the item falls on the statutory non-deductible list.

Which account the money left is not one of the three. A genuine business
purchase, properly evidenced, is not disallowed because it was paid from a
personal account.

<div class="callout callout-note">
  <p class="callout-title">What separation actually buys you</p>
  <p>Not the deduction — the ease of proving it. Where business and household
  money run through the same account continuously, the questions that follow are
  who spent it, whether it was really a business cost, and whether it was the
  proprietor's own consumption. Separating the accounts is a control that makes
  those answerable from records rather than from memory. How the ordinary VAT
  cycle works is in
  <a href="/business/korean-vat-for-small-business-owners/">Korean VAT for small business owners</a>.</p>
</div>

## 8. The business credit card is the same idea, with the same limit

The National Tax Service lets a sole proprietor register a credit card in their
own name as a **business credit card** at Hometax. Registered card usage can then
be viewed at Hometax, which makes managing purchase records for the VAT return
considerably easier.

It does not make everything on the card deductible. Household expenses,
entertainment-type spending, costs relating to non-business passenger vehicles
and the statutory non-deductible transactions are all still judged separately.

The registration is a retrieval mechanism, not a characterisation. The mechanics
are in
[registering a business card on Hometax](/business/registering-a-business-card-on-hometax/).

## 9. The simplest structure that works

<div class="steps">
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">1</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>One business chequing account</strong> and one business card, separate from personal ones.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">2</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>All sales in</strong> through that account, without exception for the convenient ones.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">3</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Materials, rent, wages, utilities and tax out</strong> of the same system.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">4</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Living costs by transfer</strong> from the business account to the personal one, recorded as a proprietor's drawing.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">5</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Report it to the tax office</strong> if you are a double-entry bookkeeping obligor, and re-report if you change accounts.</div></div>
</div>

Step 4 is the one that does the work. A single transfer with a clear character
puts a boundary in the ledger between money the business earned and money the
proprietor spent — which is exactly the boundary that is impossible to
reconstruct afterwards from a mixed account.

Opening the underlying account as a foreign national is its own procedure, and
covered in
[how to open a bank account in Korea as a foreigner](/banking/how-to-open-a-bank-account-in-korea-as-a-foreigner/).

A sole proprietor's problem is a reporting obligation and a habit. A company's
is different in kind: there, the money in the account genuinely is not the
director's, and moving it needs a reason.
