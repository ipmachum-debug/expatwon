Two administrations run in parallel and people keep merging them.

**Your visa** decides what you may do in Korea. **Tax residence** decides
what Korea taxes. Holding an alien registration card does not make you a
tax resident, and holding a foreign passport does not make you a
non-resident.

Getting this wrong is not a matter of a few percentage points. It changes
**which income is in scope at all.**

<p class="hand">The first number to work out is not a rate. It is how long you have lived here, and how.</p>

## 1 · Residence is about your life, not the calendar alone

The Income Tax Act treats an individual as a resident who has a **domicile**
in Korea, or a **place of residence for 183 days or more**.

Reading that as "183 days and you are in" is the common mistake. The
domicile limb can be satisfied before the day count is — where someone has
an occupation that ordinarily requires continuous residence of 183 days or
more, or has family sharing their livelihood in Korea and, judged on
occupation and assets, is recognised as likely to reside continuously for
183 days or more.

What actually gets weighed:

<div class="steps">
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">1</div>
    <div class="step-body">Actual time in Korea</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">2</div>
    <div class="step-body">The occupation you carry out here</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">3</div>
    <div class="step-body">Where your family lives</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">4</div>
    <div class="step-body">Your housing arrangements in Korea</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">5</div>
    <div class="step-body">Assets and the base of your life, here and abroad</div></div>
</div>

So a foreign executive on a long Korean employment contract who has moved
their family here and taken a home cannot always be treated as a
non-resident simply because 183 days have not yet elapsed.

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">And nationality is not a test at all</p>
  <p>Neither a Korean passport nor a foreign one determines this, and neither
  does the visa class. D-2, E-7, F-2 and F-5 tell immigration what you may
  do. They do not tell the tax office where you live.</p>
</div>

## 2 · What that status puts in scope

| | Resident | Non-resident |
|---|---|---|
| Basic scope | Domestic and foreign income | Korean-source income |
| Short-stay foreign exception | Applies where Korean residence is 5 years or less in the past 10 | Not applicable |
| Foreign income | Needs review in principle | Korean-source focus |
| Personal and tax credits | Resident rules available | Narrower |
| Tax treaty | Dual residence, foreign tax credit | Exemptions and reduced rates on Korean-source income |

A resident is, in principle, taxed on income arising both inside and
outside Korea. A non-resident is taxed on Korean-source income.

Which means the same salary can sit alongside a completely different
picture of overseas interest, dividends, rent and business income depending
on a status question that has nothing to do with the salary.

## 3 · The exception that changes it for most arrivals

Here is the provision that stops "resident means worldwide income" being
true for the average foreign arrival.

A **foreign resident whose Korean residence in the ten years to the end of
the tax period totals five years or less** is taxed on foreign-source
income only to the extent it is:

```
paid in Korea
  or
remitted to Korea
```

So an executive posted here for the first time, holding overseas dividends,
rent or investment income, does not mechanically report the entire worldwide
position the way a settled Korean resident does.

And the reverse follows: once cumulative residence passes that threshold,
the worldwide basis becomes much more important. The count is **cumulative
across ten years**, so it moves silently as a stay lengthens rather than on
any anniversary. Tracked at
[the 5-of-10 rule](/tracked/foreign-short-term-resident-5-of-10-rule/).

## 4 · Dual residence is a treaty question, not a Korean one

Executives moving between countries can be treated as tax residents of both
Korea and home at the same time. Korean domestic law alone does not settle
that.

The applicable **tax treaty** does, typically through a sequence: permanent
home, then centre of vital interests, then habitual abode.

Which is why the file to keep is larger than a stamp record:

| Keep | Because |
|---|---|
| Entry and exit dates | The day count |
| Korean housing contract | Permanent home |
| Overseas housing | The other permanent home |
| Where your family lives | Centre of vital interests |
| Employment contract and who pays the salary | Source and treaty analysis |
| Foreign tax paid | Foreign tax credit |
| Remittances into Korea | The 5-of-10 exception |

## 5 · The flat rate: what it is, and what it is not

A foreign employee meeting the conditions may elect a **flat 19%** on
Korean employment income instead of the progressive brackets. With local
income tax at 10% of the income tax figure, the headline combined rate is
**20.9%**.

Two conditions are attached, and both get dropped when the number is quoted.

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">It is an employment income provision</p>
  <p>Business income, interest, dividends and rental income are taxed under
  the ordinary rules whether or not you elect it. "I pay 19% in Korea" is
  only ever a statement about a salary.</p>
</div>

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">And there is an entry deadline</p>
  <p>The election is open to a foreign employee who <strong>first began
  providing work in Korea on or before 31 December 2026</strong>, for tax
  periods ending within twenty years of that first day. That date has been
  extended before and is a standing candidate in year-end tax revisions —
  which is why it is tracked at
  <a href="/tracked/foreign-worker-flat-tax-entry-deadline/">the flat tax entry deadline</a>
  rather than printed here as permanent.</p>
</div>

## 6 · 19% of what, exactly

The most expensive misunderstanding in this whole area.

The flat rate is **not** applied to a tax base computed the ordinary way. It
is applied to the relevant employment income, and electing it means giving
up, as a rule, the exemptions, earned income deduction, personal deductions,
special deductions, tax credits and reductions that the ordinary method
allows.

There is an exception: certain **welfare-type benefits** remain non-taxable
under conditions set by decree, so payroll design should be checked against
the current year's rules rather than assumed away entirely.

```
Ordinary method   income → deductions → tax base → 6–45% → credits
Flat election     employment income → 19%
```

Which is why **19% is not automatically cheaper.**

## 7 · When each one wins

The ordinary method runs the progressive scale from **6% to 45%** on a tax
base reached after the statutory deductions.

| | Flat 19% | Progressive |
|---|---|---|
| Who | Qualifying foreign employees | Any employee |
| Rate | 19% | 6–45% |
| With local income tax | 20.9% | Plus 10% of the income tax figure |
| Deductions | Excluded in principle | Available |
| Credits and reductions | Excluded in principle | Available on conditions |
| Tends to suit | High income, few deductions | Middle income, many deductions |

An employee with a spouse and dependants, and the deductions that come with
them, frequently does better on the ordinary method. A senior expatriate on
a large salary with little to deduct frequently does better on the flat
rate.

### Do not decide from the headline

A ₩200m salary is not "₩200m × 20.9%, obviously cheaper". Under the
ordinary method the earned income deduction and the rest bring the tax base
down before any rate applies; under the flat election you have surrendered
most of that.

The only reliable method is to run the same year twice:

```
expected annual employment income
  → ordinary tax base
  → progressive tax
  → deductions and credits
  → local income tax
  → compare against the flat calculation
```

The National Tax Service's own year-end settlement service for foreign
employees lets you compare flat against progressive directly, which is the
comparison to trust over any rule of thumb.

## 8 · Foreign employees still do year-end settlement

Monthly withholding and a January settlement apply to a foreign employee
receiving employment income from a Korean company much as they do to a
Korean one.

A **resident** foreign employee can generally access a deduction and credit
structure similar to a Korean employee's, though some housing-related
deductions differ in scope. A **non-resident**'s deductions are narrower.

So a payroll team cannot apply one foreign-employee profile. Five things
have to be established per person:

## 9 · Check the treaty after domestic law, not instead of it

Professors, researchers and short-term secondees may find specific exemption
articles in a treaty.

**There is no general rule that foreign professors and researchers are
exempt from Korean income tax.** Treaties differ by country, and so do the
exemption period, the qualifying institution and who pays the remuneration.

So the order is: reach a conclusion under Korean law, then read the treaty's
articles on employment income, teachers and researchers, business profits,
interest and dividends, and pensions.

And keep two things apart:

| | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Tax treaty** | Which country may tax, and at what limit |
| **Foreign tax credit** | Relief in Korea for tax already paid abroad |

They are not the same mechanism and they are not alternatives to each other.

## 10 · Build the file in your first year

The dangerous version of this is reconstructing last year's life in May,
from memory, during the filing period.

From arrival, keep one file:

- first day of work in Korea
- entry and exit dates
- Korean housing contract
- where family lives
- overseas assets and income
- foreign tax paid
- which country pays the salary
- remittances into Korea
- whether the flat rate was elected

The cumulative five-years-in-ten count belongs in that file too, updated
annually — because it changes the scope of your return without ever
announcing itself.

## The order that works

```
visa status
  → tax residence
  → scope: domestic only, or worldwide, or the 5-of-10 limit
  → treaty
  → flat 19% against the progressive scale, computed
  → withholding, year-end settlement, and a May return where needed
```

Get the residence question wrong and the rate never mattered — you were
reporting the wrong set of income all along.

Which is why the first figure a foreign taxpayer should work out is not a
percentage. It is **how many years, and under what circumstances, they have
lived in Korea.**
