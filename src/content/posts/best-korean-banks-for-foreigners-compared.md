Every major Korean bank will open an account for a foreign resident. That
is not the question worth asking.

The question is which one fits what you will actually do with the money —
because the four differ sharply on the things that matter to a foreigner:
how many languages the app really speaks, whether you can complete
identity work without visiting a branch, what a monthly transfer home
costs, and whether there is a branch open when you are not at work.

This is a comparison of the four major commercial banks on those axes.
The mechanics that apply everywhere — what documents to bring, why your
account starts restricted, how remittance limits work — are covered
separately and linked at the end. Nothing here repeats them.

## Hana: built around foreign exchange

Hana has kept the foreign-exchange and foreign-customer business it
absorbed from KEB, and it still shows. Its foreigner platform is
**Hana EZ**, a multilingual app built around remittance and
foreign-customer services.

What is genuinely useful there is administrative rather than
promotional. Work that used to require a branch visit — customer
verification registration, passport-number changes, issuing certificates
— now runs inside the app, and the designation of a remittance bank can
follow in the same flow rather than as a second trip.

That matters more than it sounds if your documents change during your
stay. Arriving on a passport, then obtaining an alien registration card,
then renewing it, produces a sequence of identity updates that each
otherwise cost a weekday afternoon.

Hana also separates its branch network for foreign customers, flagging
**Global Desk** branches and **Sunday Banking** branches distinctly. For
someone on a manufacturing or service schedule who cannot reach a bank
between nine and four on a weekday, that single feature can outweigh
every fee comparison in this article. Not every Hana branch opens on
Sunday — check the specific branch's hours before travelling to it.

## Shinhan: the furthest along on doing it without a branch

Shinhan's platform is **SOL Global**, supporting **16 languages** and
built so that account opening, currency exchange and overseas remittance
can be completed on the phone.

The app was overhauled at the end of 2025. Sign-up was simplified, and
the range of services that can be initiated remotely widened — including
lending products aimed at foreign customers.

That makes Shinhan worth considering if your intention is to keep the
whole relationship — salary account, card, later a loan — inside one bank
and mostly inside the app.

## KB: the clearest cost structure for sending money home

KB's foreigner remittance service is **KB Quick Send**, run over Visa's
global network from inside the KB Star Banking app.

The pricing is the point:

| | KB Quick Send |
|---|---|
| Fee | **₩5,000 per transfer** |
| Intermediary and wire charges | None |
| Destinations | 47 countries |
| Delivery | Within one business day |

No intermediary bank fee is unusual and it is the whole reason to look at
this product. On a conventional SWIFT wire, the deductions taken by
correspondent banks in the middle are the part you cannot see in advance
and cannot predict — which is exactly what
[the remittance guide](/banking/how-to-send-money-from-korea-abroad/)
sets out how to model.

Originally foreigner-only, the service opened to Korean nationals in
December 2025 with its own per-transaction cap for that group.

Two cautions. The applicable exchange rate and the available amount vary
by destination country and receiving currency, so the ₩5,000 is the fee,
not the total cost — the rate spread still applies. And a submitted Quick
Send instruction is restricted for cancellation or amendment, so check
the recipient details before you confirm rather than after.

## Woori: the most languages, and features aimed at workers

The draft version of this comparison that circulates online treats Woori
as "the one for students." That undersells it.

Woori's foreigner app is **Woori WON Global**, and it supports
**17 languages** — more than any other Korean bank app. It carries
several remittance routes rather than one: Woori Global Quick, mobile
MoneyGram, WING for Cambodia, Woori ONE overseas remittance (which sends
automatically on receipt of a deposit), direct remittance, and a
UnionPay-based quick transfer to China by recipient name and card number.

More interesting are two services that are not banking at all:

- **Non-face-to-face filing for departure guarantee insurance** — the
  severance-type insurance payout that foreign workers claim on leaving
  Korea, which Woori was first to accept remotely.
- **Alien registration card delivery tracking** — checking the registered
  post status of your ARC inside the banking app.

Neither will appear in a fee comparison. Both are aimed squarely at a
foreign worker's actual administrative year, and that is a legitimate
reason to choose a bank.

The student angle still holds where a university has tied tuition,
student ID and check cards to a particular bank. In that case the
institutional link is worth more than a marginal fee difference, because
it also supplies the clearest possible evidence of what your account is
for.

The mistake worth avoiding is opening accounts at all four "to compare."
Transaction history is what you are building, and splitting it four ways
weakens every record. Limit release, card issuance and later lending all
read the depth of activity at a single bank. Choose one, route salary,
utilities and card spending through it, and add a second account only for
a defined purpose.

- **Remittance limits, designated banks and the real cost of a transfer:**
  [sending money abroad from Korea](/banking/how-to-send-money-from-korea-abroad/)
- **Whether an internet bank should be your main account:**
  [KakaoBank, Toss Bank and K bank for foreigners](/banking/kakaobank-toss-bank-kbank-for-foreigners/)

One point from the last of those is worth repeating here because it
causes the most failed applications: remote identity verification fails on
mismatched records far more often than on document quality. If the English
name on your ARC differs from the name registered at the bank, or the
spacing and order of your passport name differ, or the subscriber details
on your Korean phone do not match, the check fails regardless of which
bank's app you are using. Immediately after an ARC reissue or a change of
status there is also a lag before systems reflect it. Repeating the
attempt does not help — going to a branch with passport, ARC, phone and
proof of employment or enrolment does.

## The part that is not about the bank

There is no single best bank for foreigners in Korea, and the four are
not competing on the same axis. Heavy foreign exchange and a need for
branch support points to Hana. An app-first relationship that later
includes credit points to Shinhan. Repeated fixed-amount transfers home
point to KB's flat fee. Language coverage and worker-facing
administration point to Woori.

But the bank logo is the smallest variable in the outcome. What decides
whether your account works — whether the limits come off, whether a card
is issued, whether a loan is possible in year three — is documentary
evidence of why the account exists, a stable residence status, and enough
accumulated activity in one place for a bank to read.

Choose the bank that fits your year. Then give it everything.
