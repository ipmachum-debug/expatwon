A contract saying ₩3,000,000 a month does not describe a ₩3,000,000 monthly
cost. It describes about **₩3,593,000** — and roughly **₩43,120,000** across a
year.

The gap is not hidden. It is simply on a different set of documents from the
one you signed with the employee.

<p class="hand">Twelve times the monthly salary is not the annual budget.</p>

## The employer side is about 11.44%, not 10%

The old rule of thumb — employer social insurance runs to about a tenth of
salary — was reasonable while the national pension was 9% in total. It moved to
9.5% in 2026, taking the employer share to 4.75%.

| Scheme | Employer share |
|---|---:|
| National pension | 4.75% |
| Health insurance | 3.595% |
| Long-term care | ≈0.4724% |
| Employment insurance | 1.15% |
| Industrial accident | 1.47% average |
| | **≈11.44%** |

The composite is recorded as
[the employer social insurance burden](/tracked/employer-social-insurance-burden/).

Two of those rows deserve their own attention.

<div class="callout callout-note">
  <p class="callout-title">Employment insurance is not symmetrical</p>
  <p>The employee pays 0.9% for job-seeking benefit and the employer matches it —
  then pays a further 0.25% for employment stability and vocational skills
  development at
  <a href="/tracked/employment-insurance-employer-rate/">fewer than 150 employees</a>.
  That component is charged to the employer alone and never appears on a payslip,
  which is why employees are often surprised the employer side is larger. It also
  rises with headcount.</p>
</div>

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">The industrial accident average is a placeholder</p>
  <p>[1.47%](/tracked/industrial-accident-average-rate/) is an average across
  industry classes, and the rate that applies comes from your workplace's own
  industry classification. An office-based business sits well below it;
  construction and manufacturing well above. Use the average to sketch a budget,
  then re-derive it against your classification code before committing.</p>
</div>

And the pension component is capped: contributions are calculated on standard
monthly income within
[a floor and a ceiling](/tracked/pension-standard-income-limits/), so a high
salary does not attract 4.75% without limit. The rate history itself, including
the path beyond 2026, is at
[the national pension rate](/tracked/national-pension-rate/).

## Retirement benefits: accrue 1/12, but know what it is

Statutory retirement benefits arise where an employee completes a year or more
of continuous service averaging 15 hours a week or more — and this applies
**below five employees** as well.

An employer operating a defined contribution plan pays at least one twelfth of
annual wages into the employee's account.

```
monthly salary × 1/12  ≈  8.33%
```

<div class="callout callout-note">
  <p class="callout-title">Accruing it monthly is budgeting, not a rule</p>
  <p>Not every employer must set aside exactly this every month. What makes it
  worth doing is that the liability is being earned monthly whether or not it is
  funded, and a business that has not accrued it meets it in one payment at
  exactly the moment it is losing the person who was doing the work.</p>
</div>

## Do not add annual leave to a first hire

With one employee besides the owner, the business is generally under five
employees — and the statutory annual leave provisions do not apply there.

So unless the contract or workplace rules promise paid leave, there is no
statutory unused-leave liability to accrue, and adding one produces a wrong
number.

What **does** apply below five employees:

<div class="steps">
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">1</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Weekly holiday pay</strong>, where the employee works 15 hours a week or more and meets the conditions.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">2</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Statutory retirement benefits</strong>, on a year of service.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">3</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Minimum wage</strong>, the written contract, and wage payment obligations.</div></div>
</div>

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">And do not add weekly holiday pay twice</p>
  <p>A monthly salary set using the 209-hour conversion already contains the paid
  weekly holiday hours. Adding a separate weekly holiday allowance on top of such
  a salary is double counting — the arithmetic is in
  <a href="/employment/minimum-wage-and-weekly-holiday-pay-korea/">Korea's minimum wage and the 209-hour month</a>.</p>
</div>

## Three salaries, costed

Assuming fewer than 150 employees, the pension ceiling not reached, industrial
accident at the average, one twelfth accrued for retirement benefits, and no
statutory leave liability:

| Gross monthly | Employer insurance ≈11.44% | Retirement accrual 1/12 | Monthly total | Of gross |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| ₩2,500,000 | ₩285,935 | ₩208,333 | **₩2,994,268** | 119.8% |
| ₩3,000,000 | ₩343,122 | ₩250,000 | **₩3,593,122** | 119.8% |
| ₩4,000,000 | ₩457,496 | ₩333,333 | **₩4,790,829** | 119.8% |

So the ₩3,000,000 hire:

```
salary alone, annual        ₩36,000,000
total labour cost, annual   ₩43,117,464
```

Seven million won a year that was never in the offer letter.

## What 120% does not include

The multiplier covers statutory employer costs and a severance accrual. It does
not cover:

- holiday and performance bonuses
- meals or a meal allowance
- recruitment fees
- laptop, software, desk
- training
- overtime, night and holiday premium pay

Nor does it cover what arrives at **five employees**: statutory annual leave, and
the working hours and premium pay framework. Crossing that line changes the cost
structure rather than scaling it.

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">So 120% is a floor, not an answer</p>
  <p>A first hire in a low-risk industry with no bonus and no overtime lands near
  it. Five employees, a higher industrial accident class, a regular bonus and
  unused leave to settle can put the real figure past 125% — and the components
  that push it there are the ones nobody prices at the offer stage.</p>
</div>

## Decide on the annual figure

```
gross monthly × 12
  + employer social insurance, on your own industry rate
  + retirement benefit accrual
  + bonuses actually intended
  + equipment and software
  + recruitment cost
  + expected overtime
  = annual total labour cost
```

That is the number a hiring decision should be made against. The salary is the
part of it the employee sees.

What legitimately reduces it — a national subsidy aimed precisely at a first
hire, and a non-taxable pay component that improves take-home without raising
the gross — is the other half of today's pair. The filing side, and the
deadlines that diverge between the four schemes, is in
[registering employees for the four insurances](/employment/registering-employees-for-four-insurances/).
