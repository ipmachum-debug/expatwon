You registered a business in Korea. Customers pay into the business account.
You pay suppliers, rent and operating costs out of it. At the end of the month
you move some money across to your personal account.

How much of that is taxable?

Korea does not apply a percentage to everything deposited into a business
account, and withdrawing money from one does not create a deductible salary. A
sole proprietorship is not a separate legal person from its owner, so its
business income belongs to the individual running it. The calculation starts
here:

```
business revenue − allowable business expenses = business income
```

Getting those two numbers right takes more than a bank statement. Revenue is
not always the cash received. Inventory purchases are not always expenses of
the same period. Equipment may have to be depreciated. And personal
withdrawals do not become business costs by passing through a business
account.

**The tax system is interested in your business income. Your bank balance is
one piece of evidence about it.**

## It is not corporate tax, and that changes everything

Korea separates the individual business operator (개인사업자) from the
corporation (법인사업자). A corporation is a separate legal person that
calculates corporate taxable income and files corporate tax. A sole
proprietor's business income is attributed to the individual and goes into
that person's comprehensive income tax calculation alongside other income that
must be combined.

You do not become subject to corporate tax by hiring staff, leasing an office
or earning a lot. Registering a trade name does not create legal separation
either.

This is the root of most of the confusion that follows. A company director
taking salary from a corporation is a payment between two persons. A sole
proprietor moving money to a personal account is moving their own funds. In a
banking app those look identical. In tax they are not remotely the same thing.

The structural comparison — liability, retained profit, which to choose — is
[sole proprietor vs corporation in
Korea](/business/sole-proprietor-vs-corporation-korea/). Here the structure is
already chosen.

## Registration is not permission

A foreign national can register a qualifying individual business with the
National Tax Service. Whether immigration law permits the activity is a
separate question, and it belongs before the first sale rather than after the
first return.

F-5 carries broad permission. An E-7 holder considering a side business has to
check whether it falls outside the approved status and workplace. A D-2
student cannot treat an independently run online store as equivalent to
permitted part-time work. The NTS registers business operators for tax
administration; immigration decides whether you may do the work.

Paying tax on income does not retroactively legalise activity outside the
permitted scope of stay — and a valid status does not remove the tax
obligation. The registration process itself is in [registering a business in
Korea as a foreigner](/business/registering-a-business-in-korea-as-a-foreigner/).
This guide assumes both are already in order.

## Revenue is not profit

Take a small online business selling through its own site and several
marketplaces, recording ₩80 million of revenue in a calendar year.

| Item | Annual |
|---|---:|
| Business revenue | ₩80,000,000 |
| Cost of goods sold | ₩32,000,000 |
| Business rent | ₩9,000,000 |
| Utilities and communications | ₩4,000,000 |
| Contracted services | ₩3,000,000 |
| Software and operating services | ₩2,000,000 |
| Other allowable expenses | ₩3,000,000 |
| **Total expenses** | **₩53,000,000** |
| **Business income** | **₩27,000,000** |

₩80 million of revenue, ₩27 million of business income before personal
deductions. Someone selling ₩80 million of products can end up with less
taxable income than a consultant billing ₩45 million with almost no operating
costs. Revenue measures commercial activity; business income measures what is
left after the expenses the law recognises.

Those figures are hypothetical, stated excluding recoverable VAT and assuming
every cost is properly attributable to the year. They are not average margins
for Korean online retailers.

## What makes an expense "necessary"

The Income Tax Act works on necessary expenses (필요경비): connected with
earning the business income, and satisfying the applicable rules. Paying for
something out of a business account is not the test.

Rent for premises genuinely used in the business can qualify. The cost of
goods sold or materials consumed is relevant — though buying inventory does
not automatically expense the whole purchase. Eligible wages, employer costs
and genuine third-party service fees can qualify, provided the underlying
relationship is correctly classified. Business software, business-related
electricity, internet and phone can qualify, with a supportable allocation
where the use is mixed. Travel connected to the business can, though vehicle
costs and the related VAT carry their own restrictions. So can fees for
legitimate accounting and legal services.

None of that is automatic. Amount, purpose, documentation and statutory
restrictions all bear on it. The NTS has said plainly that cash payments can
qualify where they are business-related and properly evidenced, and that using
a card at the weekend does not by itself disqualify an expense. But where a
payment could plausibly be personal, the evidence has to establish that it was
not.

**An expense is defensible because of its purpose and its records, not because
of what it is called in a ledger.**

## Your own withdrawals are not a salary expense

This is where the sole proprietorship differs most sharply from a corporation,
and it is expensive to get wrong.

A proprietor with ₩27 million of business income takes ₩2 million out of the
business account every month — ₩24 million over the year. Does business income
drop to ₩3 million?

No. The proprietor is not an employee of a separate entity. The withdrawal is
an owner's draw. Business income stays at ₩27 million before personal
deductions.

| Transaction | Ordinary treatment |
|---|---|
| Payment to an actual employee | Potential business expense, subject to rules |
| Payment to an independent supplier | Potential business expense |
| Business account → owner's personal account | Owner's draw, not an expense |
| Owner's savings → business account | Capital contribution, not revenue |
| Business pays the owner's private bill | Personal use, not deductible |

Note the last two rows. Money arriving in the business account is not always
revenue, and money leaving is not always an expense. The account records cash
movements; the tax calculation needs those movements classified by what they
actually were. Keep owner transactions apart from supplier payments and
payroll, or the year-end expense record will mislead you even though every
transaction was captured.

## Buying stock is not the same as cost of goods sold

A retailer opens the year with ₩5 million of inventory, buys ₩24 million more,
and finishes holding ₩7 million unsold.

```
opening ₩5,000,000 + purchases ₩24,000,000 − closing ₩7,000,000
  = cost of goods sold ₩22,000,000
```

₩24 million left the bank; ₩22 million is the cost recognised. The remaining
₩7 million of stock may still be an asset rather than an expense of that year.
Returns, impairment and damaged goods can move the figure further.

A retailer who deducts every purchase without accounting for closing stock
understates business income. The same applies to raw materials and work in
progress. A service business with no meaningful inventory has a different
expense structure entirely — the calculation has to follow the actual
activity.

## Equipment is a timing problem, not an expense problem

Buy ₩6 million of equipment used over several years, and the question is not
whether it is a business cost but *when*.

A purchase may create a depreciable asset rather than an immediately
deductible expense. The treatment depends on the asset, its cost, the
depreciation rules and any qualifying special provision. Suppose, purely to
show the shape, that a given asset yields ₩1 million of allowable depreciation
this year:

| | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Cash paid | ₩6,000,000 |
| Illustrative current-year depreciation | ₩1,000,000 |
| Not recognised this year | ₩5,000,000 |

That ₩1 million is invented to demonstrate timing. It is not a statutory rate
and not a claim about any particular asset; other provisions may allow
accelerated treatment in qualifying cases.

The point is the gap. The bank statement records the full ₩6 million. The tax
calculation may spread it. A laptop, a production machine, a delivery vehicle
and a box of pens cannot be assumed to share one treatment.

## VAT and income tax measure different things

A VAT-registered general taxpayer with ₩100 million of sales excluding VAT and
₩60 million of eligible purchases pays ₩10,000,000 − ₩6,000,000 = ₩4,000,000
of VAT. That calculation says nothing complete about income tax.

The business may also have wages, rent and depreciation that matter for income
tax and produce no input-tax credit. Where VAT is recoverable it is generally
excluded from the income and expense figures; where it is not recoverable the
treatment differs. The accounts have to be consistent — do not claim the same
recoverable input VAT as both a credit and an ordinary expense, and do not
assume every transaction visible in Hometax is a deductible business cost.

The VAT regimes, periods and thresholds are in [Korean VAT for small business
owners](/business/korean-vat-for-small-business-owners/).

### The ₩104 million threshold is not an income tax exemption

The NTS identifies ₩104 million as the general annual supply-consideration
threshold for simplified VAT taxpayer status, subject to industry and other
exclusions. That figure has one job, and it is a VAT job.

It is not a rule that a proprietor earning less than ₩104 million pays no
income tax, and it does not decide bookkeeping obligations either. The same
business can sit inside a simplified VAT regime, owe comprehensive income tax,
carry a particular bookkeeping duty and have withholding obligations as an
employer — four sets of rules, one business. **A VAT classification is not a
shortcut to an income tax answer.**

## Simple books or double-entry books

Korea distinguishes simplified bookkeeping (간편장부) from double-entry
bookkeeping (복식부기). The obligation turns on business category and
prior-year revenue, with significant exceptions. The NTS publishes these
principal thresholds:

| Business category | Simplified bookkeeping below |
|---|---:|
| Wholesale, retail and certain others | ₩300 million |
| Manufacturing, accommodation, restaurants and specified industries | ₩150 million |
| Professional, scientific, technical, education and specified services | ₩75 million |

These are not three tiers of one threshold — they are three different
industries. A retailer and a professional-services provider at identical
revenue can have different obligations, and certain regulated professional
businesses are on double-entry regardless of revenue. Newly started businesses
get special treatment, subject to those professional-business exceptions, and
a business running several activities may need a combined calculation. Check
against the actual registration and industry code.

Simplified books let a smaller business record transactions straightforwardly,
with the supporting information attached. Simplified does not mean
undocumented: the duty to retain records and justify claimed expenses survives
intact. Double-entry books record matched debits and credits and support
financial statements, which form part of the filing for taxpayers required to
keep them. A business that crosses its threshold cannot assume a spreadsheet
of totals still satisfies the obligation.

## Keeping no books does not hand you a percentage

Some owners believe that skipping books means the tax office simply assumes an
expense ratio. It does not work that way.

Estimated calculation exists — the simple expense rate (단순경비율) and the
standard expense rate (기준경비율) — but eligibility depends on industry,
revenue and the applicable rules, and the two produce different calculations.
Under the standard rate, major expenses such as purchases, rent and labour are
handled separately under their own evidentiary rules. You do not choose
whichever percentage yields the lowest bill, and estimated filing is not a
penalty-free substitute for a bookkeeping obligation you actually had.

The NTS describes a bookkeeping-related penalty of 20% of the computed tax in
the applicable circumstances, with exceptions for certain small taxpayers and
a different comparison — against a percentage of revenue — for businesses
required to use double-entry books. A loss can also be difficult or impossible
to establish where the required books do not exist.

So an unrecorded cash expense is not deductible because you remember paying
it, and a loss supported only by a spreadsheet assembled after year end may
not satisfy the evidentiary requirements. For a foreign proprietor running
money through Korean and overseas accounts, the problem compounds.

**Bookkeeping is not a document produced in May. It is the evidence
accumulated while the business runs.**

## How long to keep it

The NTS states five years as the ordinary retention period for books and
supporting records, and identifies 15 years in certain cases involving
carryforward losses. Filing the return or receiving a refund does not release
you from it.

Invoices, sales records, purchase records, bank statements and contracts may
all fall inside the period, and other laws can impose their own. Electronic
storage helps only if the information stays accessible and linked — a scanned
receipt with no connection to the transaction, supplier and purpose is worth
less than an indexed record. The test is whether you could reconstruct how the
reported income was calculated if someone later asks.

## Registering a business card collects data, not deductions

Hometax lets an individual business owner register eligible cards so
transaction information is gathered for tax processing — up to 50 cards under
the published system. It saves collection effort. It changes nothing about the
substance of a payment.

Put a personal holiday on a registered card and the transaction appears in the
business-card records. It is still not a business expense. In the other
direction, a legitimate expense may need review if the system has classified
its VAT treatment wrongly — the NTS puts responsibility for confirming input
tax deductibility on the taxpayer. The mechanics are in [registering a
business credit card on
Hometax](/business/registering-a-business-card-on-hometax/).

Card-data collection and legal deductibility are two different questions.

## The full calculation

Back to the ₩80 million business, with ₩53 million of allowable expenses,
₩27 million of business income, no other income to combine, and an assumed
₩3 million of income deductions — an illustrative figure, not an entitlement
every foreign owner has.

```
₩27,000,000 − ₩3,000,000 = ₩24,000,000   taxable base
₩14,000,000 × 6%  = ₩840,000
₩10,000,000 × 15% = ₩1,500,000
                    ₩2,340,000  national income tax before credits
```

The NTS shortcut gives the same answer: ₩24,000,000 × 15% − ₩1,260,000 =
₩2,340,000.

| Item | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Business revenue | ₩80,000,000 |
| Allowable expenses | −₩53,000,000 |
| Business income | ₩27,000,000 |
| Illustrative deductions | −₩3,000,000 |
| Taxable base | ₩24,000,000 |
| National income tax before credits | ₩2,340,000 |
| Illustrative local income tax | ₩234,000 |
| **Illustrative combined** | **₩2,574,000** |

No credits, reductions, prepaid tax or special treatment are assumed, and the
whole business income is taken to fall inside the ordinary comprehensive
calculation. The rate table the NTS currently displays is labelled for the
2023–2025 income years; the rules for 2026 income have to be checked against
the legislation and the 2027 filing guidance. This shows the calculation, not
a promised 2026 result.

## Why the same revenue produces different tax

| | Retailer | Consultant |
|---|---:|---:|
| Annual revenue | ₩100,000,000 | ₩100,000,000 |
| Allowable expenses | ₩75,000,000 | ₩35,000,000 |
| **Business income** | **₩25,000,000** | **₩65,000,000** |

Identical revenue, 25% margin against 65%, and a very different amount
entering the tax calculation. It also shows why bookkeeping thresholds and tax
liability are separate ideas: **revenue decides what records you must keep;
taxable income decides much of what you owe.** Neither number substitutes for
the other.

## You can owe tax without having the cash

A business can report profit and still be short. Customers have not paid.
Inventory is sitting unsold. Money is tied up in receivables and stock.

The income tax calculation is not governed by the bank balance on 31 December.
Revenue and expense recognition follow the accounting and tax rules: a January
receipt may belong to last year's revenue, a customer advance needs analysis
before it is treated as earned, December stock may still be an asset. Repaying
loan principal, buying equipment and taking personal draws all consume cash
without reducing taxable income by the same amount.

That is how a proprietor ends up with a tax bill and a thin account. A
business needs two views — the profit calculation for tax, and the cash flow
to meet obligations. Confusing them is what makes filing season painful.

## November can bring another payment

Most new proprietors expect one annual calculation in May. Korea also runs a
midterm income tax prepayment (종합소득세 중간예납) relating to the first half
of the current year, generally administered through a November assessment,
subject to exclusions.

It is not a second tax on the same profit — the amount paid counts toward the
annual settlement. The NTS lists exceptions including qualifying newly started
businesses, certain income categories and small assessments; its 2026 guidance
puts businesses not yet operating at 31 December 2025 among them, and treats
assessments below ₩500,000 under the small-amount non-collection rule.

That ₩500,000 belongs to the midterm assessment and its own rules. It is not a
general exemption for anyone whose annual income tax is under ₩500,000.

The practical consequence is cash flow. A proprietor who planned around
January VAT and May income tax can be caught by a November assessment landing
next to year-end supplier payments. Put it in the calendar — and do not treat
the prepayment as a deductible business expense. It is a payment toward the
liability.

## Paying other people creates separate obligations

A proprietor who hires may need to withhold income tax from wages, handle
payroll reporting and deal with social insurance. Three relationships have to
stay distinct: payments to employees, payments to genuinely independent
contractors, and the owner's own withdrawals.

An employee's salary can be an allowable labour cost. A payment to a genuine
contractor may carry withholding and reporting duties depending on the income
category. The owner's draw does not become wages because the transfer is
labelled "salary" each month. And the worker's legal classification is not
settled by withholding 3.3% and calling them a contractor — that is covered in
[hiring your first employee in
Korea](/employment/hiring-your-first-employee-in-korea/) and [freelancing in
Korea legally](/business/freelancing-in-korea-legally/).

## If you also have a job

Employment income and separately operated business income can coexist, given
the immigration and other conditions are met. The employer's year-end
settlement may already have dealt with the salary. It has not dealt with the
business income.

Someone with ₩40 million of employment income and ₩15 million of business
income after expenses cannot calculate the business side as though the salary
did not exist. Progressive rates apply to the taxable base, not to each
reported payment as if it were its own bracket. The comprehensive return
brings the relevant amounts together and reflects the settlement and prepaid
tax; which filing obligations follow is in [May filing in Korea: who has to,
and what it costs](/business/may-global-income-tax-filing-for-foreigners/).

The proprietor's job here is to get the business-income component right.

## A foreign customer paying into a foreign account

A Korean-registered proprietor consulting for an overseas client, paid in
dollars to an overseas account, should not assume the absence of a Korean
transfer makes the revenue invisible or exempt. Residence status, where the
activity is performed, the source of the income and any treaty all bear on it,
along with currency conversion and exchange differences.

Nor does the customer's location settle the VAT position — some services and
exports may qualify for zero-rating under specific conditions, which is a
separate question from whether the receipt is taxable business income. Keep
contracts, invoices and payment confirmations that identify the underlying
activity. The residence framework is [income tax for foreigners: resident or
not](/business/income-tax-for-foreigners-resident-or-not/).

The location of the bank account is not a substitute for either analysis.

## If the business loses money

₩50 million of revenue against ₩55 million of allowable expenses is a ₩5
million loss as arithmetic. It is not automatically a refundable credit, and
nobody owes you ₩5 million because the business lost it.

The loss may bear on comprehensive taxable income or qualify for carryforward,
but recognising and using it depends on the activity, the supporting records
and statutory conditions — and the NTS guidance specifically lists inability
to get a loss recognised among the disadvantages of not keeping required
books. Records matter most in the year a business makes nothing, because they
are what establishes that the loss was real.

## Closing does not erase the year

Closing the business mid-year does not remove the need to account for income
earned before it. Revenue, expenses, outstanding invoices, inventory and other
assets all still have to be dealt with, and closing a VAT-taxable business
creates its own final filing obligations, including how remaining stock and
assets are treated.

Do not withdraw the remaining cash and assume it is finished. Unpaid supplier
invoices, outstanding receivables, remaining inventory, equipment, unsettled
payroll and tax relating to the period before closure can all survive the
closure. If you are also leaving Korea, special filing deadlines may apply and
the closure, departure and residence questions have to be looked at together.

**Closing the registration is an administrative event. It does not rewrite the
transactions.**

## What a lender reads is not your balance

A business registration certificate proves the business is registered, not
what it earned. A bank statement shows deposits, not annual profit. A VAT
return supports the level of taxable activity, but it is not the comprehensive
income calculation. Where a lender wants documented income, the income amount
certificate (소득금액증명) is usually the relevant document, and its contents
depend on what was reported and processed; a tax payment certificate does
something else again.

So a business with large revenue and little declared income can present a
different lending profile from one with lower revenue and stronger declared
profit. That is not an argument for inflating income. It is a reason to
understand that aggressive or unsupported expense claims have consequences
past the return itself — the same declared figure gets read later by tax
authorities, lenders and administrative bodies, each for their own reasons.

## The year, not the deadline

| Period | What to review |
|---|---|
| Throughout the year | Sales, expenses, documents, bookkeeping |
| January | VAT where applicable; previous year's records |
| May | Comprehensive income tax for the prior calendar year |
| July | VAT for relevant general taxpayers |
| November | Midterm income tax prepayment, where applicable |
| When paying staff | Withholding, payroll and related reporting |
| On closure | Closing-period tax and asset treatment |

Not an exhaustive list — specific businesses carry extra reporting and
licensing duties, a simplified VAT taxpayer has a different VAT calendar from
a general taxpayer, and some qualified taxpayers have special filing
provisions. The calendar's purpose is to stop one payment being mistaken for
the whole burden.

## Before your first May return

Confirm the **registration details** — name, number, categories, registered
activity. Reconcile **revenue** across invoices, platform settlements, card
payments and deposits. Gather **expense evidence**. Calculate **inventory**:
opening, purchases, closing, cost of goods sold. Identify **fixed assets** that
need depreciation. Separate **owner transactions** from business expenses.
Identify **tax already paid**, including any midterm payment. Establish
**other income** that must be combined. And where relevant, review
**foreign-source questions** — residence, overseas income, foreign tax,
treaties.

That preparation matters more than which form you pick at the last minute. If
the records are wrong, a correctly functioning filing system still produces a
wrong answer.

## The five that distort a return

**Treating every deposit as a sale.** A loan, a capital contribution, a
customer payment and a refund all look alike in a bank feed and are not the
same thing.

**Deducting personal withdrawals.** A transaction label does not convert
living expenses into business costs.

**Expensing all inventory purchases.** Unsold stock may remain an asset.

**Mixing VAT and income tax.** Recoverable VAT is not an ordinary expense, and
a VAT threshold is not an income tax exemption.

**Ignoring the bookkeeping threshold.** It moves with revenue and industry; a
business that qualified for simplified books last year may not this year.

Each one makes reported income diverge from what is actually taxable. Each is
far easier to prevent during the year than to reconstruct after it.

## The bottom line

A sole proprietor's income tax is not the balance in the business account, not
a fixed share of gross revenue, and not a function of how much the owner
withdrew.

It starts with the activity. Revenue identified correctly. Expenses supported.
Inventory and equipment treated properly. The resulting business income then
enters the individual's comprehensive calculation with any other income,
deductions and credits. VAT stays separate. Bookkeeping obligations come from
the statute, not from a preference for spreadsheets. November may bring a
prepayment, and May brings the return. And for a foreign proprietor,
immigration permission and tax residence each have to be established on their
own terms.

**The most expensive misunderstanding is treating the business bank account as
the tax return.** The account tells you where cash moved. The books tell you
why. The return determines how much of the business income is taxable — and
keeping those three distinct is what keeps a growing business legible to its
