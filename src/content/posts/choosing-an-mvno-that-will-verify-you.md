For a Korean phone line, the monthly price is close to the least important
thing about it. If all you need is calls and data, almost any MVNO does that.

What a line actually has to do, if you are staying, is act as an identity key
— for banks, cards, government portals, tax services, shopping and payments.
And on that, MVNOs differ from each other far more than they differ from the
major networks.

## Verification is not just an SMS code

Receiving six digits by text does not necessarily mean you passed identity
verification. A plain SMS check can establish only that you are holding that
number.

**Mobile phone identity verification** compares name, date of birth or
registration number, Korean-or-foreign status, phone number and network
against the operator's subscriber record.

And the designated agencies for it are
[the three network operators](/tracked/phone-identity-verification-agencies/) —
SKT, KT and LG U+.

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">Credit bureaux are not the verification network here</p>
  <p>They are widely described as running it, which sends people to the wrong
  place when it fails. A credit bureau appears in this space as an i-PIN
  verification agency, or as an intermediary a website has chosen. The record you
  are being checked against belongs to your telecom operator — which is where a
  correction has to be made.</p>
</div>

Which is also why an MVNO subscriber is not outside the system: the line runs on
one of those three networks, and verification screens list MVNO options under
each.

## A tourist SIM and a registered line do different jobs

A prepaid SIM or travel eSIM opened on your passport is excellent for getting
connected on day one — calls, data, messages, ordinary one-time codes.

It is generally not the same thing as a line that supports Korean financial and
public identity verification against your registration number.

<div class="callout callout-note">
  <p class="callout-title">Nor is it true that a passport line can never verify anything</p>
  <p>Products differ, and some operators aimed at foreign customers offer limited
  verification, or can amend the subscriber record once your card is issued. The
  reliable structure for long-term use is still the same three things lining up:
  your registration number, a line in your own name, and the operator's customer
  record agreeing with both.</p>
</div>

What each kind of line does and does not open is set out service by service in
[what breaks without a Korean phone number](/cost-of-living/what-breaks-without-a-korean-phone-number/),
and the practical routes into a first number are in
[getting a Korean phone number before your ARC](/cost-of-living/korean-phone-number-before-your-arc/).

## When the card arrives, check the record before buying anything

The instinct is to go and buy a new SIM. The better first step is to find out
what your operator currently holds:

<div class="steps">
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">1</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Is the account registered against your passport or your registration number?</strong></div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">2</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Exactly how is your name stored</strong> — spacing, order, any dropped middle name.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">3</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Can this operator amend it</strong> with your card and passport, or not?</div></div>
</div>

If it can, that is the cheapest path. If it cannot, you need a new post-paid
subscription or a number transfer.

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">There is no single national "ARC update" procedure</p>
  <p>It is an operator-by-operator question, and writing it as one standard step
  — as a lot of guidance does — leaves people waiting for something their
  provider does not offer.</p>
</div>

## Self-activation is not a foreigner feature everywhere

Online self-activation is the headline convenience of Korean MVNOs. It does not
uniformly extend to foreign customers.

kt M mobile, for example, currently excludes foreign nationals from
self-activation and directs them to consultant-assisted signup, offering Korean,
English and Chinese support.

So this sequence is a description of *some* operators:

```
buy a USIM at a convenience store
  → photograph the residence card in the app
  → activated in five minutes
```

Ask first. A consultant-assisted signup is not worse — for a foreign customer it
is often better, because a person is checking the details that later have to
match — but it is a different plan for the afternoon.

## The subsidiary MVNOs are easier for a reason that is not legal

SK 7mobile, kt M mobile and U+ Umobile are easier to deal with because they run
large call centres and standardised processes, which matters when something
needs fixing.

They do **not** hold a higher grade of verification authority than independent
MVNOs. Verification follows the network the line runs on.

So the useful questions are not about corporate ownership:

| Ask about | Not about |
|---|---|
| Post-paid subscription for foreign nationals | Whether it is a subsidiary |
| Mobile identity verification on this line | The brand's reputation |
| PASS support | The monthly price |
| Consultant or self activation | Marketing aimed at foreigners |
| How subscriber details get amended | — |

## Registration and verification are two different checks

An operator supporting foreign-resident-card scanning and face matching at
signup does not thereby guarantee that every financial service will verify you
afterwards.

```
eKYC              confirming who is opening the line
phone verification  an outside service checking an existing line's subscriber record
```

Different purposes, different moments, and passing the first says little about
the second.

## Name mismatch is the common failure, without a statistic attached

Foreign names are stored inconsistently. Registered as `SMITH JOHN DAVID`, a
telecom record may hold:

```
SMITHJOHNDAVID
JOHN DAVID SMITH
SMITH JOHN
SMITH JOHN D
```

Any of which can fail somewhere.

<div class="callout callout-note">
  <p class="callout-title">But it is not the only cause, and nobody has counted</p>
  <p>Claims that some percentage of foreign verification failures come from name
  spacing are invented. The registration number, the Korean-or-foreign flag, which
  network you selected on the screen, and how recently a record was amended all
  produce the same symptom.</p>
</div>

Some Korean systems treat upper and lower case alike but treat different spacing
or ordering as a different string entirely. Field length limits can also silently
drop a middle name.

**Do not fix this by choosing a format you prefer.** Match the official
registration record, and align the English name on your bank account to the same
principle. When it fails, put four things side by side:

<div class="steps">
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">1</div>
    <div class="step-body">Passport name</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">2</div>
    <div class="step-body">Residence card name</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">3</div>
    <div class="step-body">Telecom subscriber name</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">4</div>
    <div class="step-body">Bank customer name</div></div>
</div>

## Before you subscribe, five sentences to a call centre

- Can I subscribe post-paid as a foreign national with a residence card?
- Does mobile identity verification work on this line?
- Can I use the PASS app and a PASS certificate?
- Can the call centre amend my registration details later?
- Can foreign nationals self-activate, or is consultant signup required?

Five answers, one phone call, and they decide more than any price comparison
will.

## And if it is already failing

Do not port to another network first. Check, in order: that you selected the
right network and the MVNO option on the verification screen; that the line is in
your own name; that the registration number is recorded; that the name matches
your registration; that the Korean-or-foreign flag is right; and that PASS
registration itself is possible.

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">After a record is corrected, wait before retrying</p>
  <p>Changes take time to propagate to the external verification systems. Twenty
  consecutive attempts immediately afterwards do not speed it up, and repeated
  failures are their own kind of noise.</p>
</div>

The certificate and mobile ID layer that sits on top of a working line — PASS,
and the mobile resident card — is the other half of this pair. Get the line right
first; everything above it inherits the same subscriber record.
