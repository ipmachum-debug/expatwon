Korean home internet is sold on the monthly price and the cash in your hand at
signup. Neither is the number that decides what it costs you.

What decides it is thirty-six months of payments, minus the bundle discounts
that survive, plus what you owe if you leave before the end — and for a foreign
resident, the odds of reaching that end at all.

<p class="hand">Not "what is the monthly fee". What is the cost of the whole thing, including leaving.</p>

## Three years is common, not compulsory

Carriers publish
[no-contract, one, two and three year prices](/tracked/internet-contract-term-options/)
for the same service, and the monthly charge falls as the term lengthens.

The three-year price is the headline because it is the lowest. But the gap
between it and the no-contract price is the thing to look at, because that gap
is the discount — and the discount is what gets recovered if you leave.

```
no-contract monthly price
  − three-year monthly price
  = what you are being lent, per month, against staying
```

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">Ask for the no-contract price even if you will not take it</p>
  <p>It is rarely volunteered, and without it you cannot see the size of the
  commitment you are making. A salesperson describing three years as simply how
  it works in Korea is describing the market's default, not a rule.</p>
</div>

## The penalty is a clawback, not a fee

The charge for cancelling early is usually a **discount clawback**: you had a
lower price on the basis of a term you did not complete, so part of the benefit
is recovered.

On top of it, separately:

- bundle discount recovery
- installation discount recovery
- equipment costs
- return of signup gifts

<div class="callout callout-note">
  <p class="callout-title">"Your cancellation fee is ₩300,000" is not an answer</p>
  <p>Ask which of those four it contains. They come from different agreements and
  some are with the shop rather than the carrier.</p>
</div>

### The bell curve is a shape, not a formula

The clawback is often described as low early, peaking somewhere in the middle,
then falling towards the end. That shape does appear.

What does not exist is a national rule that it peaks at month 13, 18 or 24. The
calculation varies by discount, elapsed time, product, bundle and the terms in
force when you signed.

**So ask the carrier for today's figure**, in the app or by phone, before
cancelling anything. It is a lookup, not an estimate.

## Signup cash is a different contract

Cash, vouchers and appliances offered at signup come from the shop's promotion,
not from the carrier's tariff. So cancelling early can trigger the carrier's
clawback **and** the shop's gift return, under separate agreements.

The regulator's own review found benefits and out-of-tariff reductions ranging
from [nothing to around ₩910,000](/tracked/internet-signup-gift-dispersion/)
across subscribers.

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">Which means nobody can quote you the legal cap</p>
  <p>There is no single national ceiling to cite. The regulatory concern is
  unequal treatment of users rather than one number, so a shop telling you its
  figure is the maximum permitted — or that a rival's offer must be illegal — is
  negotiating. Equally, a large offer elsewhere is not proof of a scam.</p>
</div>

What to check is not the amount:

<div class="steps">
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">1</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Who pays it</strong> — the carrier or the shop.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">2</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>When</strong>, and in how many instalments.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">3</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>In what form</strong> — cash, voucher, bill credit.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">4</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>What must be returned</strong> if you cancel, and within what period.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">5</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Where it is written down</strong> — an application form or official order, not a message thread.</div></div>
</div>

A shop promising instalments over several months is also asking you to carry the
risk that it is still trading when they fall due.

<div class="callout callout-note">
  <p class="callout-title">"Same-day cash or it is a scam" is too crude</p>
  <p>Official carrier shops run promotions that pay later too. The test is
  whether the channel is legitimate and the terms are documented, not the payment
  date.</p>
</div>

## Speed: buy for how you live

For one or two people doing web, streaming, video calls and ordinary remote
work, 100Mbps or 500Mbps is often enough. Several people streaming 4K at once,
large file transfers, heavy NAS or cloud use — that is where 500Mbps or 1Gbps
earns its price.

"Everyone takes the gigabit now" is a sales line, not a needs assessment.

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">And the maximum is a maximum</p>
  <p>Published speeds are the product's ceiling. Actual speed depends on the
  building's wiring, the in-home cabling, the router, the Wi-Fi standard, your
  devices and how many are connected — KT says as much in its own product terms.
  Paying for a gigabit and keeping an old router or a 100Mbps-rated cable buys
  the number and not the speed.</p>
</div>

## Bundles are a household calculation

Pairing a carrier's mobile service with its internet reduces both bills. The
problem arrives when one line moves.

```
mobile bill falls after switching to an MVNO
  → internet bundle discount disappears
      → household total rises
```

So before moving a line, price the bundle loss alongside the mobile saving. The
line-quality side of that decision — verification, PASS, whether the line does
the identity job at all — is in
[choosing an MVNO that will actually verify you](/cost-of-living/choosing-an-mvno-that-will-verify-you/).

<div class="callout callout-note">
  <p class="callout-title">And MVNO does not always mean no bundle</p>
  <p>Some MVNO plans do bundle with home internet. Not all of them, and not with
  every provider — so check the specific operator and plan rather than assuming
  either way.</p>
</div>

## If your stay is shorter than the term

This is where the standard advice inverts.

LG U+ sells **prepaid home internet to foreign nationals** — no age restriction,
passport only, in
[six or twelve month terms](/tracked/foreigner-prepaid-internet-terms/), with no
contract, no early termination charge and no separate installation fee.

It costs more per month than a discounted three-year deal. Compare it against
the right thing:

| | Three-year contract | Prepaid, 6 or 12 months |
|---|---|---|
| Monthly | Lowest | Higher |
| If you stay three years | Cheaper overall | More expensive overall |
| If you leave at month 14 | Clawback, gift return, equipment | Nothing to settle |
| Settling it from abroad | A real problem | Does not arise |
| Bundling | Available | Not the point of it |

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">The comparison is not monthly against monthly</p>
  <p>It is the prepaid total against the cost of a term you might not serve —
  which includes the part people discount to zero, namely settling a Korean
  contract, returning equipment and closing a billing relationship after you have
  left the country.</p>
</div>

## The 36-month total, written out

Before signing anything, compute this rather than comparing monthly figures:

```
monthly price × months
  − bundle discounts over the term
  + installation and dispatch charges
  + equipment rental
  − signup benefit actually received
  = the real cost of the term
```

Then ask the separate question: **what is the probability I complete it**, and
what does it cost if I do not.

What happens when the answer to that turns out to be "I move" or "I leave
Korea" — relocation, waiver conditions, equipment return and the last month
before a flight — is the other half of today's pair.
