Moving into a Korean apartment, officetel or commercial unit starts a
second administrative relationship, separate from your lease and separate
from the registry. Resident cards, vehicle registration, access cards,
booking the lift for your move, interior work, direct debit for the
monthly fee, and the settlement when you leave — all of it runs through
the management office.

The word is the same on every building. The law behind it is not.

## The office executes. It does not decide

In multi-family housing the two roles are distinct and worth keeping
apart in your head.

The **residents' representative council** is the decision-making body,
made up of representatives elected by building. The **management body**
does the work: maintaining the facilities, charging and collecting the
fee, carrying out the long-term repair plan, safety management. That
management body may be a self-management organisation, or an outside
housing management company appointed to do it.

So "the management office" in everyday speech and the management body in
the statute are not quite the same thing — and when you are told *the
council decided this*, that is a different source of authority from *the
office is doing this*.

Condominium buildings are structured differently. All the unit owners
together form a **management body corporate**, and a **manager** carries
out the actual work. Voting at the general meeting belongs to the unit
owners as a rule, weighted by their share of the exclusively-owned parts
unless the rules say otherwise.

There is an opening for tenants here that apartments do not have: in a
condominium building, an occupant with the owner's consent may in
certain cases exercise the owner's vote. So being a foreign tenant does
not shut you out of every management decision — and it does not give you
the owner's position either.

## The rulebook binds you, and does not outrank the statute

Management rules are not a welcome leaflet. They are made and amended
under the Multi-Family Housing Management Act and the local
authority's model rules, and they can govern use of common areas,
parking, pets, noise, facilities, construction work and how the fee is
apportioned.

Seoul's model rules are written so that provisions on how the managed
property may be used can reach occupants, not only owners. *I am not the
owner, so this does not apply to me* is a weak argument.

The reverse also holds. Rules that conflict with the Act or other
legislation may have no effect in that part. If the office demands
something citing the rulebook, the question is not only what the rulebook
says but whether that measure is within what the law allows.

## Day one: four things worth settling immediately

| Item | Usually asked for | Worth checking |
|---|---|---|
| Resident registration | ID, lease or proof of ownership, contact number | Who actually lives there, emergency contact |
| Vehicle registration | Vehicle registration certificate, resident proof | Base allowance, cost of additional cars |
| Access and parking system | Card or RF tag application | Deposit, replacement cost |
| Moving and construction | Dates, scope of work, contractor details | Lift use, protective covering, permitted hours |

Buildings keep a resident register or resident card of their own.
Foreign residents are typically asked for an Alien Registration Card or a
domestic residence report certificate, together with the lease.

**Parking is where assumptions cost money.** There is no national rule
granting one free space per household. The base number of vehicles and
the monthly charge for additional ones come from the building's
management rules and parking policy, and those follow the number of
units and the spaces available. If you run more than one car, or a
company is taking the unit as staff housing, that is a
before-you-sign question.

## Moving in and building out both need prior agreement

A moving truck or a ladder lift usually means coordinating vehicle
routes and working hours inside the complex. Where a move or a job ties
up a lift for a long stretch, some buildings require protective
covering, a booking, or a fee or deposit. Those amounts are not a
national schedule either — they come from that building's rules and
internal practice.

Interior work needs more care, because two different things get called
renovation:

## Owner and tenant are not the same person here

| | Owner | Tenant / occupant |
|---|---|---|
| Management rules | Bound | Use-related provisions can apply |
| Fees and usage charges | Ultimate responsibility | Pays in practice, per the lease |
| Residents' representative council | Eligible as a building representative if the statutory conditions are met | Not the same standing as an owner |
| Condominium general meeting | Holder of the vote as a rule | May exercise it in certain cases with the owner's consent |
| Long-term repair reserve | Bears it in law | Recoverable on move-out if paid |
| Use of the unit | Within ownership | Within the lease |

That fifth line is the one with money attached, and it has its own
section in
[utilities and maintenance fees](/cost-of-living/utilities-and-maintenance-fees-korea/),
including how to get the certificate you need to claim it.

One distinction to carry across from there: the long-term repair reserve
and the **repair and maintenance charge** are not the same item. The
reserve accumulates for major replacement under the long-term repair
plan and is the owner's in law. The maintenance charge covers ordinary
day-to-day upkeep and is a management fee in character. Asking for
"everything I paid for repairs" back will not survive contact with the
office.

## What the office cannot do

Floor noise, pets, items in corridors, parking disputes — the management
office is usually the first place these land. It can establish the facts,
ask for restraint under the rules, and attempt to mediate between the
parties.

It cannot order compensation or order anyone to leave. Those belong to
courts and administrative bodies, and knowing where the office's
authority stops saves months of complaints that were never going to
resolve anything:

## Defects are a different track from bills

Leaks, cracks, windows, pipework and shared facilities in a newer
building are construction defects, not a billing dispute, and they run
on their own procedure.

Defects in your own unit go from the household to the management office
or the project owner. Defects in common elements are usually gathered by
the management body or the residents' council and handled collectively.

Where there is a genuine dispute — whether it is a defect in law, whether
the warranty period still runs, whether the proposed repair is adequate
— the defect review and dispute mediation procedure under the Ministry
of Land, Infrastructure and Transport is available. Under the current
Act, defect review and mediation is to be handled within 60 days of
application as a rule, and 90 days for common elements. Adjudication
carries longer statutory periods.

Which is why repeatedly raising it with the office and waiting is the
weaker move. Build the record instead: a formal defect submission to the
project owner, photographs, the date it was filed, and what the repair
actually did.

## Getting the fee reviewed means getting the documents first

If a charge looks wrong or unexplained, start with the bill and the
detailed calculation behind it. Multi-family housing has to disclose and
retain certain management records and meeting materials under the Act
and the management rules.

Ask the management body in writing, check it against the rules and the
council's resolutions, and compare your own meter readings and household
registration status before escalating. Where it does not resolve, the
local authority's dispute mediation committee for multi-family housing
management is the route.

**For officetels and commercial buildings that route may not be
available in the same form.** Approach it through the condominium
building's accounting records, its manager and its management rules
instead. For condominium buildings above a certain size of
exclusively-owned area, local authorities can also require the manager
to produce management records — a supervisory route that exists
separately from the apartment system.

## Your resident card is not your immigration filing

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">Two different administrations, and only one of them is the law</p>
  <p>Handing a resident card to the management office does not complete a
  report of change of residence under the Immigration Act. The building's
  registration is that building's internal administration. The immigration
  filing is separate, and it turns on documents proving where you actually
  live — a lease, an accommodation confirmation. Management office paperwork
  can support that proof. It does not replace the filing.</p>
</div>

Three things, collected right after you sign, prevent most of what goes
wrong later: **the building's management rules, a recent fee bill with
the basis it was calculated on, and the office's contact details along
with its move-in and move-out procedures.**

Parking, construction, billing, neighbour disputes and the final
settlement all resolve faster when you already hold those three.
