"Foreigners pay 19%" is not what this provision does.

It lets a qualifying foreign employee elect **19% on Korean employment
income** instead of the ordinary 6–45% scale — 20.9% once local income tax
is included — in exchange for giving up, as a rule, the earned income
deduction, personal deductions, special deductions and most credits.

Which means the decision is not about your salary. It is about **what those
deductions were worth to you.**

Whether you are a Korean tax resident at all, and what that puts in scope,
is the prior question — answered in
[income tax for foreigners: resident or not](/business/income-tax-for-foreigners-resident-or-not/).
This guide assumes that is settled and deals only with the election.

## 19% of income, not of a tax base

The two methods do not differ only in the rate. They differ in **what the
rate is applied to.**

```
Progressive   gross pay
              → earned income deduction
              → other deductions
              → tax base
              → 6–45%
              → tax credits

Flat          employment income
              → 19%
```

Reading the flat rate as "tax base × 19%" is the expensive mistake, because
under the progressive method the base is a good deal smaller than the gross
before any rate touches it.

## What the election gives up

Electing the flat rate sets aside, in principle, the exemptions, deductions,
reductions and tax credits under the Income Tax Act and the Restriction of
Special Taxation Act. That includes the most basic one of all — the
**earned income deduction** — along with:

- basic and dependant personal deductions
- special deductions such as insurance premiums and housing funds
- the credit card usage deduction
- the child tax credit
- medical, education and donation tax credits
- the monthly rent tax credit

<div class="callout callout-note">
  <p class="callout-title">But not literally everything</p>
  <p>The law keeps an exception for certain welfare-type benefits. The benefit
  from employer-provided housing, for example, can remain non-taxable within
  the scope set by decree even where the flat rate is elected. So "every
  exemption disappears" overstates it — check the actual pay components line
  by line rather than assuming.</p>
</div>

## Side by side

| | Flat 19% | Progressive |
|---|---|---|
| Rate | 19% | 6–45% |
| With local income tax | 20.9% | Plus 10% of the income tax |
| Earned income deduction | No | Yes |
| Basic and personal deductions | Excluded in principle | Available |
| Special deductions | Excluded in principle | Available |
| Tax credits | Excluded in principle | Available |
| Exemptions | Excluded in principle, some statutory exceptions | Available on conditions |
| Tends to suit | High income, few deductions | Middle income, many deductions |

Comparing 19% against 24%, 35% or 38% directly is what produces the wrong
answer. Under the progressive method those rates apply **band by band**, not
to the whole salary, and only after the earned income deduction has come
off.

## The modelled crossover

Take a single foreign employee with no dependants, the basic deduction, the
earned income deduction and the earned income tax credit, and no material
card, rent, insurance or donation deductions. Local income tax counted on
both sides.

| Gross salary | Progressive (modelled) | Flat 19% | Better |
|---:|---:|---:|---|
| ₩80,000,000 | ≈ ₩10,210,000 | ₩16,720,000 | Progressive |
| ₩100,000,000 | ≈ ₩15,220,000 | ₩20,900,000 | Progressive |
| ₩120,000,000 | ≈ ₩22,260,000 | ₩25,080,000 | Progressive |
| ₩130,000,000 | ≈ ₩26,360,000 | ₩27,170,000 | Progressive, narrowly |
| **₩135,000,000** | **≈ ₩28,250,000** | **≈ ₩28,220,000** | **Level** |
| ₩140,000,000 | ≈ ₩30,130,000 | ₩29,260,000 | Flat |
| ₩150,000,000 | ≈ ₩33,900,000 | ₩31,350,000 | Flat |
| ₩200,000,000 | ≈ ₩53,820,000 | ₩41,800,000 | Flat |

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">This table is a model, not a rule</p>
  <p>It is the output of the current rate bands, the earned income deduction
  and the earned income tax credit for a single filer with nothing else — so
  it moves whenever any of those change, even though nothing about the flat
  tax itself has moved. It is carried as a computed observation at
  <a href="/tracked/flat-tax-break-even-model/">the modelled break-even</a>
  precisely so it can be recalculated rather than quoted forever.</p>
</div>

What it does establish firmly is a negative: **the frequently repeated claim
that the flat rate starts winning somewhere between ₩70m and ₩100m is
wrong**, and following it costs a mid-career employee millions of won.

## Every deduction pushes the crossover higher

Two employees on ₩150,000,000 can reach opposite conclusions.

**A** is single, no dependants, negligible rent, education, medical or
donation deductions. The flat election starts paying off quickly.

**B** has dependants, qualifying housing-related deductions, and meaningful
medical, education, donation and card deductions. Every one of those reduces
the progressive tax base and none of them touches the flat calculation — so
B's break-even sits far above ₩135m, possibly above any salary B will
realistically earn.

Which is why the decision rule is not a salary threshold at all:

```
Is the tax saved by the flat rate
  >
the value of the exemptions, deductions and credits you give up?
```

Answer that and the salary figure becomes an output rather than an input.

## At the top the gap opens fast

Past roughly ₩150,000,000 the progressive tax base starts occupying the 35%
band and then 38% and above, while the flat calculation stays at 19%.

Modelled, ₩150m favours the flat election by around ₩2.5m; ₩200m by around
₩12m. So for a senior expatriate executive or a highly paid professional
with few Korean deductions, running the comparison is worth real money.

The reverse warning matters just as much: **being in the 35% band is not a
reason on its own.** Middle-income employees frequently sit in a high
marginal band while their average rate stays well below 19%.

## Two applications, two moments

The provision has a payroll layer and an annual layer, and conflating them
causes unnecessary alarm.

<div class="steps">
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">1</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Monthly withholding at 19%</strong> — file the flat-rate withholding application by the <strong>10th day of the month following</strong> the month you first provided work in Korea. It goes through your employer as withholding agent to the head of the competent tax office. Tracked at <a href="/tracked/flat-tax-application-period/">the application deadline</a>.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">2</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>The final election</strong> — at year-end settlement, attach the foreign employee flat-rate application to your deduction and credit return and give it to the withholding agent. On an annual return instead, it goes to the district tax office.</div></div>
</div>

**Missing the first does not forfeit the provision.** It changes your cash
flow through the year — more withheld each month, settled later — but the
final tax position is decided at step two.

## Waiving it takes effect next year

If you have flat-rate withholding running and your circumstances change,
the waiver application takes effect **from the tax period after the one in
which it is filed.**

So telling payroll in June to switch you back from July is not how it works.
Whether the year as a whole ends up on the flat rate or the progressive
scale is settled at year-end settlement or on the return — which is the
argument for modelling both in January rather than discovering the answer in
December.

<div class="callout callout-note">
  <p class="callout-title">One more structural point</p>
  <p>Where the election applies, that employment income is not aggregated into
  the ordinary global income tax base. It is taxed separately at the flat
  rate, which is part of why it behaves so differently from the progressive
  route.</p>
</div>

## What payroll should actually run

Not one calculation in December. Two, in January, refreshed when anything
moves.

| Recalculate when | Because |
|---|---|
| Salary is set for the year | Both cases start here |
| A bonus or equity award lands | It can move you across the crossover |
| Dependants change | Progressive gets cheaper, flat does not |
| Housing or rent deductions change | Same |
| Foreign pay may be Korean employment income | It changes what the 19% applies to |

The band worth watching closely is **₩120,000,000 to ₩150,000,000**, where
the two methods run close enough that a bonus decides it.

The National Tax Service's own year-end settlement service compares flat
against progressive directly. Use it against real payroll figures rather
than any threshold quoted in an article — including the one in this table.

## The decision, restated

The flat rate's value is not simplicity. It is capping the part of a large
salary that would otherwise be taxed at 35% and above.

Its cost is the exemptions, deductions and credits you stop being able to
use.

**Compare those two, not the salary against a threshold.** On a single filer
with nothing to deduct, the model says the line is near ₩135,000,000 — and
your own line is somewhere else, higher, by exactly as much as your
deductions are worth.

cannot deduct under the ordinary method, is in
