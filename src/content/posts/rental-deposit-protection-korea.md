A jeonse deposit is not a bank deposit. Nothing about it is automatically
principal-protected. It is a **claim** — hundreds of millions of won
entrusted to a landlord, recoverable only by demand at the end of the
contract.

Which means a jeonse contract's safety is never judged by the landlord's
demeanor or the agent's reassurance. It is judged in a fixed order: the
priority of rights at signing, the tenant's opposing-power requirements,
the fixed date, the property's realistic disposal value, and whether a
return guarantee will accept the contract.

Foreigners are not outside this system. The Supreme Court has held that a
foreigner who lawfully completes alien registration and reports the
leased home as their place of stay obtains the same public-notice effect
as resident registration under the Housing Lease Protection Act — and
likewise for overseas Koreans' domestic residence reports. An ARC holder
who perfects the requirements holds opposing power and priority repayment
like any Korean tenant. The real question is not whether the rights
exist. It is whether you **completed them at exactly the right time.**

This is the protection companion to
[our jeonse-vs-wolse guide](/cost-of-living/jeonse-vs-wolse-korean-rental-system/) —
that article prices the deposit; this one keeps it recoverable.

## Read the registry before you read the contract

The first document in any jeonse decision is the certified registry
extract (등기사항전부증명서 — the 등기부등본), in three parts:

- **표제부 (heading):** location, structure, area — confirm the contract
  address and the registered property are the same physical object.
- **갑구 (section A):** ownership — is the current owner the landlord on
  the contract, and are there seizures, provisional attachments, or
  auction-commencement entries?
- **을구 (section B):** non-ownership rights — mortgages and registered
  leases. For mortgages, read the **registered maximum secured amount
  (채권최고액) and its priority**, not the landlord's claimed loan
  balance.

Now do the arithmetic. A villa with a ₩500M market price, a senior
mortgage with a ₩240M maximum secured amount, and a ₩250M deposit sums
to ₩490M — 98% of price. Calling that safe "because the house is worth
₩500M" is how deposits die: auctions clear below market, execution costs
come first, and other priority claims may exist. The conservative test:

> **Deposit risk exposure = (senior rights + your deposit) ÷ conservative
> disposal price**

Use a distress price, not the listing price. At an 80% disposal
assumption — ₩400M — the example above is already under water. The 70–80%
figures are not statutory "kkangtong jeonse" thresholds; they are risk
management: **the closer senior debt plus deposit gets to a realistic
disposal price, the thinner your recovery margin.**

And mortgages are not the whole senior universe. In multi-household
buildings (다가구), other tenants' deposits and fixed-date priorities
share the same single collateral — you cannot price only your own unit.
Seizures need their causes examined, and certain tax claims affect
auction distribution, so for large deposits the landlord's tax-arrears
exposure is worth separately confirming. HUG's own underwriting for
detached/multi-household homes demands the building's other lease
contracts and senior deposits for exactly this reason. Big deposit +
villa/multi-household = maximum conservatism.

## Foreigners perfect opposing power through the stay-address report

Basic opposing power arises from **delivery of the home plus resident
registration, effective the following day** — move-in reporting counts
as resident registration. Foreigners, who sit outside the resident
registration system, substitute **alien registration and the
stay-address change report**, which the Supreme Court has expressly
equated in legal effect.

Practically: on the day you pay the balance and physically move in, file
the stay-address change **immediately**. And match the address exactly —
a mismatch between the contract's property description and your reported
address can leave the public notice incomplete. For condominium-type
buildings with per-unit registration, the dong and ho must be precise;
for multi-household buildings with different registry structures,
confirm the reported address matches the actual building designation.

## The fixed date is a different right from opposing power

Do not merge the two concepts:

- **Opposing power (대항력)** lets you assert the lease against a new
  owner if the building changes hands.
- **Priority repayment (우선변제권)** lets you get paid before junior
  creditors in an auction — and it requires the opposing-power
  requirements **plus a fixed date (확정일자)** stamped on the contract,
  under the Act's fixed-date and lease-information provisions.

For a foreign tenant, the working unit is a set of three, done together,
fast:

> **actual move-in + stay-address change report + fixed date**

Signing the contract and wiring the deposit completes none of them.

## The most dangerous hours: between the balance payment and the next day

The Act's peculiar risk is timing: opposing power takes effect **from
the day after** delivery and registration — in practice understood as
00:00 the next day. Registrations at the registry, by contrast, take
effect when made.

So: a tenant pays a ₩300M balance Monday morning, moves in, files the
address change immediately. The landlord, the same Monday, draws a
mortgage and registers it. The tenant's opposing power starts Tuesday;
the mortgage started Monday. That one-day seam is one of the classic
structures exploited in jeonse fraud.

Two practical mitigations:

1. **A special clause**, along these lines: *"The landlord shall not
   create any new mortgage, registered lease, security right, or other
   right impairing the tenant's priority on the property through the day
   after the balance date; on breach, the tenant may rescind, claim
   damages, and demand immediate refund of all sums paid."* This does not
   physically block a registration, and it does not outrank a bank's
   registered lien — but it converts the landlord's behavior into clear
   contractual liability.
2. **Registry checks twice**: immediately before paying the balance, and
   again the next business day, watching for new entries.

## The small-deposit top-priority right is not full protection

Tenants under statutory deposit thresholds get an extra shield: Article 8
grants qualifying small-deposit tenants payment of a defined portion
**ahead of secured creditors**, provided the opposing-power requirements
existed at least before the auction-commencement registration.

As of July 2026, the qualifying deposit ceilings and the top-priority
amounts are:

| Region | Qualifies if deposit ≤ | Top-priority amount up to |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Seoul | ₩165,000,000 | ₩55,000,000 |
| Overconcentration areas, Sejong, Yongin, Hwaseong, Gimpo | ₩145,000,000 | ₩48,000,000 |
| Certain metropolitan cities and designated cities | ₩85,000,000 | ₩28,000,000 |
| Other regions | ₩75,000,000 | ₩25,000,000 |

An overall cap of one-half the housing value also applies. So "small
tenants get their whole deposit first" is false — a Seoul tenant with a
₩100M deposit who qualifies protects only the statutory slice with top
priority. For deposits in the hundreds of millions, the real machinery
remains **priority repayment plus a return guarantee.**

## The HUG guarantee transfers the landlord's credit risk

A deposit-return guarantee works as finance, not fraud detection: if the
landlord fails to repay at the end, the guarantee institution pays the
tenant within the covered terms and pursues the landlord itself. You are
buying a transfer of the landlord's repayment credit risk to an
institution.

As of 2026, HUG's standard guarantee covers deposits up to **₩700M in
the capital region and ₩500M elsewhere**, with application generally
required before half the lease term passes. And foreigners are not
excluded as a class — HUG's current document list explicitly names the
**alien-registration fact certificate and the ARC** for foreign
applicants. With an ARC in hand, the binding constraints are the
contract and the property, not your passport.

## Where the "126% rule" actually comes from

The 126% figure quoted around villa jeonse is **not** an independent
statutory jeonse-price cap. It is arithmetic inside HUG's underwriting:
for some housing types HUG values the home at **140% of the official
assessed price** and applies a **90% collateral-recognition ratio**:

> official price × 140% × 90% = official price × **126%**

Example: an official apartment-assessed price of ₩200M gives a valuation
of ₩280M; at 90% recognition, the underwriting house value is about
**₩252M** — the practical ceiling for deposit plus senior debt, since
HUG requires deposit + senior claims not to exceed housing price × 90%.

But do not apply 126% mechanically to everything: HUG values apartments
and officetels from KB or Korea Real Estate Board market prices first,
falling back to official-price × 140% only when market data is
unavailable — while row houses and multi-family homes lean on the
official-price method. **126% is a derived rule for homes valued by the
official-price method**, not a universal law.

## HUG, HF, and SGI are not interchangeable

Same product category, different doors. HUG currently accepts foreign
tenants. HF's standard Jeonse Jikim guarantee requires, as of 2026,
**all tenants to be Korean nationals** — a contract HUG can accept may
not even be eligible at HF. SGI Seoul Guarantee's deposit-protection
products run yet another set of limits, valuations, and identity
requirements. The move for a foreigner is not "if HUG fails, try HF" —
it is confirming **before signing** which institution actually fits your
nationality, status, housing type, and deposit.

## Guarantee eligibility is a pre-contract condition, not an afterthought

Contract first, guarantee later is a risky sequence: if the valuation or
senior debt kills the application after you have paid, you are an
uninsured creditor with no exit. If the deal assumes a guarantee, verify
the property against the institution's baseline criteria before signing,
and consider a special clause to the effect that *"if enrollment in a
deposit-return guarantee proves impossible without fault of the tenant,
the landlord consents to refund of the down payment and rescission."* No
clause can force an institution's approval — but it pre-defines your
escape when ineligibility surfaces.

## Never move out first: the continuity trap and the lease-registration order

The classic endgame: the lease has expired, the landlord says "you'll
get it when the next tenant arrives," and your new home is already
contracted. Moving out and shifting your address anyway is how tenants
disarm themselves — opposing-power requirements must be **maintained**,
not merely acquired, and the Supreme Court has confirmed the continuity
requirement.

If you must relocate before repayment, the tool is the
**lease-registration order (임차권등기명령)**: after the lease ends with
the deposit unpaid, you apply to the court, and once the registration is
completed your existing opposing power and priority survive your
departure. The discipline is in the sequencing — do not move on the day
you *file*; move after confirming the registration is **completed**.

For foreigners this couples directly to immigration: moving means a
stay-address change report, so if you must leave before repayment, order
the steps as *lease registration completed → relocate → re-file stay
address* — never the reverse.

## With a jeonse loan, the deposit is not only your money

A tenant using [jeonse financing](/loans/jeonse-loan-for-foreigners-explained/)
sits inside a web at maturity: landlord,
tenant, bank, and guarantee institution. The loan does not auto-extend
because the landlord is late, and your bank debt does not vanish because
the landlord defaulted. From two to three months before maturity,
confirm in parallel: the landlord's repayment plan, the loan's extension
terms, and the guarantee's claim-filing conditions. Foreign borrowers add
one more axis — loan terms can be coupled to stay periods, so a visa
renewal landing on top of a lease maturity is a compounding to plan
around, per
[the foreigner lending mechanics in the loans pillar](/loans/can-foreigners-get-a-loan-in-korea/).

## Three defenses, one sequence

Registry analysis, opposing power with a fixed date, and the return
guarantee are not alternatives — they cover different risks:

| Defense | Risk it addresses |
| --- | --- |
| Registry and rights analysis | Senior claims already existing at signing |
| Opposing power + fixed date | Legal priority against third parties and auctions |
| Return guarantee | The landlord's actual failure to repay |

Run them as one sequence. Before contract: verify the owner and senior
rights, and test whether the deposit survives a worst-case disposal
price. On balance day: re-check the registry, complete move-in, the
stay-address report, and the fixed date immediately, with a no-new-liens
clause covering the next-day gap. Then, where possible, transfer the
remaining landlord credit risk to a guarantee institution.

A fixed date does not fix an underwater deal; an available guarantee
does not excuse skipping the registry; a clean registry does not protect
an unreported address. The goal of jeonse safety is not choosing a good
house — it is **constructing a good claim**. The moment you hand a
landlord hundreds of millions of won, you are a creditor as much as a
tenant, and the first numbers to check are never the rent or the
interior. They are the senior debt, the deposit, and the realistic
disposal price.
