Two opposite beliefs about car finance in Korea are both wrong. *"Foreigners
can't get an installment plan"* does not match the market — foreign residents
finance cars here routinely. But *"it works like it does for locals: pick the
car, sign, done"* is not right either.

Car installment financing (할부) evaluates two things at once: the value of a
physical asset and the creditworthiness of the borrower. The lender combines
vehicle price, down payment, expected resale value, your income, employment,
credit file, residence status and remaining stay into a single recovery
calculation:

**approval odds = credit file + verifiable income + stay stability + vehicle
collateral value + down payment − existing debt and recovery risk.**

Two corrections before anything else. First, **six or twelve months of
employment and one year of remaining stay are not statutory requirements** —
they are internal policies that differ by lender and product. Second,
classifying a card company's auto loan as "first-tier" is inaccurate: credit
card companies and capital companies are both **specialized credit finance
companies**, conventionally grouped in the second tier. What separates them is
customer selection and product design, not regulatory class.

## What you are actually financing

The lender pays the dealer; you repay principal and interest over a fixed
term. The financed amount is simply:

> **financing principal = vehicle price − down payment − other amounts paid
> upfront**

A ₩60M car with a ₩20M down payment finances ₩40M. Terms typically run
**12 to 60 months** with equal monthly installments of principal and interest,
and rates are fixed and set by credit score — one major lender's published
new-car band spans roughly **4.7% to 16.9%** depending on the borrower, which
tells you how much the individual file matters relative to the headline
"from" rate in an advertisement.

Which is why comparing monthly payments is the wrong exercise:

> **total car finance cost = down payment + total principal and interest +
> acquisition and registration costs + prepayment costs + other finance fees**

Compare that number against the alternatives in
[the long-term rental vs lease vs buying breakdown](/cars/long-term-car-rental-janggi-rent-korea/).

## The down payment reduces exposure, not just interest

If your file is thin — the common position for a recent arrival — increasing
the down payment is the single most effective lever you control.

What does *not* exist is a published table reading "10% down → X% approval
odds, 30% down → Y%." Lenders weigh the down-payment ratio alongside income,
credit data, the model's resale value, employment and stay status — and
publish none of it.

There is also a limit to the tactic. Cash spent on a down payment is cash you
no longer hold. For a business owner, compare the interest saved against what
that money would earn — or protect — inside the business.

## Balloon (deferred) plans lower the payment, not the price

Captive lenders often offer deferred or residual-value plans: defer, say,
₩20M of a ₩60M car to the end of the contract, and the monthly payment drops
because you are not amortizing the whole principal.

At maturity, though, that deferred principal is still there — to repay,
refinance, or settle by selling the car. The trade is exact:

**lower monthly payment ↔ larger balance at maturity.**

If you intend to keep the car long term, compare total interest against a
standard equal-installment plan. If you plan to replace it in three or four
years, compare the deferred balance against the car's likely resale value —
because if the market value lands below the balloon, the shortfall comes out
of your pocket in cash.

## Captive finance: read the discount you are giving up

A captive finance company is tied to a manufacturer, and its job is to sell
cars as much as to earn interest. That is how a 2.9% or even 0% offer appears
in a market where ordinary rates sit at 7–9%.

The catch is rarely hidden — it is just easy to ignore. Promotional rates are
often tied to manufacturer support, and taking them can mean forgoing a cash
purchase discount or another promotion. So compare like this:

> **effective vehicle cost = actual vehicle price − discounts forgone + total
> finance cost**

A 0% plan on a car you could have bought for ₩3M less in cash is a 0% plan
that costs ₩3M.

## The lien, the stamp duty, and who pays what

Lenders can register a lien (근저당) over the vehicle so they can sell it to
recover the debt if you default. But "a car loan always means a lien for 100%
of the vehicle price" is not a rule — whether a lien is registered, and for
how much, varies with the product, your credit, the vehicle's value and the
down payment.

## The visa is stay-stability data, not an approval grade

F-5 permanent residency reads as low departure risk, which makes long-dated
receivables easy to assess. F-6, F-4 and F-2 often signal long-term
settlement. E-7 professionals with a Korean employment contract and verifiable
payroll are perfectly financeable customers.

None of that supports a table like *F-5 = automatic approval, E-7 = 20% down,
E-9 = refused*. Auto lenders' foreigner underwriting rules are internal credit
policy; they do not publish approval rates or fixed down-payment ratios by
visa. Statuses with more constrained income or stay structures — D-2, D-4,
E-9 — may face more conservative assessment of long-dated debt, but no visa
is legally barred from car finance.

What matters is not the visa's name but **the income and stay continuity that
visa lets you document**.

## An ARC gets you considered; documents get you a limit

Your Alien Registration Card establishes identity. Repayment capacity is a
separate proof.

For employees, the strong file is the familiar one: withholding tax receipts,
income certificates, employment certificates, health-insurance enrollment
records, and salary deposit history. For the self-employed, a business
registration certificate alone is thin — what counts is the income
certificate, VAT filings and actual revenue records.

Earn ₩8M a month in cash with no Korean tax record and, to a lender, that
income does not exist. The same
[declared-income logic](/loans/personal-credit-loan-requirements-korea/) that
governs credit loans governs car finance.

## Remaining stay and loan term do not have to match

"Your visa has a year left, so the loan maxes out at a year" is not a market
rule. Some lenders do weigh remaining stay heavily and shorten terms; others
look further.

If **remaining stay ≥ loan term** were a universal requirement, E-7 holders
who renew and live in Korea for a decade could never structurally access 48-
or 60-month finance. In practice lenders assess the visa's nature, your
employment, renewal history and total time in Korea alongside the expiry date.

So ten months of remaining stay does not legally block a 36-month plan — and
F-5 does not automatically unlock 60 months.

## The DSR distinction that most guides get wrong

This is the correction worth internalizing.

  the exception items in the borrower-level DSR calculation</strong>. So do
  not take a ₩50M car loan's annual payments, drop them into the 40% DSR
  formula, and conclude your future mortgage limit fell by that much.</p>
  <p><strong>But</strong> "excluded from the regulatory formula" is not
  "invisible to the bank." Car finance is a credit transaction: it sits in
  your credit file, and banks assess total debt, monthly cash flow, bureau
  data and their own scoring when they review a mortgage.</p>

Keep the two questions separate — *is it in the regulatory DSR?* and *will it
affect my next housing loan?* The first has a clean answer; the second is
underwriting judgment. Both are unpacked in
[the DSR, LTV and stress-DSR hub](/loans/dsr-ltv-korea-lending-rules-explained/).

## No one can tell you how many points your score will drop

"A capital company loan costs 30 KCB points; a card company auto loan costs
10" is invented precision. NICE and KCB weigh the new debt amount, your total
debt level, the form of the credit transaction, rates and repayment history
together. The same ₩40M car loan lands differently on a clean, established
file than on one already carrying several credit lines.

What matters after drawdown is repayment. A delinquency does not stay in the
car-loan column: it propagates to cards, credit lines and housing finance —
the mechanism described in
[the credit-score guide](/loans/korean-credit-score-for-foreigners-nice-kcb/).

## If you might sell early, the prepayment terms outrank the rate

Car finance runs three to five years, but foreign residents frequently hold
the car for far less — a return home, a status change, a job move. Then the
prepayment terms decide the cost.

With published fees running up to about 2%, calculated on remaining term, a
plan you will exit after 18 months can easily cost more under a "cheaper"
rate with a stiff prepayment clause than under a slightly higher rate with a
soft one. Ask for the prepayment formula, in writing, before you sign.

## Sort the financing before you choose the car

The worst sequence is paying a vehicle deposit and *then* investigating
finance — especially for imported or used cars, where approval terms diverge
most from expectations. Used-car limits in particular are set by the
vehicle's market value and age as well as your credit, so the approved amount
can land well below the asking price.

## Borrow what makes sense, not what was approved

Suppose a lender approves 100% financing on an ₩80M car. That is permission,
not advice.

If your cash earns less than the finance rate and your emergency reserve is
intact, a larger down payment is usually rational. If the rate is 3% and the
same cash funds working capital returning far more, keeping the cash is the
better capital allocation. The decision rule:

> **after-tax, opportunity-adjusted car TCO = acquisition cost + total finance
> cost + running costs − expected resale value − benefit of retained capital**

Installment finance is a way to buy a car. It is not a way to make the car
cheaper. Access is genuinely open to foreign residents — but the right move is
not accepting a heavy down payment or a high rate simply because it gets
approved. It is treating your **stay plan, declared income, holding period
and future housing finance as one capital-allocation problem.**
