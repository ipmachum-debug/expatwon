Korea's transport payment is unusually good once you are inside it, and the
card that gets you inside has one property nothing else in Korean payments
does: **you can get it on your first day with no identification, no account
and no application.**

What confuses visitors is not the card. It is that the price, the top-up rules
and the refund route each work differently from the equivalents at home.

## Buying it

Standard physical T-money is sold at GS25, CU, 7-Eleven and emart24, and at
station machines. No identification, no account, no registration — pay and use
it.

The card costs
[around ₩4,000](/tracked/tmoney-card-price/), varying by design; character and
collaboration editions cost more and work identically.

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">The card price is not a deposit</p>
  <p>Several countries issue transport cards against a refundable deposit, and
  visitors reasonably assume Korea does the same. It does not. You are buying
  the card and separately loading value onto it, and only the loaded value comes
  back.</p>
</div>

## It is not only a Seoul subway card

T-money works on a large proportion of buses and subways nationwide, and at
taxis and shops with a T-money reader — convenience stores among them, which
makes it a useful small-payment card in exactly the places a foreign card is
weakest.

That said, "everywhere" is too strong. Regional networks, individual taxis and
particular shops differ, so read the reader rather than assuming national
coverage.

## Topping up: cash is the answer that always works

For a standard physical card, plan on **won in cash**. Hand the card over at a
convenience store counter and say the amount, or use a station top-up machine —
T-money's own guidance notes that some machines are cash-only.

<div class="callout callout-note">
  <p class="callout-title">But "Korean transport cards are cash-only" is now too strong</p>
  <p>Since 17 March 2026 Seoul has operated
  <a href="/tracked/seoul-transit-foreign-card-machines/">440 new ticket machines across 273 stations</a>
  on lines 1 to 8 that take overseas-issued Visa and Mastercard for single
  journeys and the short-term Climate Card, at an average
  <a href="/tracked/seoul-transit-foreign-card-fee/">3.7% service fee</a>. That is
  a different product from topping up an ordinary T-money card, and it does not
  extend to ordinary top-up machines or to other operators. Four things —
  standard T-money, the Climate Card, mobile T-money and visitor cards — have
  four sets of top-up rules, and collapsing them into one is where the bad advice
  comes from.</p>
</div>

## Payment balance and transport balance are separate

Cards that combine payment with transport — the WOWPASS family among them —
keep **two balances**, and this catches people at the gate.

```
payment balance   spends at shops
transport balance pays bus and subway fares
                  ── not the same pot ──
```

A well-funded payment balance does not pay a fare. WOWPASS takes top-up from
[overseas Visa, Mastercard and JCB](/tracked/wowpass-topup-networks/) and spends
as a domestic card, which is genuinely useful where a foreign card is refused —
but the transport side is loaded separately.

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">Treat product specifics as perishable</p>
  <p>Top-up methods, fees, supported networks and limits on visitor cards change
  frequently. Confirm in the app or on the card's own site rather than from an
  article — this one included. What is stable is the structure: two balances, and
  a card price that is not a deposit.</p>
</div>

## Running out at the gate

Prepaid means no credit assessment and easy spending control, and it also means
an empty balance stops you at the barrier. For a visitor that is a minor
annoyance; for a resident commuting daily it is the recurring cost of the
prepaid model, and the reason to move to a post-paid transit function once a
Korean account exists —
[post-paid transport cards for foreigners](/banking/postpaid-transportation-cards-for-foreigners/).

## Refunds, and why the amount decides the counter

Leftover balance can be refunded at convenience stores and station points, less
a handling fee of [about ₩500](/tracked/tmoney-refund-fee/).

The part that is not uniform is **how much a given channel can pay out**. Small
balances are straightforward at a convenience store; larger ones may need a
station office or a designated centre, and physical and mobile cards follow
different procedures.

<div class="callout callout-note">
  <p class="callout-title">Solve it at top-up, not at refund</p>
  <p>Load what the next few days actually need rather than a comfortable
  cushion. Do that and the last-morning question — which counter can refund
  ₩40,000, and is it open before my flight — never comes up.</p>
</div>

## A worked example

```
card                ₩4,000    not refundable
topped up          ₩30,000
spent              ₩22,000
remaining           ₩8,000
refund fee            ₩500
back in hand        ₩7,500
```

Total cost of the card as an object: ₩4,000 plus ₩500. Reasonable for two
weeks of frictionless transport, and not what someone expecting ₩12,000 back
has budgeted for.

## Which to carry, by stage

| Stage | Sensible choice |
|---|---|
| Arrival, before a Korean account | Standard T-money, cash top-up |
| Short trip with shopping | A visitor travel card, or a payment card with a transport balance |
| Concentrated travel inside Seoul | Compare a short-term Climate Card |
| After foreign registration and a Korean account | A domestic card's post-paid transit function |

Where foreign cards do and do not work for everything else you pay for is in
[paying in Korea with a foreign card](/banking/paying-in-korea-with-a-foreign-card/).

Once you are registered, banked and commuting daily, there is also a government
refund on transport spending that foreign residents are widely assumed to be
excluded from and are not — the subject of the afternoon guide.
