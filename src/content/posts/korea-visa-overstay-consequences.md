Your residence card expired yesterday. You notice this morning. One day.

The question that follows is always the same one, and the answer is the part
people do not want: Korea's Immigration Act does not create a general
several-day grace period for remaining after the authorised stay simply
because the delay was short.

Your explanation may be a good one. The delay may have been a genuine mistake,
after years of lawful residence. Those facts can matter a great deal to how
immigration handles it. They do not change the first question, which is
whether you were still here after the permitted period ended without the
required extension.

<p class="hand">The extension belongs before the expiry date, not after it.</p>

## The appointment is not the application

A continuing stay requires an extension obtained before the existing period
ends, and the decree likewise requires the application to be submitted before
expiry. So the sequence is: expiry approaching → file through an authorised
route → immigration processes it. Not: expiry passes → a few days appear in
which to fix it.

Which makes the reservation system genuinely dangerous to misread. A visit
reservation selects the date and time to attend an immigration office; you
then attend and submit the petition. If your period ends on 20 November and
the earliest slot is 25 November, those five days are not covered by having
booked the 25th.

This is exactly when slots being full stops being an inconvenience and becomes
the problem itself — the
[trouble with booking a stay extension](/visas/hikorea-stay-extension-booking-problems/)
is worth solving before the deadline rather than after.

## The schedule, and what it is not

| Overstay period | Base monetary amount |
| --- | --- |
| Under 1 month | {{fig:overstay-penalty-under-one-month}} |
| 1 to under 3 months | ₩3,000,000 |
| 3 to under 6 months | ₩4,000,000 |
| 6 months to under 1 year | ₩7,000,000 |
| 1 to under 2 years | ₩10,000,000 |
| 2 to under 3 years | ₩15,000,000 |
| 3 to under 5 years | ₩20,000,000 |
| 5 to under 7 years | ₩25,000,000 |
| 7 years or more | ₩30,000,000 |

Note what the first row does and does not say. The bracket begins below one
month; there is no line reading *one to three days: nothing*. Equally, it is
an assessment framework rather than an invoice — the rule permits adjustment
within half the standard amount for age, circumstances, the reason for the
violation, its consequences, ability to pay and prior violations, with
exceptional treatment available in specified cases.

So: *the current base bracket for an overstay under one month is ₩2 million,
subject to adjustment*. Not *every short overstay costs exactly ₩2 million*,
and not *short overstays are free*.

## Three things that are not the same

The Act contains a **criminal ceiling** — up to three years' imprisonment or a
fine of up to ₩30 million for specified violations including art. 17(1).
Separately there is the **통고처분** system, under which an immigration office
that establishes an offence may issue a notice requiring payment of an amount
equivalent to a fine. And separately again, there is the **scheduled
assessment** above.

A tourist who finds a one-day overstay is not thereby facing a prison
sentence. But the ceiling is real and belongs in a different sentence from the
schedule, and collapsing the three into one is how both panic and complacency
get manufactured.

## Paying does not restore anything

This is the point to hold on to if you want to stay. Money addresses the
violation in the manner immigration determines. It does not extend the expired
status, approve a new one, continue employment permission or guarantee
re-entry after departure.

And removal remains legally available: art. 46 includes art. 17 violations
among the grounds on which a foreign national **may** be forcibly removed.
*May* is doing real work there — it is not automatic for every short overstay,
and it is also not switched off by payment. Anyone saying *pay the fine and
they can't deport you* is describing a rule that does not exist.

A **departure order** under art. 68 — available where a person falls within a
removal category but intends to leave voluntarily at their own expense — is
also not the same disposition as forced removal. Get the actual notice and
read what it says, because the distinction matters later, whenever you have to
account for your immigration history.

## What to do when it is one day

Confirm the real expiry date from the official record rather than a photograph
of an old card, and make sure you are not confusing passport expiry,
residence-card validity, visa-sticker validity and period of stay — four dates
that routinely get mistaken for one another.

Then establish whether an application was actually filed before expiry: an
electronic petition submitted, an in-person application received, a receipt in
hand. A reservation is none of those.

Then contact immigration immediately, on 1345 or at the competent office, with
the exact expiry date, the reason for the delay, your current situation,
whether you intend to stay or leave, and whether anything had already been
filed. Preserve evidence of any exceptional circumstance while you do —
hospital admission and discharge records, medical certificates, dates showing
you could not attend or travel. A hospital bracelet is not a residence permit,
but the circumstances of a violation are something the rule expressly allows
the authority to weigh.

Then follow the disposition you are actually given. Do not invent a cure.

## Why waiting is the expensive choice

Noticing at two months puts the case in the ₩3 million bracket; at seven
months, ₩7 million, with the next threshold at one year. The brackets are the
visible cost. The invisible one is that a longer violation reshapes the
immigration history you will be explaining at every later extension, status
change or review.

If a genuine obstacle prevents departure, the Enforcement Rule has a procedure
for an extension for departure purposes where, for instance, no means of
departure is available or another unavoidable reason applies. The operative
word is *application* — an emergency does not extend a stay by itself, so
contact immigration before the period ends wherever that is possible.

Two more things, while the status is uncertain. Work authorisation depends on
immigration status, so do not assume previous work rights simply continue
while the problem is unresolved; one issue becoming several is the usual way
this gets worse. And when a later form asks about violations, departure
orders, penalties or immigration history, answer accurately — paying does not
erase the event, and a false statement on a later filing is a second and
heavier problem.

<p class="hand">"How much is the fine?" is the seventh question, not the first.</p>

How long, whether anything was filed before expiry, what status you hold now,
whether you intend to stay or go, what disposition immigration makes, what
amount applies, and what restores or ends the stay lawfully — in that order.
