You fill a black plastic bag, leave it outside, and the next morning it is
still there. Or you buy the official bag, move across town, and find another
district's name printed on it. Or you leave a sofa next to the recycling bins,
because it obviously will not fit in a bag, and a week later it is exactly
where you put it.

None of that is one unusually complicated national rule. Korea has a national
pay-as-you-throw principle and a great many local implementations of it, and
almost everything you actually need to know is decided at the level of your
district rather than your city.

## The same bag, two prices, one city

Incheon publishes a citywide table, and as of July 2026 a 20-litre
general-waste bag costs {{fig:incheon-20l-waste-bag-price}} depending on the
district: ₩620 in Jemulpo, Yeongjong, Seohae and Geomdan; ₩750 in Michuhol;
₩870 in Yeonsu, Namdong, Bupyeong and Gyeyang.

On one bag that difference is nothing. As evidence it is the whole article —
there is no national price, because the city itself tells residents the bags
vary by district and to check before buying. Seoul works the same way, routing
household-waste charges and bulky-waste fees through borough systems.

So an article that tells you a Korean bin bag costs X has already skipped the
only question that determines the answer.

## General waste, and the move problem

Ordinary household waste that is neither recyclable nor food goes in the
official bag, bought locally, and then into the designated place at the
designated time. Apartments usually have a dedicated area; villas, detached
houses and small multi-unit buildings often use neighbourhood points or the
kerb. Collection schedules vary too — in Seoul, contractors and
responsibilities can differ not only by borough but by administrative
neighbourhood inside it. "Collected every night" is not a safe assumption
anywhere.

Which creates a small, very common problem: you buy twenty bags, then move,
and the printing no longer matches your address. Local rules can address what
happens to leftovers — Incheon, for one, gives guidance for residents moving
between areas. So do not bin them and do not assume they still work. Ask in
the new district.

It is the same pattern as everything else in
[moving in Korea](/cost-of-living/moving-in-korea-the-filings-and-their-deadlines/):
the household did not change, the administrative system under it did.

## Food waste is a recycling input

Because much of it becomes feed or compost, the stream is defined by material,
not by whether something was once food. Commonly excluded: hard fruit pits,
nut shells, bones and feathers, shellfish and crab or lobster shells, tea
bags, some tough vegetable matter. Moisture is meant to be drained first —
Seoul gives the same advice about removing non-food objects and water.

The test is not *could a person eat this*. It is *does my local food-waste
system accept this material*.

And the charging method is a third variable. Dedicated bags, dedicated
containers with a paid tag, or RFID machines that weigh what you put in —
Incheon uses all three depending on district and housing type, with machines
in apartment complexes running
{{fig:food-waste-rfid-rate}}. Neither the bag nor the machine is the Korean
way. Both are local implementations of the same principle, which is why your
neighbour's instructions may be confidently wrong for your building.

## The building operates the system

The district publishes the legal framework; your complex runs the daily
routine — a recycling room, collection hours, a food-waste machine, separate
bins for glass, cans, PET and paper, battery boxes, styrofoam rules, a corner
for waste electronics.

That is not the building inventing environmental law. It is the building
operating the local collection system, which makes the management office a
better source than any article for *where exactly does this go tonight*.

## Recycling is where the money is saved

Paper, glass, cans, plastic, PET, vinyl and the rest come out of the paid
stream, and specified recyclables must be separately collected.

But the symbol on the object does not survive contamination. An oily
container, a vinyl bag covered in food, a composite item that cannot
practically be separated — those may be handled differently from a clean one.
Empty it, rinse where required, remove foreign material, separate materials,
follow your building's method. The incentive is direct: everything correctly
recycled is volume that does not have to fit into a bag you paid for.

## A sofa is a registration, not a bag

Furniture and other items too large for a bag are 대형폐기물 — bulky waste.
Sofas, beds, mattresses, desks, wardrobes, tables, large chairs, some
appliances.

The shape of the process is consistent even where the details are not:
identify the responsible district, register the item, select the item or size,
pay, receive a registration number or label, display it as instructed, put the
item out on the designated date. In Incheon that runs through a district
office, a designated seller or the district's online system; Seoul directs
residents to the borough or a community service centre and states outright
that fees differ by borough.

Charging is by item, dimension or category, so *how much does it cost to throw
away furniture in Korea* has no useful answer. A single chair, a three-seat
sofa, a mattress, a wardrobe and a dining table are different entries, and the
same object can carry different charges under different district ordinances.
Check the price before you carry it downstairs.

## Two things that are not bulky waste

**Large appliances.** Do not reflexively pay a furniture fee for a fridge,
television or washing machine — recycling-centre collection can apply, and
small electronics often have their own free channel. Identify the category
before paying. Large does not mean the paid furniture route is correct.

**Renovation debris.** Tiles, bricks, bathroom fixtures and construction
rubble are not household rubbish in larger quantities. Seoul separates bulky
items, recyclables, combustible waste and non-combustible material such as
glass, tile, brick and stone, with special sacks for small quantities of the
last and direct or contracted disposal above that. Do not pack a renovation
into household bags.

## Five questions after moving in

Which official general-waste bag applies to this address, and where is it
sold. How does this building handle food waste — bag, container, tag or
machine. Where and when do recyclables go. How is bulky waste registered in
this district. And which items have a separate channel: appliances,
batteries, fluorescent lamps, electronics.

Move from Yeonsu-gu to Seohae-gu and the bag changes, the price drops from
₩870 to ₩620, and the food-waste method may change with the building. Nobody
left the country and no national law moved. That is why the search worth
running is not *how do I throw away trash in Korea* but *how do I throw away
this item at this address*.
