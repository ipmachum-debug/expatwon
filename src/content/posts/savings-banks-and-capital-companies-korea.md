The cost of a second-tier loan in Korea is not the contract rate. Savings
banks and capital companies accept risk that banks decline, and they
price that risk — but the borrower repays it twice: once in interest,
and again later in refinancing options, DSR headroom, credit
assessment, and the price of the *next* loan.

Equally, the folklore in the other direction is wrong. *"Touch a savings
bank and your score collapses by tens of points and banks lock you out
forever"* is not how the system works. NICE and KCB do not judge you by
lender sector alone; they evaluate debt size, rate levels, repayment
history, product form and usage behavior — and the actual approval is
then decided by each lender's own credit scoring system. KCB says as
much: the bureau score is a reference indicator, while approval, limit
and rate come from the lender's internal model combined with income and
employment data.

So the useful question is not *"is second-tier bad?"* It is: **what does
this loan cost in total, and how do I get out of it?**

## Savings banks and capital companies are not the same animal

They get lumped together as "제2금융권," but their funding differs, and
funding drives price.

**Savings banks** take deposits. They lend from that deposit base and
run their own scoring models aimed at mid- and low-credit borrowers.

**Capital companies** (여신전문금융회사) cannot take deposits. They fund
themselves in the market — corporate bonds, specialized finance bonds,
borrowings from financial institutions — so their loan pricing moves
with their own credit rating, funding spreads, and market liquidity, not
just the base rate. Auto installment loans, leases, personal credit
loans and corporate finance are their core.

For a foreigner, thin domestic credit history, a bounded stay period and
the recovery difficulty if you leave Korea all enter the lender's risk
view. What does *not* exist is a legal "foreigner surcharge" — no
statutory add-on applies because of nationality. Pricing is always:

**funding cost + operating cost + expected loss + capital cost + target
margin**, combined with your NICE/KCB profile and the lender's internal
risk grade.

Then add what the rate does not include:

**Total cost of borrowing = interest + mandatory fees + prepayment costs
+ collateral-related costs.**

Annualize that against principal and term and you approach a true
effective rate. Korean product disclosures do not always present a
single US-style APR, so treat "the rate" on a term sheet as one
component, not the answer.

## The prepayment fee is far smaller than the folklore says

English guides still warn about "up to 2% prepayment penalties." That
number is out of date.

<div class="callout callout-note">
  <p class="callout-title">📌 What changed in 2025</p>
  <p>From <strong>January 13, 2025</strong>, prepayment fees on new loans
  may only recover the lender's <strong>actual costs</strong>. Disclosed
  averages fell across the board — savings-bank variable-rate credit
  loans to about <strong>1.33%</strong>, for instance. Under the
  Financial Consumer Protection Act these fees are <em>prohibited in
  principle</em>, with an exception for repayment within three years of
  drawdown, and each lender's current rate is published through its
  industry association.</p>
</div>

Roughly, what you pay looks like:

**prepayment fee ≈ amount repaid × applicable rate × remaining-term
ratio** — but the exact formula and waiver point live in your contract.

This matters for refinancing math. Moving from 14% to 9% is not
automatically a win: add the old loan's prepayment cost and the new
loan's setup costs, then check whether the interest saved over your
realistic horizon exceeds them. Korea's loan-switching infrastructure is
built to compare exactly that — net interest saved including prepayment
costs — rather than headline rates.

## Stamp duty and lien costs are not automatically all yours

Secured lending brings stamp duty, mortgage registration costs, and
appraisal fees. Stamp duty is tiered by loan size and is commonly
**split between lender and borrower** — the FSC has published a 50/50
split for specialized-finance lending. Registration tax, judicial
scrivener fees, national housing bonds, appraisal and lien-release costs
are likewise not all borne by one party by default.

So "second-tier lenders dump every fee on the customer" is not a fact —
it is an assumption. The product disclosure statement names **who pays
what**, line by line. Read that page; it is where the surprises live.

## Auto finance: a loan and a lease are different cost objects

Using a capital company does not mean one cost structure. An auto loan
or installment plan is credit. An operating or financial **lease** has
its own economics: residual value, early-termination charges, transfer
fees, and return settlement.

Excess-mileage charges and damage-based depreciation at return are
**lease/rental settlement costs**, not hidden loan fees. Folding them
into "capital company loan charges" produces nonsense comparisons —
price them separately, as in
[the rental vs lease vs installment TCO breakdown](/cars/long-term-car-rental-janggi-rent-korea/).

## There is no fixed point penalty for using a second-tier lender

This deserves precision, because it is the single most repeated error in
English-language advice.

NICE and KCB publish no table reading "savings bank loan −50, capital
loan −30." A personal credit score aggregates loan amounts, repayment
history, product and rate profile, debt level and credit-usage form —
KCB describes its 1–1,000 score as a statistical estimate of serious
delinquency within the next year. NICE tracks lender sector and product
type as *information*, but publishes no fixed sector deduction.

The accurate statement is therefore:

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">⚠️ The real mechanism</p>
  <p>It is not the lender's tier that moves your score — it is the
  substance: more high-rate unsecured debt and a larger total burden
  raise your measured risk. That can lower NICE/KCB scores and make
  lenders' internal models more conservative. Same loan, smaller amount,
  clean repayment: much smaller effect.</p>
</div>

## The expensive part arrives at your *next* loan

The largest indirect cost of second-tier borrowing is what it does to
future credit — above all through DSR.

Take a bank-tier borrower earning ₩80M. At 40% DSR, total allowable
annual debt service is roughly ₩32M. If existing credit-loan payments
consume ₩10M of that, a future mortgage gets ₩22M — and the
[stress-rate calculation](/loans/dsr-ltv-korea-lending-rules-explained/)
shrinks the principal that ₩22M supports. So the long-run cost stacks up
as:

**high interest + elevated credit risk + reduced DSR headroom +
worse bank pricing later.**

If a home purchase or a
[jeonse loan](/loans/jeonse-loan-for-foreigners-explained/) is anywhere
in your two-year plan, borrowing to your maximum today is how you find
your housing limit collapsed tomorrow.

## Refinancing upward is normal — if you engineer it

"Once you go second-tier you can't come back" is overstated. Refinancing
depends on your current state: score, income, debt ratio, delinquencies,
and the lender's screening. Recover the score, extend tenure, grow
declared income, reduce principal — and bank refinancing becomes
realistic. The regulator's loan-switching infrastructure exists to move
consumers from credit, mortgage and jeonse loans into better products.

The point: enter with an **exit plan**, not a hope.

Consider an E-7 worker who borrowed ₩20M at 15% from a savings bank on
arrival, when no Korean credit file existed. A year later — same
employer, twelve clean payments, higher declared income, accumulated
payroll, card and health-insurance records — the risk profile is
genuinely different, and the main bank or a foreigner-specialist bank
can be asked again.

What did the work was not "waiting a year." It was five things moving
together:

## Use the interest-rate reduction right — it is real

If your creditworthiness improves after drawdown — employment, promotion,
higher income, lower debt, better score — you can formally request a rate
reduction (금리인하요구권). Specialized finance companies disclose their
acceptance counts, acceptance rates and interest reduced, so the channel
is measurable rather than theoretical. Since February 2026, MyData
operators at some institutions can even file these requests on a
borrower's behalf automatically.

It is a right to *request*, not to receive: the lender must judge that
the improvement actually changes its pricing. But it costs nothing to
ask, and a rejected request leaves no mark on your file.

## Check policy lending before you accept a commercial high rate

Before signing a high-rate commercial loan, check whether you qualify
for policy finance. In 2026 savings banks launched a **mid-rate living
stability loan** for borrowers in the bottom 50% of credit scores: up to
**₩10M in total across institutions**, at **5.9–15.27%** at the launch
institutions — a maximum rate about 1.24 percentage points below the
previous mid-rate product, and expanding to more savings banks and to
banks, card and capital companies. Sunshine Loan (햇살론) products can
also beat ordinary high-rate credit loans where you meet the income and
credit conditions.

For foreigners the caveat is firm: **policy products publish their own
nationality and residence conditions**, and being a registered foreigner
does not automatically qualify you. Note too that the living stability
loan carries conditions of its own — borrowers agree not to purchase a
home for a year or until full repayment. The right move is to verify
formal eligibility, not to look for a workaround.

## Choose the loan with an exit, not the one with the highest approval odds

Foreign borrowers rejected at a bank often pick the second-tier lender
purely on approval probability. The better criterion is **when and on
what terms can I leave?**

A 16% loan refinanced into a 7% bank loan after eight months costs far
less in total than the sticker suggests. The same loan, stacked with a
card loan and another credit line, raises total debt and DSR, elevates
risk, and makes refinancing *less* likely — the trap closes.

So before signing, run two numbers:

**minimum borrowing = funds needed − own funds**, and
**interest until your realistic refinancing date + prepayment cost**.

Borrowing the full approved limit because it was offered is usually the
most expensive choice available.

## The bottom line

Savings banks and capital companies provide genuine access to borrowers
banks decline — which, for someone with a short Korean credit history,
is a market worth having. The price is more than a rate gap, because it
propagates:

**higher borrowing cost → larger total debt → less DSR headroom →
elevated credit risk → worse bank pricing later.**

Borrow the minimum, repay cleanly, build declared income and a readable
credit file, then refinance — and second-tier lending is a *funding
stage*, not a permanent financial caste. The genuinely dangerous choice
for a foreigner is not the second tier itself. It is **holding a
high-rate loan for years with no exit plan.**
