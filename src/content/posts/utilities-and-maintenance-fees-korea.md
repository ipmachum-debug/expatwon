Renting in Korea costs more than the monthly rent or the deposit. Every
month there is metered consumption — electricity, city gas, water — and,
in an apartment or officetel, a maintenance fee covering general
management, security, cleaning, lift upkeep and heating.

For a foreign tenant, the difficulty is rarely the amount. It is **who
bears which cost, and where one occupant's period ends and the next
begins.** How far the previous tenant's electricity and gas were settled;
whether water is inside the maintenance fee or billed separately; whether
the fee includes a line that is legally the owner's to bear — none of
this is obvious from the bill, and all of it surfaces either in your
first month or on the day you leave.

So the work starts on move-in day, with a camera.

## Photograph the meters before you unpack

The lease term and the utility billing period do not line up. A previous
tenant leaves in the morning and you arrive in the afternoon; or you
collect the keys a few days early to clean.

On the day you take possession, photograph the current reading on the
electricity meter, the gas meter and — if water is individually metered —
the water meter. Frame each shot so the **meter number and the reading
are both legible.** That single set of photos is the simplest possible
evidence of where the previous occupant's consumption ends and yours
begins.

In an apartment where the management office reads electricity or water
centrally, tell the office your move-in date and ask whether the prior
period has been settled before you do anything else.

## Electricity: find the customer number first

Electricity is billed under a supply contract with KEPCO, identified by a
customer number. Find yours on an existing bill, from the management
office, or through KEPCO ON; the customer centre on **123** handles the
same business by phone. KEPCO ON covers customer number lookup, name
transfer, billing enquiry, payment and direct debit.

Before you try to transfer anything, settle one question with the
management office: is electricity billed **individually by KEPCO**, or
**read by the management office and charged inside the maintenance fee**?
In the second case there is no KEPCO contract for you to change — you
update the occupant record with the management office instead. Getting
this backwards is the most common wasted afternoon of a Korean move-in.

### The tier cliff is why your summer bill exploded

Residential electricity runs on three progressive tiers. Two features
make bills jump more than consumption does.

First, the per-kWh rate rises with each tier, applied only to the units
in that tier. Second — and this is the part that surprises people — the
**basic charge is set by the highest tier you reach**, for the whole
month. On a low-voltage residential contract, tipping into the top tier
lifts the basic charge from ₩1,600 to ₩7,300: ₩5,700 added before a
single extra kilowatt-hour is priced.

Apartments supplied at high voltage use the same tier boundaries with
different rates, so check which contract type applies to your building
before predicting a bill.

<div class="callout callout-tip">
  <p class="callout-title">The practical version</p>
  <p>If your usage sits just under a boundary near month end, deferring
  laundry or the dryer by a few days is worth more than it sounds — you
  are avoiding both the higher unit rate and the basic-charge step.</p>
</div>

## City gas: there is no national supplier

Unlike electricity, city gas is supplied regionally. Seoul alone is
divided among several companies — Seoul City Gas, YESCO, Coone Energy
Service and others — so the first question at a new address is **which
company supplies it.**

Once you know, book a move-in with that supplier. Where required, a
technician or an authorised contractor visits to check the meter, connect
the range and perform a safety inspection. If the user has changed, the
account name is transferred at the same time. Suppliers also use that
visit to check the meter reading and whether the previous occupant left
arrears or an unsettled bill.

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">Do not connect gas appliances yourself</p>
  <p>Connecting a gas range or gas piping is a safety matter, and it is
  done through the supplier or a contractor holding the relevant
  qualification. This is not a formality you save money by skipping.</p>
</div>

On moving out, book a move-out reading and settle the final consumption.
And do not assume a direct debit follows you: end the mandate at the old
address and register again against the new contract or customer number.

## Water: it depends on the building

Water is handled differently by building type.

In most apartments the management office reads each household's water use
and bills it inside the maintenance fee. You update the occupant record
with the office; there is nothing to change at the city waterworks.

In a detached house, or a building where each unit holds its own water
contract, the local waterworks office handles the name change, bill
enquiries and direct debit. In Seoul that is Arisu, which covers bill
enquiry, move-out settlement, name change and payment registration — by
customer number, or in some services by address and occupant details.

One trap: water is read on a different cycle from electricity and gas,
and some areas bill **every two months**. The last bill you received
before moving is therefore not proof that the account is settled. Work
from the meter reading on your move-out date instead.

## What a maintenance fee is actually made of

The monthly maintenance fee is not one cost. It is many, aggregated —
which is why the total tells you almost nothing about what you are paying
for.

## The line the landlord owes you back

The long-term repair reserve funds replacement and repair of the
building's major systems under a long-term repair plan — lifts, exterior
walls, piping, waterproofing, mechanical plant. It is capital
accumulation for the asset, not a service you consumed.

The Multi-Family Housing Management Act is explicit that it is collected
**from the owner of the dwelling**. In practice it appears on the
household bill along with everything else, so the tenant pays it month
after month. Paying it does not make you the one who bears it: where an
occupant has paid it, the owner is to refund that amount.

**How to actually collect it:**

<div class="steps">
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">1</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Before moving out, ask the management office</strong> for the total long-term repair reserve paid during your occupancy. Where an occupant requests confirmation of payment, the management body is to issue a certificate without delay.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">2</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Take the certificate, not your own arithmetic.</strong> The monthly amount changes over time, so a figure you calculated yourself will not match.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">3</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Claim the total from the landlord</strong> as part of the move-out settlement, alongside the deposit.</div></div>
</div>

Three years at ₩25,000 a month is ₩900,000 — enough to be worth the
fifteen minutes it takes to ask, and routinely left behind by tenants who
never knew the line was theirs to reclaim.

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">"Repair and upkeep" is not the same line</p>
  <p>A bill may show 수선유지비 (repair and maintenance expense) as well.
  That is an ordinary operating cost with a different legal character, and
  it is not refundable. Read the exact item name on the bill before
  claiming anything.</p>
</div>

## The ₩100,000 rule is about listings, not bills

Small-unit listings used to say "rent ₩500,000, maintenance ₩150,000" and
stop there, leaving the tenant to discover what was inside the second
number. The advertising standards were tightened in response.

Where an agent advertises a dwelling with a **fixed maintenance fee of
₩100,000 or more**, the fee has to be broken out into general and common
management, usage charges — electricity, water, gas, heating — and other
items, rather than shown as one number. Advertising in breach can attract
a penalty.

Read that rule for what it is. It governs **how a property is
advertised**, not how a landlord must bill you monthly. So at contract
stage, go past the listing:

- Is the fee **fixed**, or settled against actual use?
- Does it include electricity, water, gas?
- Internet and TV?
- Is parking extra?
- Is individual heating and cooling extra?
- Does it include the long-term repair reserve?

Ask for the **last three months of actual bills** before signing. In a
building with central or district heating, compare a summer bill and a
winter bill — the gap can exceed the difference in rent between two units
you were choosing among. For an officetel in particular, whether
electricity, water and climate control sit inside the fee decides your
real monthly housing cost.

For an apartment, K-apt publishes actual maintenance-fee data by complex,
so you can check a specific building rather than relying on what the
listing claims.

A contract that says only "maintenance fee ₩100,000 per month" with no
itemisation is where most of these disputes begin. The related deposit
and contract mechanics are covered in
[protecting your rental deposit](/cost-of-living/rental-deposit-protection-korea/),
and the jeonse-versus-wolse trade-off in
[the Korean rental system](/cost-of-living/jeonse-vs-wolse-korean-rental-system/).

## Direct debits: easy to start, easy to forget

Electricity, gas, water and the maintenance fee can all be paid
automatically from a Korean bank account or card, and for a long-stay
resident that is the sensible default — particularly with frequent travel,
where paper bills and missed due dates are a real risk. Pair it with
electronic billing.

Moving out reverses the advice. **A name transfer does not necessarily
cancel the existing mandate.** Check each of the four separately, confirm
the final charge has been taken, and only then cancel.

And close the Korean bank account **last**. The final utility settlements
and the deposit return both need somewhere to land — the same sequencing
error that catches people
[disposing of a car before leaving](/cars/selling-or-exporting-your-car-when-leaving-korea/).
If your account is still restricted, see
[limited accounts and how they are released](/banking/korean-limited-accounts-hando-gyejwa-explained/).

## Arrears from the previous occupant

It does not follow that a previous tenant's unpaid bills become your debt
simply because you moved in — the supply contract has a party and a
period of use, and yours starts when it starts.

What does happen is administrative friction. If the account name is never
brought into line with who is actually living there, the arrears surface
when you register supply or ask for it to be restored, and you end up
having to explain the boundary rather than assert it. Gas suppliers check
the meter and the previous occupant's payment status as part of the
move-in visit precisely for this reason.

So the answer is evidential, not legal. On move-in: get the previous
occupant's final settlement receipt, and register the account in your own
name immediately. Same principle for electricity and water. You are not
inheriting someone's debt — you are proving where your period of use
began.

Where the maintenance fee runs to month end, ask for an interim
settlement statement dated to your move-out. Keeping same-day receipts for
electricity, gas and water makes the deposit conversation with the
landlord short, because there is nothing left to argue about.

## What Korean housing actually costs per month

Rent is one line of several. The honest calculation is:

> monthly rent (or the financing cost of a jeonse deposit)
> \+ general maintenance fee
> \+ your own electricity, water and gas
> \+ heating and cooling
> \+ parking and communications
> **− the long-term repair reserve you will reclaim on the way out**

Set the meters and the account names right on the way in, watch the
itemisation and the direct debits while you live there, and separate
consumption from capital on the way out. Do those three things and Korean
utilities stop being a source of surprises — and the last one puts money
back in your pocket that most tenants never ask for.
