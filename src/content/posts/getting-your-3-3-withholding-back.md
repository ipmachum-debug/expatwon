A client pays ₩2,000,000 and ₩1,934,000 lands in your account. The ₩66,000 is
gone and everyone calls it tax, so it feels settled.

It is not settled. It is a deposit against a bill nobody has calculated yet,
and it only comes back to you if two separate things happen — the payer
reports it under your registration number, and you file the return that
compares the deposit with what you actually owed.

Most guides cover the second. The first is where the money actually goes
missing.

## Start with the record, not your invoices

Your own paperwork tells you what you were paid. It cannot tell you what was
declared about you, and only the second one funds a refund.

So the first move is to look at the income data held under your resident or
foreign registration number at Hometax, and ask a narrow question of each
engagement: does this payment appear, and does it carry withheld tax.

Three outcomes, three different next steps.

| What the record shows | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| Payment present, tax withheld | The prepayment exists. It is a filing question now. |
| Payment present, no withholding | The income is declared but nothing was prepaid — expect a bill, not a refund. |
| Payment absent entirely | A reporting problem sits in front of your refund. |

That third row is the one worth catching early, because the fix runs through
somebody else.

## When the payer never filed

Withholding and reporting are separate obligations on the payer — deducting
the money does not discharge the duty to declare it. When they come apart,
the deduction is real and the record is empty, and there is nothing under your
number to claim against.

That is the payer's failure, not yours, but it is your refund behind it. Raise
it with them first, holding what you already have: the contract, the invoice,
the amount received, the amount deducted, the date. If it is not corrected,
the same evidence goes to the tax office.

What you must not do is write a number into a return to make it balance. A
return that claims a prepayment the record does not show is a second problem
layered on the first, and the first was never yours.

The adjacent obligations — who has to withhold, and on what deadlines — are in
[who withholds what](/business/withholding-tax-for-freelancers-korea/).

## Then: is there actually a refund?

A refund is not produced by the withholding. It is produced by the gap between
the prepayment and the tax finally due once deductions and expenses are
applied.

At modest freelance income the prepayment commonly exceeds the liability, and
money comes back. As income rises, or where this income stacks on top of
employment or other sources, the gap narrows and can reverse — in which case
filing produces a bill rather than a refund.

Both outcomes are the system working. Filing is how you find out which one you
are in, and
[who has to file in May](/business/may-global-income-tax-filing-for-foreigners/)
decides whether the question is even yours to answer this year.

## The years you did not file are not gone

This is the part most people never hear, and it is the reason this guide
exists.

Where tax has been overpaid for a year whose filing deadline has already
passed, a claim for correction can recover it — filed separately for each
year, within {{fig:amended-return-claim-window}} of that year's statutory
filing deadline.

So a freelancer who had 3.3% deducted for four years and never once filed in
May is not looking at four closed years. They are looking at four unclaimed
refunds, each with its own calculation and its own expiry.

And the expiry moves. Every May, the oldest recoverable year drops off the
back. Nothing announces it; the amount simply stops being reachable. That is
the actual cost of putting this off — not a penalty, just a year going quiet.

A caveat worth stating plainly: this recovers tax that was overpaid. It is not
a way to reopen a year in which nothing was overpaid, and it is not an
extension of the current year's deadline.

## The order that works

Pull the income data for each year under your registration number. Confirm,
year by year, that the payments appear and the withholding is attached. Chase
anything missing with the payer, and with the tax office if that fails.
Calculate each year on its own figures rather than assuming the ratio repeats.
File the current year in May, and claim the earlier ones year by year inside
the window — oldest first, because that is the one about to expire.

Keep what you already hold while you do it: contracts, invoices, the amounts
received against the amounts deducted, and the withholding tax receipts
themselves, which
[reading your withholding tax receipt](/business/korean-withholding-tax-receipt-explained/)
unpacks line by line.
