Owning Korean property and borrowing against it are two different legal
questions, and foreign buyers routinely collapse them into one.

With an alien registration card you can, in principle, buy a Korean
apartment. That says nothing about whether a bank will lend against it.
The lender is not assessing your right to own — it is assessing your
Korean income, your remaining period of stay, your employment, your
domestic credit record and your existing debt.

So a foreign purchase is not a property transaction with a loan attached.
It is five processes on one timeline: the sale contract, the mortgage
review, the transaction report, bringing the money into Korea, and the
simultaneous transfer of title and registration of the bank's charge.

## Being foreign does not exempt you — in either direction

There is no rule barring foreign nationals from Korean mortgages. There
is equally no route around Korea's household lending rules by being
foreign. A household mortgage from a Korean financial institution carries
LTV and DSR according to where the property is, how many homes you own,
the purpose of the loan and your capacity to repay.

For a first-time buyer or a one-home owner selling on, the baseline in
2026 is **LTV 70% outside regulated areas and 40% inside them**.

But LTV alone does not produce your loan amount.

## The hard cap most people miss

Purchase-purpose mortgages in the capital region and regulated areas
carry an **absolute limit by property price**, on top of LTV:

| Property value | Maximum loan |
|---|---|
| Up to ₩1,500,000,000 | ₩600,000,000 |
| ₩1.5bn – ₩2.5bn | ₩400,000,000 |
| Above ₩2,500,000,000 | ₩200,000,000 |

So on a ₩2,000,000,000 apartment, 40% LTV computes to ₩800,000,000 —
and you may borrow ₩400,000,000. The lower ceiling is the real one.

## Valuation is not "KB price × LTV"

Mortgage conversations lean on the KB property price index because it is
convenient, which produces the shorthand *KB price × LTV = your loan*.
That is too simple to plan on.

A bank may use the KB figure, Korea Real Estate Board data, the actual
transaction price, a formal appraisal, or its own internal collateral
standard. Large estates with dense transaction histories value easily.
A standalone block, a villa, a detached house or a high-value property
may need an appraisal or a separate internal assessment — and the number
that comes back is the one your loan is built on.

Ask the bank what it will value the property at **before** you sign the
sale contract.

## The bottleneck is usually recognised income, not DSR

The bank-sector borrower DSR threshold is generally 40%: annual principal
and interest across *all* your household debt, divided by recognised
annual income. Existing credit loans, car finance and other mortgages all
consume the same room.

For a foreign borrower the contested term is almost always the
denominator.

**Korean employment income** is straightforward: withholding tax receipt,
income certificate, payslips, employment certificate, salary transfer
history. **Korean business income** works from the income certificate,
business registration and global income tax filings.

**Overseas income is not automatically equivalent.** You can submit
foreign tax returns, payslips, employment certificates and overseas
account records and still find the bank applies its own internal credit
policy to decide whether the income counts and at what proportion —
weighing exchange-rate movement and how durable the income looks.

<p class="hand">If most of your income arrives from abroad, ask the branch what it will actually recognise before you fall in love with an apartment.</p>

## Stress DSR is a limit calculation, not your interest rate

Stress DSR adds a notional stress rate on top of the loan rate when
computing your capacity. **You are not charged that rate.** It exists to
size the loan conservatively against the possibility that rates rise.

In 2026 a strengthened stress framework applies to capital-region and
regulated-area mortgages. So your actual rate can be low while the limit
calculation assumes a higher one — and the same income that supported a
given loan two years ago supports less today. There is no general foreign
exemption from it.

## Residence status shapes bank policy, not the statutory limits

F-5, F-6, F-4, F-2 and E-7 holders do not all borrow on the same terms.
But it is equally wrong to state visa-by-visa loan terms as if they were
law — "F-5 gets 40 years, E-7 only to the end of the visa" is a
description of some banks' internal policy, not a rule.

What is actually assessed:

| Status | What the file turns on |
|---|---|
| **F-5** permanent residence | Settled long-term presence in Korea |
| **F-6** marriage migrant | Family ties and likely long-term residence |
| **F-4** overseas Korean | Korean employment, income and length of domestic residence |
| **F-2** residence | Sub-type, remaining period, stability of Korean income |
| **E-7** specific activity | Employer, contract length, remaining stay, salary, renewal prospects |

This is not a ranking. An E-7 holder with years at a large Korean
employer, high declared income and a thick credit file is a different
proposition from an E-7 who started work last month — and the second one
is the reason the category looks weak in blog posts. The underlying
variables are the ones described in
[whether foreigners can borrow in Korea](/loans/can-foreigners-get-a-loan-in-korea/)
and [the Korean credit score](/loans/korean-credit-score-for-foreigners-nice-kcb/).

## Since October 2025, Seoul is a permit zone

This is the change that most reshapes a foreign purchase, and it is
missing from nearly every English guide.

Under the measures of **15 October 2025**, all of Seoul and twelve
Gyeonggi districts were designated land-transaction permit zones and
regulated areas together. Two consequences dominate everything else:

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">Permission first, and then you must live there</p>
  <p>Buying an apartment — or a multi-unit building containing one — in a
  permit zone requires <strong>the local authority's permission</strong>,
  and the buyer must <strong>actually reside in it for two years</strong>
  from acquisition. Buying with a sitting jeonse tenant, or letting it
  out, is not available.</p>
</div>

For a foreign buyer that second line deserves a hard look before anything
else. A two-year residence obligation sits awkwardly against a residence
status that renews annually, or against an assignment that may end. If
there is any real prospect that you will not be living in that property
in two years' time, take advice on your specific position before you
commit — this guide cannot tell you how the obligation interacts with
your status.

Regulated-area purchases also require a **funding plan with supporting
evidence of where the money came from**.

## The 60-day report that does not apply to you

Here is the most repeated error in English-language material on this
subject.

An ordinary sale contract is reportable by everyone, foreign or not,
under Article 3 of the Real Estate Transaction Reporting Act: buyer and
seller report the actual price **within 30 days of the contract**, and
where a licensed agent brokered it, the agent normally files.

Article 8 of the same Act creates a separate foreigner acquisition
report — and its first paragraph **expressly excludes contracts already
covered by Article 3.** An ordinary apartment purchase is one of those.
So a foreigner who buys by ordinary sale and files the Article 3 report
does **not** then file a second "foreigner acquisition report" within 60
days. The 60-day report exists, but for acquisition contracts outside
Article 3's scope.

Separately, certain land — military installation protection zones among
them — requires **advance permission** for acquisition by a foreign
national. That is a different provision from either report above, and it
is a permission, not a notification.

Where a funding plan is required, a foreign buyer's answer is a chain,
not a number. Self-funds, bank borrowing, assumed lease deposits, gifts
or inheritance, and overseas money each have to be identified separately.

## Resident or non-resident matters more than nationality

The Foreign Exchange Transactions Act does not use nationality. It uses
**resident** and **non-resident**, and a foreign national living in Korea
long-term can be a resident under it.

So two people with the same passport can face different filing duties.
Where a **non-resident** acquires Korean property with money remitted or
carried in from abroad, the Foreign Exchange Transaction Regulations can
require a filing with a foreign exchange bank.

Before you contract, establish with your main foreign exchange bank:
which side of that line you are on, what the source of the funds is, and
by what route they will enter Korea. "I'm a foreigner, so I designate one
bank and that's that" is not the shape of this rule.

## Completion day moves as one transaction

The mortgage draws down at completion. The bank secures itself by
registering its charge at the same moment you take title — so payment,
transfer of ownership and registration of the bank's mortgage all
interlock on the same day.

A foreign buyer typically assembles: alien registration card or domestic
residence report card, passport, proof of address, the sale contract, the
transaction report certificate, acquisition tax documents, the loan
agreement papers, and identity documents for the registration filing.

One practical note: **a Korean seal certificate is not required in every
case.** Depending on whether you have registered a seal and how the
transaction is structured, a signature verification certificate, notarised
signature or other identity evidence may be used instead. Fix the exact
list with the judicial scrivener and the bank *before* completion day.

## Get the approval before the contract, not after

The most expensive mistake available to a foreign buyer is paying a
deposit and then looking for a mortgage.

LTV can look ample while recognised income cuts the loan through DSR. The
hard cap can bite before either. Remaining stay can shorten the term. And
in a permit zone the purchase needs permission and a two-year residence
commitment on top.

## After drawdown

A mortgage does not evaporate because your status changes or you leave
Korea. But foreign-borrower loan agreements can carry their own
undertakings about residence period, Korean address and employment. Read
those clauses before a long absence or a change of status, rather than
after.

The through-line of this entire guide: for a foreign buyer in Korea the
decisive question is never nationality. It is whether you can evidence
four things — **what the property is worth to the bank, what income it
recognises, which ceiling binds, and how your money got here.** Settle
all four, then sign.
