Korean ATMs are not one network. A good proportion are built for domestic
cards only and will decline an overseas card without telling you why.

The ones that take foreign cards are marked, clustered in predictable
places, and charge you in three separate ways.

## Finding a machine that will work

Look for **"Global ATM"**, "Foreign Card" or "Overseas Card" on the casing or
the first screen, then the network logos your card carries. Hana Bank's Global
ATM service, for example, lists
[seven networks](/tracked/foreign-card-atm-supported-networks/) — Mastercard,
Maestro, Cirrus, Visa, PLUS, JCB and UnionPay. A machine showing none of them is
probably domestic-only.

<div class="callout callout-note">
  <p class="callout-title">Ask about the machine, not the bank</p>
  <p>"KB works" and "CU works" are the wrong shape of question. One bank runs
  machines with the Global service and machines without it; one convenience
  store chain has different operators and models from branch to branch. The
  logos on the unit in front of you decide it, and they take two seconds to
  read.</p>
</div>

Reliable places to find them:

<div class="steps">
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">1</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Airports.</strong> The most foreign-card-friendly machines in the country, and the right place to take your first cash.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">2</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Bank branches</strong>, particularly the larger banks and branches in central or international districts.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">3</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Major stations</strong> and transport hubs.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">4</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Some convenience store chains</strong> — acceptance varies by machine, so read the logos rather than the shop sign.</div></div>
</div>

Your card network's own ATM locator, used before you leave the hotel, saves
more time than walking between machines.

## Choose won, at the machine too

The dynamic currency conversion offer appears at ATMs exactly as it does at
shop terminals: "withdraw in KRW" against "charge my account in my own
currency".

**Choose KRW.** Letting the machine convert hands the rate to the operator's
side; choosing won hands it to your own issuer, which is normally better.

This does not remove your bank's foreign withdrawal fee — that applies
regardless. It removes the second markup.

### Three costs, and no formula worth memorising

```
the Korean ATM operator's fee
+ your issuer's overseas withdrawal fee
+ the currency conversion itself
```

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">Be suspicious of "₩4,000 + 1% + $3"</p>
  <p>Guides publish arithmetic like that as though it were a national tariff. It
  is not: the operator's fee varies by operator and machine, and issuers differ
  completely — some waive the overseas withdrawal fee, some charge a flat sum,
  some a percentage. The two numbers you can actually get are the fee shown on
  the ATM screen before you confirm, and the one in your own card's terms. Read
  those, and ignore anyone else's formula.</p>
</div>

## Machines close

This is the detail that catches visitors from countries where cash machines
simply run.

Korean ATMs commonly have **operating hours**, and some charge more outside
core hours. A machine inside a branch lobby may shut when the branch does.

Two things follow, and both cut against the tidy tables published elsewhere:

<div class="steps">
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">1</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>A 24-hour lobby is not a promise.</strong> Korean banks' after-hours machine areas are widely assumed to run around the clock; building access, system hours and maintenance all cut into that.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">2</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Even one bank at one site differs.</strong> At Incheon Airport, the same bank runs some facilities around the clock and others on fixed hours such as 06:00 to 22:00. Check the location, not the brand.</div></div>
</div>

There is no single nationwide window in which Korean machines stop, either.
Systems do go down for overnight maintenance and overseas card approval can be
briefly unavailable, but banks and networks schedule that separately — so treat
late-night withdrawals as less certain rather than impossible at a specific
hour.

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">Take cash at the airport on a late arrival</p>
  <p>Landing at night with no won and a plan to find a machine near the hotel
  is how visitors end up paying a hotel's exchange rate or going without. The
  airport machines are open, they take foreign cards, and it is one
  withdrawal.</p>
</div>

## Two limits, and the smaller one wins

Every withdrawal is capped twice:

| | Set by | What it covers |
|---|---|---|
| **Machine limit** | The ATM operator | Per transaction, and per day |
| **Card limit** | Your own bank | Foreign withdrawals, per day |

Because the fees are largely **fixed per withdrawal**, the arithmetic
favours fewer, larger trips — subject to how much cash you are willing to
carry, which in Korea is a genuinely low-risk consideration but still your
call.

Which limit stopped you decides whether moving is worth it. If the machine
capped the transaction, another machine may allow more. If your **own bank's**
daily overseas withdrawal limit is spent, no machine in Korea will help — and
that limit often resets on your issuer's clock rather than at Korean midnight,
so "tomorrow" may not mean what you expect.

If a withdrawal is declined without an obvious reason, the usual causes are
your bank's own overseas block, a daily limit already reached, or the machine
not supporting your network. A card that works in a shop and fails at a machine
is usually the third.

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">Two rejections, then stop guessing</p>
  <p>Do not put the card through the same machine repeatedly — repeated failures
  can register as risk with your issuer and get the card blocked. Try one other
  bank's Global ATM. If that also refuses, the problem is almost certainly your
  card's overseas cash settings rather than Korean machines, and the fix is a
  call to your bank.</p>
</div>

## When the machine keeps the card, or the cash

Rare, and worth knowing before it happens rather than during.

**The card is retained.** Inside a branch, tell the staff immediately — that is
the strongest argument for using branch machines for your first withdrawal.
Outside opening hours, or at an independently operated unit, find the operator's
service number and the machine's ID on the front of the unit. What they will ask
for is the location, the machine number, the time, the last four digits of the
card, and what the screen said. If it cannot be returned quickly, call your
issuer about suspending or replacing it.

**Cash does not come out but your account is debited.** Do not immediately
withdraw the same amount again from another machine, which is the instinct and
which turns one dispute into two.

<div class="steps">
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">1</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Keep the receipt or photograph the error screen.</strong> This is the whole case.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">2</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Report it to both sides</strong> — the Korean ATM operator and your own issuer.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">3</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Some reverse automatically</strong> in the operator's settlement. Where that does not happen, your issuer's dispute process is the route.</div></div>
</div>

Keep ATM receipts until the cash is in your hand and the amount has settled
correctly. It costs nothing and it is the difference between a claim and a
recollection.

## By machine type, not by bank

| Type | Foreign cards | Strength | Watch for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bank Global ATM | High | Staff on hand in hours | Location and opening hours vary |
| Ordinary bank ATM | Varies | Everywhere | May have no overseas function |
| Airport Global ATM | High | Usable on arrival | Hours and fees differ by facility |
| Convenience store Global ATM | Fairly high | Open late | Fees and limits vary widely |
| Other convenience store units | Varies | Convenient | May not take international cards |
| Station and mall machines | Varies | Well placed | Depends on operator and model |

For a first withdrawal with an unfamiliar card, or for a large one, a bank's
Global ATM during opening hours is the conservative choice — not because the
machine is better, but because someone is there if it goes wrong.

## How much cash a visitor actually needs

Less than in most countries, and more than a card-only habit expects.

Cards cover hotels, department stores, chains and larger restaurants
comfortably. Cash covers the gaps — small restaurants, markets, older
terminals, some taxis — and those gaps are where a lot of the good food is.

Where cards get refused and why, and what to carry instead, is in
[paying in Korea with a foreign card](/banking/paying-in-korea-with-a-foreign-card/).

<div class="callout callout-note">
  <p class="callout-title">A transport card removes most of the cash you thought you needed</p>
  <p>Buses, the subway, many taxis and convenience-store purchases all run on
  it, and it tops up with notes. Load it early and the amount of loose cash
  you carry drops sharply.</p>
</div>

## Staying longer than a visit

All of this is what you do **before** you have a Korean account. After that,
your own bank's card withdraws from its own network without any of the three
charges above.

That is the real reason to stop patching: not convenience, arithmetic. Two
or three months of foreign-card withdrawals costs more than the afternoon it
takes to open an account.
