A lease that protects a tenant is not one with more clauses in it. It is one
whose clauses say what happens **when they are broken** — whether the tenant can
withhold the balance, terminate, and get back what has already been paid.

Five clauses do most of that work. One of them is also the most commonly
misunderstood clause in Korean residential tenancy, so it goes first.

## 1. Why the first clause exists: the day-long gap

Under the Housing Lease Protection Act, a tenant who has been delivered the
housing and completed resident registration can assert the lease against third
parties **[from the following day](/tracked/housing-lease-opposability-effective-time/)**.

A mortgage registered by the landlord takes its rank on the day it is registered.

Put those two together and a structural gap appears. A tenant who pays the
balance on Friday morning, moves in, and files that same morning does not have
opposing power until Saturday. A mortgage registered Friday afternoon can rank
ahead of them.

Nothing the tenant did was wrong. The gap is in the statute.

## 2. The no-encumbrance clause, written properly

The clause worth having reads roughly:

> The landlord shall not, from the date of this contract until the date on which
> the tenant's opposing power arises, create any new mortgage, chonsegwon or
> other security interest over the leased property, nor cause any attachment,
> provisional attachment or other change in rights affecting the tenant's
> recovery of the deposit.

The end point is the drafting decision. Writing "until the balance payment date"
is the common version and it stops one day early — precisely the day the gap is
open. Write it as **the date the tenant's opposing power arises**.

## 3. What that clause cannot do

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">A breached clause does not void the bank's mortgage</p>
  <p>If the landlord ignores the clause and registers a mortgage in favour of a
  financial institution, that mortgage is <strong>not automatically invalid</strong>.
  The clause is a contractual obligation between landlord and tenant. Breaching it
  can ground termination, return of the deposit and damages — all against the
  landlord. The priority of a third party's registered security interest is
  decided separately, on the register.</p>
</div>

This is not a technicality. It changes what the clause is for.

It is a claim against the landlord, and a landlord who has just mortgaged the
property to raise money is not the counterparty you want to be holding a claim
against. Which means the clause never travels alone: it is written **and** the
register is re-checked in the hour before the balance moves.

The register in question has two parts worth reading separately. Section A
(갑구) carries the current owner, attachments, provisional attachments,
injunctions and transfers of ownership. Section B (을구) carries mortgages,
chonsegwon and other security interests. A register that was clean when the
contract was signed can carry new entries by the balance date, which is why the
minimum is twice — before signing, and immediately before paying — and for a
large deposit, a fresh copy pulled minutes before the transfer.

<div class="callout callout-note">
  <p class="callout-title">A registered mortgage amount is not the loan balance</p>
  <p>Mortgages are usually registered at a maximum claim amount rather than the
  outstanding debt, and the registered figure is customarily set above the loan
  itself. Reading it as the debt understates the encumbrance. The conservative
  test is prior-ranking claims plus your own deposit against a cautious valuation
  of the property — not against what similar units are currently listed at, because
  an auction does not promise a market price. The wider framing is in
  <a href="/cost-of-living/rental-deposit-protection-korea/">protecting your housing deposit in Korea</a>.</p>
</div>

## 4. The ownership-change clause: ask for a remedy, not a veto

"The landlord may not sell without the tenant's consent" is a clause people
write and it does not do what they hope. A landlord may in principle sell their
own property, and the clause will not by itself stop a transfer being registered
to a buyer.

The version that works gives the tenant something to do:

> Where the landlord pursues a transfer of ownership before the balance payment
> date, the landlord shall notify the tenant in advance; and where a change of
> ownership arises that materially affects the tenant's recovery of the deposit,
> the tenant may terminate this contract and shall be repaid the sums already
> paid.

Same pattern as the first clause. Not a prohibition on the outside world — an
express exit for the tenant.

## 5. The guarantee clause, and why eligibility comes before signing

The HUG deposit return guarantee has the institution repay the deposit, on
conditions, where the landlord does not. Two figures decide whether it is even
available:

| Condition | Current position |
|---|---|
| Maximum deposit | [₩700M capital region · ₩500M elsewhere](/tracked/hug-deposit-guarantee-limit/) |
| Application window | [Before half the lease term has elapsed](/tracked/hug-application-deadline/) |

Underwriting then looks at the deposit against the property's value and
prior-ranking claims, and at the landlord.

Which is why this is a pre-contract question. A property that cannot be
guaranteed is a property whose deposit has no institutional backstop, and finding
that out in month thirteen of a twenty-four month lease leaves nothing to be
done.

The clause:

> Where the deposit return guarantee is refused for reasons not attributable to
> the tenant — the landlord, the property's rights position, the housing's value,
> prior-ranking claims, or the landlord's own eligibility — the tenant may
> terminate this contract, and the landlord shall immediately return the contract
> deposit and any sums received.

Note what the list is doing. "If the insurance does not work out, the contract is
cancelled" invites an argument about a tenant who applied late or did not submit
documents. Naming the causes that count removes it.

## 6. The tax clause has changed, and most templates have not

Unpaid taxes can outrank a tenant's deposit. The old difficulty was that
checking needed the landlord's cooperation, which a landlord in arrears had
every reason to withhold.

That is no longer the whole picture. A tenant who has **concluded a lease** with a
deposit above
[₩10,000,000](/tracked/unpaid-tax-inspection-without-landlord-consent-threshold/)
may inspect the landlord's unpaid national and local taxes **without the
landlord's consent**, up until the lease term begins, at the tax office or the
local authority. The office notifies the landlord that the inspection happened.

Before signing, inspection still runs on consent. So the clause should do more
than record consent:

> The landlord shall disclose any existing arrears of national or local taxes,
> shall not incur new tax arrears capable of ranking ahead of the tenant's
> recovery of the deposit between the date of this contract and the balance
> payment date, and shall notify the tenant of any such arrears.

Consent covers the past. This covers the window between signing and paying,
which is the window the tenant is exposed in.

## 7. The balance-payment condition ties the other four together

> Where, immediately before the balance payment, the certified copy of the
> register differs from its state at the date of contract in any respect
> affecting the tenant's recovery of the deposit, the tenant may withhold payment
> of the balance and may terminate this contract.

This is the clause that converts a discovery into a decision. Without it, a
tenant who pulls a fresh register at 9am and finds a new entry is in an argument
about whether they are in breach for not paying at 10am.

## The five, and what each actually gives you

| Clause | What it gives the tenant | What it does not do |
|---|---|---|
| No new encumbrances | Termination, return of deposit, damages | Does not void a registered mortgage |
| Ownership change | Advance notice and a right to terminate | Does not block a sale |
| Guarantee refusal | Exit where refusal is not the tenant's fault | Does not create eligibility |
| Taxes | Disclosure now, notice of new arrears | Does not outrank tax already secured |
| Balance condition | A right to stop and walk away | Does not undo an entry already made |

Every row's right-hand column points the same way. These clauses govern the
relationship with the landlord. They do not rearrange the register.

Which is why the operational answer to the day-long gap is not a better clause.
It is a fresh copy of the register minutes before the transfer, the balance paid,
and delivery plus the address filing completed the same day — for a foreign
tenant, the stay-address report rather than a resident registration.

What happens after you have moved in — who pays for the boiler, what
"restoration to original condition" actually covers, and what to do if the lease
ends and the deposit does not come back — is the other half of the contract.
