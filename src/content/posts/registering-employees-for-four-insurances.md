Hiring is not finished when the contract is signed and the first salary
goes out. Before that, you have to work out which of Korea's four social
insurances the person falls under — and file each one with the right
agency, by its own deadline.

The four are not one system. National Pension, National Health
Insurance, Employment Insurance and Industrial Accident Compensation
Insurance each run on their own statute. The same employee, on the same
contract, can be covered by one and excluded from another, and the day
each filing is due is not the same day.

## The deadlines diverge, and that is the trap

| Insurance | What you file | Deadline | Agency |
|---|---|---|---|
| National Pension | Workplace subscriber acquisition | 15th of the month after the event | National Pension Service |
| Health insurance | Employee subscriber acquisition | **Within 14 days of coverage starting** | National Health Insurance Service |
| Employment insurance | Acquisition of insured status | 15th of the month after the event | Korea Workers' Compensation & Welfare Service |
| Industrial accident | Employment, status and wage information | 15th of the month following the hire, subject to statutory exceptions | Korea Workers' Compensation & Welfare Service |

The industrial accident line carries a genuine statutory exception rather
than a soft "usually": the Act on the Collection of Insurance Premiums
fixes the 15th-of-the-following-month duty, and the Enforcement Decree
then relieves some employees from being reported at all — short-hours
work under 60 hours a month among them. That is an exception to the
*reporting duty*, and it is not the same question as whether the person
is covered, which the next section takes separately.

The Four Major Social Insurance Information Portal, and each agency's own
EDI system, will take several of these filings together. That convenience
is where the mistake starts: submitting through one screen does not put
the four on one statutory clock.

So the common practice — *file last month's new starters on the 15th* —
is late for health insurance for almost everyone. Someone who starts on
the 3rd has a health deadline of the 17th, and the batch does not go out
until the 15th of the *next* month.

If this is your first employee, there is a second question in front of
all four: whether the workplace itself is registered for each insurance.
Employee-level acquisition reporting assumes the workplace already
exists in each system.

## The wage you report is not the salary you agreed

Each insurance calculates on its own wage concept. National Pension uses
a standard monthly income figure, health insurance uses a monthly
remuneration figure, and employment and industrial accident insurance
work from remuneration or an average monthly figure.

At the point of hire, all of them start from what the person is expected
to earn for their work — base pay, position allowances, allowances paid
on a fixed basis, regular bonuses — and then remove the pay that is
exempt from income tax.

Two exemptions come up constantly:

<div class="check-card">
  <div class="check-row ok"><span class="mark"></span><span><strong>Meal allowance</strong> — up to ₩200,000 a month is exempt, but only for an employee who is <em>not</em> provided with meals in kind.</span></div>
  <div class="check-row ok"><span class="mark"></span><span><strong>Own-vehicle allowance</strong> — up to ₩200,000 a month, where the employee uses a car they own or lease in their own name for work, and receives the allowance under the company's rules instead of claiming actual local travel costs.</span></div>
  <div class="check-row miss"><span class="mark"></span><span><strong>Renaming a payslip line does not do it.</strong> Calling part of the salary "meals" or "vehicle" changes nothing unless the conditions above actually hold.</span></div>
  <div class="check-result">Exempt pay is removed before the contribution is calculated, so it lowers the employer's share as well as the employee's deduction — which is exactly why it is looked at.</div>
</div>

## Hours and contract shape decide coverage, not the job title

Whether someone is "full-time" or "contract" in your own vocabulary
decides nothing. The working conditions do, and each insurance draws its
line differently:

<div class="steps">
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">1</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Health insurance</strong> — generally excludes short-hours employees working under 60 contracted hours a month.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">2</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>National Pension</strong> — 60 hours a month is the same starting point, but someone employed for a month or more can still be covered if monthly income reaches the published floor, which for 2026 is ₩2,200,000.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">3</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Employment insurance</strong> — generally excludes work under 60 hours a month or 15 hours a week, but covers it where the work is the person's livelihood and continues for three months or more.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">4</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Industrial accident</strong> — not excluded on short hours as a general rule. Assess it separately rather than assuming it follows the other three.</div></div>
</div>

The first month is also charged differently depending on the start date.
For National Pension, acquiring on the 1st means the contribution is
charged for that month; acquiring on the 2nd or later normally starts the
following month, though the employee may ask to pay for the acquisition
month. For health insurance, acquiring employee status on the 2nd or
later normally means no workplace contribution for that month, with
charging from the next one.

## A foreign employee's visa alone does not decide this

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">The order that actually matters</p>
  <p>Immigration law decides whether this person may do this job on this
  residence status. Social insurance sits downstream of that. Registering
  someone for the four insurances does not make employment lawful that the
  Immigration Act does not permit — and a D-10 job-seeking status is the
  clearest case where the first question is whether the activity is
  permitted at all, not which insurance applies.</p>
</div>

| Status | Health | Pension | Employment | Industrial accident |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| F-2 · F-5 · F-6 | Covered if the employee test is met | Check nationality, reciprocity, treaty | Compulsory as a rule | Covered as a rule |
| E-7 | Covered if the employee test is met | Covered as a rule; check nationality and treaty | By application | Covered as a rule |
| F-4 | Covered if the employee test is met | Covered as a rule; check nationality and treaty | By application | Covered as a rule |
| E-9 · H-2 | Covered if the employee test is met | Check nationality and treaty | Voluntary under the current scheme | Covered as a rule |

Health insurance is not really a question about visa letters. It asks
whether the person is lawfully working here and meets the employee
subscriber test.

National Pension is the complicated one. A foreign employee is in
principle covered at a Korean workplace, but three further questions
follow: whether their home country gives Koreans an equivalent pension
scheme, whether a social security agreement keeps them on their home
scheme instead, and whether their residence status is itself excluded.

Employment insurance is where residence status matters most directly.
F-2, F-5 and F-6 sit in compulsory coverage, while E-7 and F-4 are
structured around applying for it.

Dependants are a separate filing again. Registering the employee does not
automatically register their family. The relationship has to qualify, the
income and asset conditions have to be met, and it has to be applied for
— and a foreign family member may need documents proving the
relationship.

There is also a waiting period that catches families relocating together.
**Anyone who entered Korea on or after 3 April 2024 must have lived here
six months before they can be registered as a dependant.** The exceptions
apply from the day of entry, and the notice names them individually:

<div class="check-card">
  <div class="check-row ok"><span class="mark"></span><span>A <strong>spouse</strong> and <strong>minor children</strong></span></div>
  <div class="check-row ok"><span class="mark"></span><span>Study <strong>(D-2)</strong>, and general training for primary and secondary students <strong>(D-4-3)</strong></span></div>
  <div class="check-row ok"><span class="mark"></span><span>Non-professional employment <strong>(E-9)</strong></span></div>
  <div class="check-row ok"><span class="mark"></span><span>Permanent residence <strong>(F-5)</strong> and marriage migration <strong>(F-6)</strong>, alongside residence-purpose status</span></div>
  <div class="check-result">A parent arriving with an employee on a status outside this list waits six months for cover. That is a real cost to plan for, not a formality.</div>
</div>

## The real cost of filing late is not the fine

There is a claim that circulates in English-language advice that each
unregistered employee costs ₩300,000 to ₩500,000. There is no such
common rule. Each statute sets its own penalty:

- Under the **National Pension Act**, a failure to report or a false
  report by the employer carries a fine of up to ₩500,000.
- For **employment insurance**, the current administrative fine for
  failure to report — including late filing — is ₩30,000 per insured
  employee, subject to an aggregate cap of ₩1,000,000. False reporting is
  a separate, escalating schedule: ₩50,000 per person for a first
  offence, ₩80,000 for a second, ₩100,000 from the third.
- **Health insurance** and **industrial accident insurance** follow the
  violation-specific schedules in their own statutes.

In practice the retroactive assessment is the heavier problem. If
employee status is confirmed later, or the acquisition date is corrected,
contributions are charged backwards — and by then you have already paid
salary without deducting the employee's share. You now have to recover it
from someone who has already spent it, alongside the employer's share you
also owe. The same thing happens when an employee files a claim to
confirm insured status, or an agency audit finds the gap.

## The order that keeps this stable

  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">2</div>
    <div class="step-body">Settle the contract and the contracted hours.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">3</div>
    <div class="step-body">Separate taxable pay from exempt pay, on the conditions rather than the labels.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">4</div>
    <div class="step-body">Decide coverage insurance by insurance, not once for all four.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">5</div>
    <div class="step-body">Check the workplace itself is registered for each.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">6</div>
