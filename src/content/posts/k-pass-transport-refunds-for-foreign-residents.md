Korea refunds part of what residents spend on public transport. It is a
genuine subsidy on an ordinary commute, and a large number of foreign
residents never claim it because they have been told, or have assumed, that it
is not for them.

It is. What stands in the way is registration, not nationality.

## What it actually is

Not a card. K-Pass refunds a share of qualifying public transport spending,
worked out from usage recorded on a payment method registered to the scheme.
The fare is still paid by that card or mobile product; the refund arrives
afterwards.

Which is why one common line of reasoning fails:

```
refused a post-paid transit function
  → "I'll use K-Pass instead"
      → K-Pass still needs a supported payment method
```

You need something to tap either way. What K-Pass changes is what happens to
the money afterwards.

## The rates, as of 2026

The standard refund is
[20% of qualifying spending](/tracked/kpass-refund-rate-general/), within the
2026 unified travel card framework, with higher tiers:

| Who | Rate |
|---|---:|
| Standard | 20% |
| Young people, two-child households, older people | 30% |
| Households with three or more children | 50% |
| Low-income users | 53.3% |

A separate **flat-amount refund** applies to spending above a threshold, added
in the 2026 expansion.

## Eligibility is administrative, not national

The registration flow accepts a **foreign registration number**. Where an
address cannot be verified automatically — which is common for foreign
residents — a document evidencing residence in Korea is uploaded instead.

So the requirements are:

## You do not need a credit card

Worth stating plainly, because it changes who can use the scheme.

Mobile T-money offers K-Pass products in **both** a top-up version and a
post-paid billing version. So the prepaid route is real.

That matters specifically for foreign residents: a post-paid transit function
is a small credit facility, assessed by the card issuer separately from the card
itself, and it is declined often enough to be a familiar experience. Why that
happens and what it does not mean is in
[post-paid transport cards for foreigners](/banking/postpaid-transportation-cards-for-foreigners/).

Being refused post-paid does not close K-Pass. It decides which supported method
you register.

## Where it sits in the sequence

```
arrival
  → standard T-money, cash top-up
  → foreign registration
  → Korean bank account
  → domestic debit or credit card
  → post-paid transit function, if approved
  → K-Pass registration and a registered payment method
```

Each step depends on the one before it, which is why this is a guide for
residents rather than for arrivals. Getting the first step right is in
[T-money: buying it, topping it up, getting it back](/cost-of-living/t-money-buying-topping-up-and-refunds/).

## Worth doing the arithmetic once

A daily commute across a month is a real number, and 20% of it recurs every
month for as long as you are here. Registration is a one-off administrative
task with a small, permanent return — which is the profile of exactly the kind
of thing people postpone indefinitely.

Do it in the same week you set up the card, while the verification tools are
already in front of you.
