Getting the D-4 was one problem. Keeping it is a different one, and it is
administrative rather than academic.

Five things interlock: **alien registration, the reported address,
attendance, the extension, and permission to work.** A failure in any one
of them surfaces at the next extension or the next change of status, often
months after the mistake.

**How the visa is issued in the first place** — the certificate of
admission, financial evidence, and what consulates refuse on — is in
[the D-4 visa for studying Korean](/study/d-4-visa-korean-language-study/).
This guide begins the day you land.

<p class="hand">The dangerous mistakes are the ones nobody stops you from making.</p>

## 1 · Alien registration, within 90 days

Article 31 of the Immigration Act requires a foreign national intending to
stay beyond 90 days from entry to register within 90 days of entry. A
D-4-1 trainee is on a long stay by definition, so this is not optional and
there is no reason to leave it late.

Registration is not an ID card errand. The registration number it issues
is what a bank account, a phone contract, a lease, health insurance and
online identity verification all run on. **Register late and the whole of
your practical life is late with it.**

### Booking, and what to bring

Confirm your jurisdiction, then book through HiKorea. Ask your institute's
international office first — some schools submit as a group, which removes
the booking problem entirely.

| | What it is |
|---|---|
| Identity | Passport |
| Application | Integrated application form |
| Status | D-4-1, with enrolment or training confirmed |
| School | Certificate of enrolment or training confirmation |
| Residence | Lease, dormitory confirmation, or equivalent |
| Photo | Standard ID photograph |
| Biometrics | Fingerprints, where applicable |
| Fee | Confirm the current amount when applying |

**The residence proof is what fails.** A lease in your own name is
straightforward and a dormitory confirmation is straightforward. Living in
someone else's place needs a written accommodation-provision confirmation
plus evidence of that person's identity and right to the property. Sort
this out with the room, not after the appointment is booked.

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">Book first, worry about the date second</p>
  <p>Offices run on reservations, and slots near a university fill up around a
  term start. Making the booking is the action that protects you; the
  appointment itself can be weeks out. Booking on day sixty is how people
  discover the first free slot falls after day ninety.</p>
</div>

## 2 · Moving means reporting, within 15 days

Once registered, your address is a declared fact with an obligation
attached. Article 36 of the Immigration Act gives a registered foreign
national **15 days from the date of moving in** to report the change.

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">Fifteen, not fourteen</p>
  <p>Plenty of guides still say fourteen days. They are out of date. Check the
  date on anything you read about this — and this is a good instinct for
  Korean immigration guidance generally, where stale numbers circulate for
  years after the rule moved.</p>
</div>

Report at the si/gun/gu or eup/myeon/dong office covering the new address,
or at the immigration office. HiKorea handles it online where the case
supports it. Bring the registration card and proof of the new address.

**What does not count:** filling in a residents card at the building
management office, telling the landlord, or updating your bank. Those are
separate administrations that happen to involve the same address.
Management offices in particular have nothing to do with immigration —
what they actually govern is in
[management offices in Korean apartments](/cost-of-living/apartment-management-offices-explained/).

This catches language students constantly, and always the same way: the
first room is temporary, moving into a real one feels like part of
arriving, and nobody mentions the clock.

## 3 · Attendance is the evidence, not a school rule

D-4-1 is permission to be here **for a specific purpose**. When immigration
assesses an extension it is not only checking that tuition was paid — it
is checking that the training actually happened.

Attendance and grades are the documents that answer that.

University language institutes generally manage attendance strictly, and
80% recurs as the institute's own threshold for progressing a level,
re-enrolling, or transferring to another institution. The government's
Study in Korea materials show 80% used that way.

<div class="callout callout-note">
  <p class="callout-title">What 80% is, and what it is not</p>
  <p>It is the figure schools enforce and a central input to the extension
  assessment. It is <strong>not</strong> a statutory cut-off — nothing says 79%
  means automatic refusal. Extensions are decided on attendance together with
  enrolment status, academic diligence, tuition, financial standing and stay
  history. So the sensible reading is not "80% is the pass mark" but "80% is
  the floor, and comfortably above it is where you want to be."</p>
</div>

### Long absences do not stay inside the school

If a student is absent for an extended period, cancels enrolment or stops
studying, the consequence is not confined to academic administration. Under
the framework for managing foreign students, the institution reports the
change to immigration — and the Ministry of Justice publishes notices where
such reports have led to summonses and to changes in stay permission.

So an unavoidable absence — illness, a family emergency — is handled by
**telling the school at the time, and keeping the documentation**. Clinic
receipts, certificates, whatever exists. The worst available approach is
accumulating unexplained absences and then explaining them at the extension
counter.

```
Absence reported at the time  →  a record
Absence noticed at extension  →  a problem
```

## 4 · Part-time work is a permission, not a right

D-4-1 is not an employment status. Convenience store, restaurant, café —
any paid part-time work requires meeting the conditions and **obtaining
permission from immigration first**.

### The conditions

<div class="steps">
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">1</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Six months must have passed</strong> since entry, or since the change of status. Current Study in Korea guidance sets this for D-4-1 and D-4-7 holders. You cannot begin work in your first term.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">2</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Korean proficiency decides your hours.</strong> The standard is indicated as TOPIK level 2 or an accepted equivalent — completing KIIP stage 2, 41 or above on the KIIP pre-assessment, or beginner level at a King Sejong Institute.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">3</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>The school has to confirm it</strong>, through the international student officer, before the application goes in.</div></div>
</div>

| | D-4-1 language trainee |
|---|---|
| Earliest eligibility | 6 months after entry or status change |
| Korean standard | TOPIK level 2 or accepted equivalent |
| If the standard is met | Around 20 hours on weekdays; a higher ceiling, indicated at 25, for weekends and vacation |
| If it is not met | A narrower permitted range |
| Also required | School confirmation, then prior permission from immigration |

Actual permitted hours depend on the institution's certification status and
the guidance in force at the time. **Confirm both with your international
student officer and with immigration before signing anything**, because
these are the figures most often quoted from a forum post two years old.
This site tracks them: [D-4 part-time work conditions](/tracked/d4-parttime-hour-limit/).

### The sequence, and where people break it

```
Employer agreed
  → employment contract written
  → part-time work confirmation completed
  → international student officer confirms
  → application via HiKorea or the immigration office
  → permission granted
  → first shift
```

The break happens between the contract and the permission. **Having a
signed contract is not permission to work.** The contract is one of the
documents you submit in order to be permitted. Starting before the permit
is granted is unauthorised employment, and it does not stay in the past —
it surfaces at the next extension and at every later application.

### Permission does not cover every job

Part-time work under D-4 assumes ordinary work a student would normally do.
Gambling-adjacent and entertainment industries, work connected to gambling
activity, and work inconsistent with the purpose of study or restricted by
law can fall outside what is permitted.

**Private tutoring is not treated like a shift at a convenience store.**
Government guidance says it is managed strictly, given the particular
circumstances of where and with whom it happens.

Which means the pattern of finding a job first and fitting the permission
around it afterwards is exactly backwards. Confirm the work is permissible
before you agree to it.

## 5 · Prepare the extension by the term, not by the deadline

Because attendance and grades are what the extension turns on, checking
your attendance a month before applying is too late — the term that
produced it has already finished.

Once a term, check these together:

<div class="steps">
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">1</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Attendance rate</strong> — against your institute's threshold, with margin.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">2</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Grades</strong> — the other half of the same evidence.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">3</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Tuition paid</strong> — and the receipt kept.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">4</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Stay expiry date</strong> — not the visa sticker, the permitted stay.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">5</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Passport validity</strong> — see below. This one surprises people.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">6</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Registered address</strong> — still correct, still reported.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">7</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Part-time permit status</strong> — still valid, still matching where you actually work.</div></div>
</div>

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">Stay is not granted beyond your passport</p>
  <p>The Ministry of Justice does not grant a period of stay extending past the
  passport's validity. A passport with eight months left quietly caps what you
  can be given, however good your attendance is. Renew first, then extend —
  and check the passport at the start of the term, not at the counter.</p>
</div>

## 6 · Plan the status after this one

D-4-1 exists for language training. It is a stage, not a destination.

- **A degree** — consider changing status to D-2.
- **Job hunting after the programme** — D-10.
- **Employment** — E-7 and the other work statuses.

A clean D-4 record is not just about staying now. Attendance, reported
addresses and properly permitted work all form the history that later
applications are assessed against.

```
Alien registration
  → address correctly registered, and re-registered on every move
  → attendance and grades managed each term
  → extension
  → part-time permission obtained in advance where needed
  → programme completed
  → D-2, D-10 or E-7 considered
```

## What actually goes wrong

Not the expiry date. Almost everyone watches the expiry date.

What accumulates quietly is: **a student who is barely attending and
believes the valid visa means everything is fine; work started without the
school's confirmation and immigration's permission; an address that changed
and was never reported.** Each is invisible until an extension, and by then
none of them can be undone.
