A Korean payslip is not a payment notice. Article 48 of the Labor Standards
Act requires an employer to issue a wage statement showing the **components
of the wage, how it was calculated, and what was deducted** under law or
agreement — in writing or electronically.

So it is a document you can check. And for a foreign employee it is worth
checking, because deductions that look wrong are often correct for reasons
that have nothing to do with payroll.

## Three blocks, one subtraction

```
earnings
  − deductions
  = net pay
```

Earnings hold base pay, position and duty allowances, overtime, night and
holiday premiums, meal allowance, vehicle allowance. Deductions hold the
four insurances plus income tax and local income tax.

The trap is assuming all of those are calculated on the same figure. They
are not, and that is the single most useful thing to understand about a
Korean payslip.

## Taxable pay is not gross pay

Base pay is employment income. So are position, duty and qualification
allowances, performance pay and ordinary bonuses, unless a specific
exemption applies. Overtime, night and holiday premiums are wages under the
Labor Standards Act and taxable income in principle.

Some items are exempt on conditions:

| Item | Treatment |
|---|---|
| Meal allowance | Up to ₩200,000 a month can be exempt where the conditions are met, including the employer not also providing meals |
| Vehicle allowance | Exempt within a monthly limit only where the vehicle is your own or otherwise qualifying, used for work, and no separate travel expenses are paid |

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">"Non-taxable" printed on the payslip is a claim, not a fact</p>
  <p>A line labelled as exempt still has to satisfy the statutory conditions.
  A vehicle allowance paid to someone with no qualifying vehicle is taxable
  however it is labelled, and that is the employee's tax position, not the
  payroll system's.</p>
</div>

So on ₩5,000,000 gross made up of ₩4,300,000 base, ₩500,000 position
allowance and ₩200,000 exempt meal allowance, tax starts from roughly
₩4,800,000 — and that figure is **not automatically the basis the three
insurances use.**

## Three different bases

| | Uses |
|---|---|
| Income tax | Taxable employment income |
| National pension | Standard monthly income, with a floor and a ceiling |
| Health, long-term care, employment insurance | Monthly remuneration, on their own rules |

Each has its own inclusions, timing for reflecting pay changes, and
reconciliation. Multiplying one number by four rates and expecting the
payslip to match is why verification usually fails.

## 2026 rates, and where the employee share sits

| | Total | Employee share |
|---|---:|---:|
| National pension | 9.5% | **4.75%** |
| Health insurance | 7.19% | 3.595% |
| Health plus long-term care | 8.1348% | **4.0674%** |
| Employment insurance, unemployment benefit | 1.8% | **0.9%** |
| Industrial accident | By industry | **0%** |

National pension began stepping up in 2026 under the pension reform, so
guidance quoting 9% total and 4.5% for the employee is now out of date. Both
values are recorded at
[the national pension rate](/tracked/national-pension-rate/), which is the
point of keeping a history rather than a current figure.

Long-term care is charged as a percentage **of the health insurance
contribution** rather than of pay, which is why the combined figure is
8.1348% rather than a round number —
[long-term care rate](/tracked/long-term-care-rate/).

On a ₩4,800,000 basis and assuming full coverage, the employee side lands
near:

```
national pension        ≈ ₩228,000
health insurance        ≈ ₩172,560
long-term care          ≈  ₩22,675
employment insurance    ≈  ₩43,200
                          ─────────
                        ≈ ₩466,435
```

Actual billing differs with the declared standard monthly income, rounding
and reconciliation. Treat these as a check, not a target.

## National pension: three ways a foreign employee is outside it

A foreign national aged 18 to under 60 at a covered workplace is in
principle a workplace subscriber. The exceptions are real and they are why
a missing line is not automatically an error.

<div class="steps">
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">1</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Reciprocity.</strong> Where your home country does not apply an equivalent pension scheme to Korean nationals, you can be excluded.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">2</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Social security agreement.</strong> A worker seconded from an agreement country who has filed a certificate of coverage at home can be exempt from Korean coverage.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">3</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Status of stay.</strong> Several statuses are excluded — <strong>D-1, D-2, D-3, D-4, D-6, F-1, F-3 and G-1</strong> among them.</div></div>
</div>

So an E-7 employee with no pension line has three things to check before
anyone calls it a mistake.

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">Contributing and getting it back are separate questions</p>
  <p>Whether a lump-sum refund is available on departure depends on your home
  country's equivalent treatment of Korean nationals, any social security
  agreement, and whether your status of stay is one the law specifies. A
  deduction on the payslip is not evidence that a refund is coming. Do not
  budget your departure around one you have not confirmed.</p>
</div>

## Health insurance does not follow the pension's logic

This is where the package assumption breaks most visibly.

Workplace health insurance is judged on the employment relationship and the
coverage conditions under the health insurance rules — **not** on
nationality reciprocity or a social security agreement in the way the
pension is.

Which produces payslips with **no national pension line and a full health
and long-term care line**, entirely correctly. That is not an inconsistency
to report; it is two different statutes reaching different answers about the
same person.

What the health insurance actually buys, and how dependants work, is in
[NHIS health insurance for foreigners](/insurance/nhis-korean-health-insurance-for-foreigners/).

## Employment insurance is where the visa shows up hardest

The employee share is **0.9%** — the unemployment benefit portion, matched
by the employer. Employment stability and vocational skills development
contributions are charged to the employer additionally and never appear as
your deduction.

Coverage, though, splits by status:

| Status | How the Act applies |
|---|---|
| F-5, and certain F-2 and F-6 holders | The Act applies in full |
| F-4, and E-1 to E-7 work statuses | Application-based, once an employment contract exists |

<div class="callout callout-note">
  <p class="callout-title">Do not build a visa-to-rate table</p>
  <p>"E-7 means 0.9%" and "F-4 means no coverage" are both too crude. The
  sub-type of the status and the current enrolment state decide it, and an
  application-based structure means the answer can change without your salary
  changing. Ask payroll what your enrolment state actually is rather than
  inferring it from your visa letter.</p>
</div>

This is the concrete answer to the question people actually have: two
colleagues, same job, same salary, different payslips — and both correct.

## Industrial accident insurance is never your deduction

The employer pays it in full. Full stop.

If a line for employee-borne industrial accident insurance is reducing your
net pay, that is the one item on this page worth querying immediately.

What the employer side of all four costs, and the deadlines for registering
staff, is in
[registering employees for the four insurances](/employment/registering-employees-for-four-insurances/).

## Income tax is a prepayment, not a rate

Monthly income tax is withheld using the National Tax Service's **simplified
withholding tax table**, which sets an amount from your monthly pay and your
number of dependants. The 6–45% progressive scale is not applied to your
monthly salary.

You may also elect **80%, 100% or 120%** of the table amount by filing an
adjustment request with your employer. Nothing is chosen means 100%.

**80% is not a 20% discount.** It reduces the monthly prepayment and the
year is settled in January either way — the difference simply lands then
instead.

Local income tax is normally **10% of the income tax withheld**:

```
income tax withheld  ₩200,000
local income tax     ₩20,000
```

If those two bear no relationship to each other on your payslip, ask.

## Electing the flat rate changes the tax line

A foreign employee who has elected the 19% flat rate can be withheld
differently from the simplified table, at a nominal 20.9% including local
income tax.

The monthly figure being lower is not a reason to elect it — the election
gives up most deductions and credits, and the crossover sits far higher than
usually quoted. That comparison is in
[the 19% flat rate: when it beats the brackets](/business/the-19-percent-flat-rate-when-it-wins/).

## A worked net pay

₩5,000,000 gross: ₩4,300,000 base, ₩500,000 position allowance, ₩200,000
exempt meal allowance. Insurance basis simplified to ₩4,800,000, all
coverage applying.

```
gross                        ₩5,000,000
− social insurance             ₩466,435
− income tax                   (from the table)
− local income tax             (10% of the above)
= net pay
```

With income tax and local income tax at, say, ₩300,000 together, net pay is
roughly **₩4,230,000**.

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <p class="callout-title">That ₩300,000 is an illustration, not a rate</p>
  <p>The real figure comes from the table, your dependants and your elected
  withholding percentage. Anyone quoting a single "take-home percentage" for
  Korea is skipping every variable that actually moves it.</p>
</div>

## What to look for first

Not the net figure. These:

<div class="steps">
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">1</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Pension line missing but you are covered</strong>, or present when a social security agreement exempts you. Both accumulate quietly over months.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">2</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Meal allowance paid but reported as fully taxable</strong>, so the exemption was never applied.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">3</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Overtime worked but no hours and rate shown</strong> — Article 48 requires the calculation method, not just a total.</div></div>
  <div class="step"><div class="step-num">4</div>
    <div class="step-body"><strong>Industrial accident insurance deducted from you.</strong> Query it the same day.</div></div>
</div>

## The order to check in

```
contracted monthly pay
  → earnings, allowances, exempt items
  → gross
  → national pension eligibility
  → health and long-term care enrolment
  → employment insurance, by status
  → each insurance's own basis
  → withholding method: table or flat rate
  → local income tax at 10%
  → net pay
```

Reading a Korean payslip properly is not confirming what landed in the bank.
It is checking that the pay components, the insurance eligibility, the
contribution bases and the withholding method all agree with each other —
and for a foreign employee, that they agree with a status the payroll team
may never have been told about.
